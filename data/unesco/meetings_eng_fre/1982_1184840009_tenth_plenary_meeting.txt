1.1 THE PRESIDENT:  Ladies and gentlemen, I wish you a very good morning. We are now beginning the two final  days of our general debate as foreseen in our timetable of work. But what was perhaps less  foreseeable was the number of speakers we would have on our list this morning. In order to cope  with this situation, I am afraid that we shall have to hold continuous meetings throughout the day,  and certainly night meetings tonight and tomorrow night.
1.2 We still have 65 heads of delegations who intend to take the floor in the plenary debate, as  well as the representatives of two liberation movements and of 10 intergovernmental and non-  governmental organizations who wish to speak. The draft resolutions concerning the plenary  must be discussed as well.
1.3 My proposal concerning continuous sessions and night meetings will be discussed during the  next meeting of the General Committee this afternoon, and I shall convey to you its decision. As  far as night meetings are concerned, I hope that they will not go on beyond 11 p.m.
1.4 I now give the floor to the first speaker on our list, H.E. Ms Jaqueline Creft, Minister of  Education, head of the delegation of Grenada.
2.1 Ms CREFT (Grenada):  Mr President, Mr Director-General, distinguished colleagues, allow me first of all to say  how fully I share the sentiments expressed by previous speakers in congratulating you,  Mr President, on your well-deserved election as President of the fourth extraordinary session  of the General Conference.
2.2 This session is being held at a decisive moment in history, when mankind is faced with the  ultimate challenge of peace or self-destruction, This central issue of our time--the question of  peace--is one which demands of us all the most resolute political will and action to guarantee the  survival and well-being of the peoples of the world. In response to those who irresponsibly use  the language of war, we have seen banners of peace, with slogans in many languages, being  unfurled by an unprecedented mass movement on several continents. In response to the vulgar  and wasteful increased expenditure on advanced systems of mass destruction, we have heard the  rising clamour of people demanding instead more education, better health, an end to poverty  and exploitation and, above all, a world guided by the irrevocable principles of social and  economic justice, mutual tolerance and self-determination. In short, a world of peace.
2.3 Mr President, Unesco has contributed in no small measure to this profound awakening of  the peoples of the world. It has to a great extent assisted in constructing not only the defences of  peace in the minds of men but, equally, viable programmes for the consolidation of international  co-operation, the preservation of the noblest traditions and testaments of the human spirit, and  the alleviation of poverty, want and suffering. Under the high-principled leadership of  Mr Amadou-Mahtar M'Bow, Unesco has played on indispensable role in the promotion of world  peace. In our distinguished Director-General, the poor and the suffering have found a sagacious  and just arbiter and a tireless champion of peace.
2.4 It is under such leadership and against the back ground of such a record that Unesco and we,  the representatives of Unesco's Member States, face the historical imperatives of our time. For  many of us in the Latin-American and Caribbean region, the question of peace, independence  and development continues to be our primary preoccupation. The People's Revolutionary Govern-  ment of Grenada has reiterated time and time again, in every forum, that peace is the main
346   desire of the peoples and the essential condition for social progress. As Minister Tunnermann 1 0  Bernheim, the representative of our brave sister nation of Nicaragua stated earlier, the people  of Nicaragua need peace in order to construct their new society. We also state that the strug-  gling peoples of El Salvador, Palestine, South Africa, the Sahara and Namibia require peace--  a just peace, a peace founded on their right to self-determination--to achieve the noble objec-  tives outlined in this Draft Medium-Term Plan. Without peace, justice and independence,  Mr President, there can never be education for all; communication in the service of men cannot  become a reality; the peoples’ rights to knowledge, science, culture and the rational use of the  earth's resources cannot be exercised.
2.5 The People's Revolutionary Government of Grenada wishes to state emphatically that the  developmental objectives of the Draft Medium-Term Plan, given the character of the historical  imperatives of our era, can only be fully attained by a primary act of political will of the  nations. The establishment of education as the right of all may become an empty phrase if we  ignore the actions of those who brutally murder volunteer literacy teachers in Nicaragua or who,  in other parts of the world, by an inverted logic, transform schools into military garrisons.  Communication may never be at the service of man if the mass media continue to be manipulated  by transnational corporations for purely pecuniary objectives and continue to churn out images  and concepts which are the negation of the human. The extraordinary human achievements result-  ing from Unesco's work to preserve the world cultural heritage and safeguard the great monu-  ments of the past is threatened and negated by the senseless destruction of war. Mr President,  to what avail the painstaking reconstruction of the temples of the Nile when the Israeli aggressors  blast Beirut and deface the city of Jerusalem?
2.6 In the context of these historical contradictions, Mr President, Unesco--and this Draft  Medium -Term Plan now under consideration--stand out in favour of peace. The Draft Plan  embodies the genuine hope of the peoples that, in the face of war, meaningful construction is  possible; in the face of degradation, digiity may be asserted; in the face of the existing awesome  nuclear arsenals and postures of confrontation, the defence of peace will stand unassailably in  the minds of man.
2.7 The analysis of world problems by the Director-General is full of insight and outlines in a  balanced and reflective manner the main character of the challenges we are facing; it quite  correctly forms the basis for the tasks and programmes of the Draft Medium-Term Plan. For  us, the most outstanding characteristic of the Draft Plan is the underlying objective of democra-  tization. In both its method of preparation and its content, it is evident that the Draft Plan seeks  to ensure the democratization of education, culture science and technology. The programmes  seek to involve the widest and broadest mass of people in, and provide access for them to, these  pillars of human civilization. All the programmes of the Draft Plan converge: they point towards  a deepening and broadening of the human rights of the peoples by seeking to guarantee the right  to education, communication, culture and science. In so doing, and by establishing lines of  international co-operation--bridges of friendship--the Draft Medium=-Term Plan is an instrument  of world peace. It is in itself a defence for peace.
2.8 Mr President, if the underlying characteristic of this Draft Plan is democratization, it is  logical that the call for the new international economic and information orders be linked with its  implementation. This is so because we live in a world of structural inequalities in which the  transnational corporations and the developed capitalist societies are bent on maintaining their  exploitative hold on the so-called Third World.
2.9 The provision of education for all, the availability of communication in the service of man,  the full and humanistic application of science to development, the provision of unimpeded access  to knowledge, the equitable and rational use of the earth's resources, the recognition of  cultural integrity, all of these involve the replacement of structures of inequality, oppression  and dependence. If the programmes of the Draft Plan serve to buttress peace, they also serve  as a platform for justice because, Mr President, the provision of these hitherto denied rights  is a matter of justice for our people of the so-called Third World. It is from this platform of  justice that we urge the countries of the North, in particular the developed capitalist world,  to provide the required financial and material support for the successful implementation of the  Draft Medium-Term Plan.
2.10 The great urgency of the need to implement the Draft Medium-Term Plan and construct  the new international and information orders makes it all the more necessary for the under-  developed countries to unite around a shared perspective of our common needs. Grenada comes  to this session, Mr President, fresh from a Summit of the Heads of Government of the
347   Caribbean subregion and confident, from the results of that summit, in the ability of under-  developed nations to unite around common and fundamental objectives. The language of mutual  respect and international co-operation is an indivisible one, and we are particularly pleased  that Unesco is providing even more opportunities for such collaboration. The Major Project in  the Field ui Education in Latin America and the Caribbean, which we wholeheartedly support,  will not only make it possible to achieve major gains in education but, equally importantly, to  join hands together across a linguistically diverse, ideologically plural, but historically and  geographically common, region.
2.11 We are proud to note that the Draft Medium-Term Plan (1984-1989) coincides in many  respects with the programme and National Medium-Term Plan of our Revolution. In the field  of education, we welcome in particular the programmes relating to the democratization of  education, adult education and equality of educational opportunity.
2.12 In the field of communication, we call firmly for the establishment of the new international  information order and the attainment of more balanced, freer flows of information to upgrade the  development of communications in the underdeveloped countries.
2.13 Our main efforts in the fields of science and technology, in addition to the formation of a  Science and Technology Council as a consultative and research body, include the formation of a  young scientist movement in our schools, aimed at stimulating the spirit of scientific inquiry  among youth.
2.14 We look to Unesco for guidance and support in the development of information systems  consistent with the requirements of the age, and will certainly participate in the relevant pro-  grammes outlined. Major Programme X, 'The human environment and terrestrial and marine  resources’, is made up of well-elaborated programmes to assist in the solution of certain  delicate ecological and resource problems.
2.15 Mr President, we view the inclusion in the Draft Plan of Major Programmes XII and XIII,  ‘The elimination of prejudice, intolerance, racism and apartheid' and 'Peace, international  understanding, the freedom of peoples and human rights’, as well as Chapter XIV, 'Note on the  lines of emphasis of the Plan in the field of the status of women', as indicative of the holistic  approach of this Draft Plan to world problems. We note, however, like India, that youth as a  population gector as vitally important as women and upon whom the continuity of this Plan for  peace rests, has not been given an appropriate focus.
2.16 In closing, Mr President, allow me to restate the commitment of the People's Revolution-  ary Government of Grenada to the successful implementation of the Medium-Term Plan for
1984-1989, our determination to struggle for the cause of world peace as a sure guarantee of  the advent of tomorrow, our desire to see the vital issues involved settled in a resolute and  principled manner and our unshakeable belief that, through this Medium-Term Plan, we will  strengthen the pillars of peace, equality among nations and international understanding. Thank  you, Mr President.

Dans tous les autres pays, bien qu'à des degrés divers,  le problème majeur est la misère et tout ce qu'elle entratme à sa suite : sous-alimentation, mortalité  infantile, écoles en nombre insuffisant, analphabétisme, chômage chronique,
(6.14) I faut répéter une vérité évidente, D'un côté, une humanité de plus en plus consciente d'elle -  même, où les puissances dominantes ont imposé leur mode de vie, leurs goûts, leurs comportements  et leurs aspirations, Cette uniformisation a été rendue possible par le cosmopolitisme de nos grandes  villes et par les moyens de communication de masse, De l'autre côté, le fossé s'élargit, On pourrait  penser, par exemple, que nos universités se rapprochent du niveau de celles des pays les plus développés. 14.1 Mr GORANSSON (Sweden)  Madam President, Mr Director-General, Excellencies, ladies and gentlemen, Unesco  should be seen as both an idea and an instrument. Firstly, as an idea expressing a generally  acknowledged need for international co-operation and solidarity in the intellectual domains of  education, science, culture and communication--an idea to which my Government wishes to give  its firm and unequivocal support. Secondly, and since Unesco is an organization, it should be  seen as an instrument for the administration of a programme which renders this basic idea  explicit.
14.2 At the outset, Madam President, permit me to make some general remarks concerning  the important role of Unesco in relation to its idea in two particular fields which are becoming  more and more interconnected. I refer to the development of mass communication and the new  situation thus created for the development of cultural industries; and to the functions of the new  media in the cultural field.
14.3 Few of Unesco's spheres of competence, if indeed any, today witness such rapid develop-  ment as that of communication and information. At the same time, the rapid technological  advance of the new media seems to leave large segments of the peoples and nations of our world  behind. Direct-broadcasting satellites, cable television and video machines, as well as other  innovations in communication are here to stay. The question is--for what purpose? In what ways  will they be used? What effects will they have upon older media and upon different forms of art  and culture? Maybe it is more important for a country to provide every citizen with the possi-  bility of viewing one television programme than to have a situation where a number of citizens in  more densely populated areas have access to several programmes?
14.4 In many countries--industrialized as well as developing--the situation as regards the mass  mcdia is characterized by strong tendencies towards monopolization. In the developing countries,  a situation of absolute poverty in this field is sometimes prevalent; or else a situation of exter-  nal dependence on foreign sources and powers. In many industrial countries, and more es-  pecially in those which belong to small linguistic areas and which have small populations, the  principle of pluralism is threatened by direct commercial pressures: for example, the costs of  producing books, films and newspapers have risen sharply. The availability of cultural products  would decrease drastically if they were priced on purely commercial grounds. A similar develop-  ment is now taking place in the field of broadcasting, and even more so in television, where  small countries face growing difficulties in keeping up with rising costs.
14.5 These considerations have a bearing not only on mass media development but, more  generally, on the whole spectrum of cultural development. Indeed, in this field, Unesco has an  important task: it must indicate methods, policies and strategies for dealing with the new  situation.
14.6 A number of useful international recommendations in the field of cultural policy have been  worked out, with a definite impact on national policies in Member States. Some useful studies  have been conducted and more useful ideas are likely to result from our present debate on the  Director-General's suggestions in the Draft Medium-Term Plan.
14.7 Madam President, Unesco's future work in the field of culture should be inspired by the 1 0  new aspect, called 'the cultural dimension of development’, which, in one particular, is  especially important to us. I refer to the similarity of the situation facing most of those indus-  trialized countries which belong to smail linguistic areas and have small populations, like Sweden,  as well as the developing countries. This similarity could provide an occasion for fruitful inter-  action, and for a common search for solutions to problems which are becoming more and more  identical in all the countries concerned.
14.8 Technological change, sooner or later, means change in human circumstances. These  changes will most certainly offer important improvements in work and life. But the new media  may also bring with them new dangers. They may create more tensions within and between  countries as the already thin web of common history, traditions, values and interests gets even  thinner and perhaps vanishes altogether. These dangers should not be underestimated. They can  only be averted through concerted and determined efforts. I am convinced that we still have time  for reflection and action, although it is running short. In order to make the best use of the new  media we must better understand their potentialities, both positive and negative.
14.9 Madam President, we have to ask ourselves whether we really need all the possibilities  offered by the new media; and whether we all need the same possibilities at the same time. I  believe, for instance, that while some countries may benefit already today from the use of  direct-broadcasting satellites, others might be better served by developing terrestrial systems  for local and regional broadcasting.
14.10 We most definitely need to extend and deepen our knowledge of the consequences for  national cultural creation of both the quantity and quality of products emerging from the cultural  industries. There is a danger that international competition will force indigenous artists to  adapt themselves to alien mass-products, to the detriment of their own identity. We cannot  simply stand by and watch large parts of our cultural heritage being scrapped. Solutions to these  problems must also be sought internationally, although it is ultimately a national responsibility  to build up active policies that provide adequate conditions in which an indigenous creativity may  flourish.
14.11 The development of new media, such as satellites, cable television and video, will make  international co-operation and international agreements more necessary than ever. No country  would like to see such media structures as it has already possessed for a number of decades  destroyed by the overspill from direct-broadcasting satellites. Every country should have the  right to develop whatever structure of mass media it wishes. In Sweden, for example, we wish  to preserve radio and television broadcasting as a public service function, and we will not  accept advertising in radio and television programmes.
14.12 Madam President, to my mind, it is obvious that a certain amount of governmental  support is necessary in order to maintain a reasonable range and an acceptable quality of  cultural products, as well as to uphold the principle of pluralism in the mass media.
14.13 It is essential to point out that we, like most other countries, apply models for public  support which interfere neither with the content of the mass media themselves, nor with  cultural expression. In my country, for example, radio and television broadcasting are organ-  ized as a public service system, financed by the public and with independent status; that is to  say, they are free from governmental control. Public support to the press may, for instance,  be given in accordance with general rules and by channels that do not allow for influence on the  content. We have some experience in these fields which might be of interest to other countries--  specifically small countries--facing similar problems.
14.14 It is not generally true that government subsidies must constitute a threat to the freedom  of the mass media or of artistic creation. On the contrary, properly organized, they increase  freedom and pluralism. It is quite natural for us to suggest that Unesco take models of this kind  into consideration when preparing assistance for communication development. To my mind,  international support for mass media development is not a matter of choice between exclusive  alternatives: intergovernmental organizations and professional bodies alike have a rightful place,  on an equal footing with private co-operative initiatives of which we have already seen some prom=  ising examples.

Thus tue primary purpose of a plan should be to establish the main pri-  orities for action.
