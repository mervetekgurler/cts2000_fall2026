Mr. CARTER (Head of the Department of  Exchange of Persons) stressed the fact that one  of the principal activities of Unesco in the field  of exchange of persons during 1948 had been the  development of its clearing-house functions. The  first three and a half pages of the working paper  were devoted to eliciting the views of delegations  on the whole problem of the collection and  dissemination of information by Unesco on  exchange of persons activities, with a view to  aiding Member States in planning and promoting  fellowship programmes. In the course of 1948  the Secretariat had drawn up a world handbook  on international fellowships, scholarships and  related opportunities, which was at the moment  being printed. The handbook had been based on  information received in reply to a questionnaire  sent out by Unesco in May, 1948, to which answers  had been received from some twenty countries.               Approximately 700 different types of fellowships  and 10,000 specific opportunities were listed as  available in 1948-1949, and additional information  was given on possible opportunities in 1949-50  and 1950-51. It was hoped to publish supple-  mentary surveys later on. The handbook was  minutely indexed, and included not only details  of individual fellowships but also an analysis of  fellowships under subjects covered. The Secre-  tariat sought guidance on the continuance of the  survey, and methods of publicizing it effectively.  Mr. HOLCROFT (New Zealand) thought that  it would be possible for the National Commission  in his country, with the help of the Co-operating  Bodies, to supply Unesco with regular information  to keep the handbook up to date, and suggested  that university libraries should be sent copies of  the handbook for dissemination of the information  contained in it.  Mr. MARSHALL (United States of America)  congratulated Unesco on its work in the collection  of information in 1948, expressing the hope that  it would be continued with vigour in the future,  and pledging the co-operation of his country in  that work, which he considered to be of para-  mount importance in the spreading of Unesco’s  ideals and aims.  M. SANDBO (Norway) had two criticisms to  make on behalf of the persons in Norway who  had been asked to contribute information for the  handbook. Many of them had considered that  the Unesco questionnaire should have been more  simple in the first instance, and that, in any case,  persons wishing to apply for fellowships would  not be able to find all the necessary information  in the handbook. He was, however, in agree-  ment with the delegate of the United States of  America that work on that publication was of  primary importance.  Sir Ronald ADAM (United Kingdom) supported  the delegate of Norway's view that, once the hand-  book was published, more persons would be  willing to contribute to it. He thought the infor-  mation contained in the handbook could best be  publicized by National Commissions. The passing  of the Fulbright Bill by the United States Congress  would lead to a far greater number of fellowships  being offered in the next two years, so that, if  the handbook were to be kept up to date, publi-  cation of annual supplements would perhaps be  necessary.
