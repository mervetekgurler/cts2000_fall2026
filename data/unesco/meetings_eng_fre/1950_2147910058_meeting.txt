SUMMARY RECORD OF THE MEETING  OF THE JOINT COMMISSION—PROGRAMME AND  BUDGET, OFFICIAL AND EXTERNAL RELATIONS  Thursday, 8 June 1950, at 4 p.m.  Chatrman: Mr.  Election of Chairman.  [1] On the proposal of Dr. BARALT y ZACHARIE  (Chairman, Official and External Relations Com-  mission), M. Roger Seydoux (Chairman, Pro-  gramme and Budget Commission) took the Chair.  Unesco’s activities in Germany during 1951  [document 5C/5 (I) Rev., Part Ill].  General Discussion.  [2] Sir John MAUD (Chairman, Executive Board)  recalled the debates which had taken place at  previous Conferences on the question of Unesco’s  activities in Germany, and observed that the pro-  gramme which he was presenting was the logical  outcome of the decision taken at the Mexico Con-  ference to initiate a programme of work in Ger-  many. At the Beirut Conference in 1948, it had  been decided to expand that programme, and  following that Conference, the Executive Board  had decided that an expert committee should be  established to advise the Board and the Director-  General on the way in which Unesco could best  contribute to world peace through its work in  Germany. The Expert Committee was composed  of representatives from Czechoslovakia, Nether-  lands, Poland, United States of America, Norway,  Belgium, France, United Kingdom and Switzer-  land. The Committee held its first meeting in  May 1949 and its second in the autumn of that  year. The Board had had the advantage of the  Committee's advice in preparing the draft pro-  gramme for Germany in document 5C/5 (I) Rev.,  although the Czechoslovak and Polish represen-
470  \OGER SEynoux (France)  tatives had unfortunately been absent on that  occasion.  [3] The Executive Board had recently taken an  important decision regarding Unesco’s work in  Germany in 1950: it had authorized the Director-  General to continue his consultations with re-  presentatives of the appropriate Allied Authorities  and German circles, and with experts on German  questions, with a view to furthering Unesco's  action in Germany in the fields of education, youth  movements and the social sciences and, In  particular, with a view to preparing plans for the  establishment of centres or institutes in those three  fields. That decision of the Board should be borne  in mind in considering the 1951 programme, for  which the 1950 programme would serve as a  spring-board.  [4] The United States delegation had submitted  two proposals, contained in document 5C/55, as  an amendment to the Board’s programme. The  Board had no comment to make on the first  proposal, but what it had decided in 1950 had a  certain relevance thereto. The second proposal  was a slight amendment to the seventh paragraph  on page 60 of the Board’s programme. The Board  was prepared to accept this proposal, provided the  words ‘from private sources and from Member  States” were substituted for “from sources outside  the regular Budget”, which the Board had felt  were not sufficiently precise.  [5] In conclusion, Sir John observed that no part  of Unesco’s work had constituted a more serious  responsibility for the Executive Board and the  General Conference than the programme in Ger-  many. It was in the hope that results would be  achieved more fruitful than those of previous        Conferences, that he presented the Board’s pro-  posals for 1951.  [6] The CHAIRMAN welcomed the three ob-  servers of the Allied High Commission in Germany,  Mr. Schmitlein, Mr. Reid and Professor Marshall.  [7] Mr. SCHMITLEIN (France) (Allied High  Commission in Germany) stated that the Allied  High Commission was on the best of terms with  the Unesco Office in Germany and strongly in  favour of the Organization's activities.  [8] Mr. REID (United States of America)  (Allied High Commission in Germany) said that  all those at present working in Germany were  impressed by the crucial point which had been  reached and by the fact that the German mind was  at present more open and fluid than it had been  for many years. There were encouraging and  discouraging signs: an encouraging sign was the  reaction of university students. Polls showed that  they had had their fill of militarism and were  looking for a new spirit. On the other hand, the  spiritual outlook of many of the unemployed  aroused grave concern, and the time was perhaps  ripe for Unesco to increase its activities for peace  and international understanding in Germany. So  far, not much was known of Unesco and its goals,  although that was not the fault of the Unesco  representatives in Germany, and Dr. Thompson's  excellent work, carried out in difficult circum-  stances, was very much appreciated. Upon the  initiative of the Unesco staff, a German Com-  mittee of forty or fifty members representing  major cultural and scientific groups had been set  up on a democratic basis, and the Bonn Govern-  ment had allocated 100,000 marks to it.  Mr. Reid himself, and his British and French  colleagues in the Allied High Commission all  hoped that Unesco would take further concrete  steps in Germany.  [9] Professor MARSHALL (United Kingdom)  (Allied High Commission in Germany) fully  endorsed what his colleague had said. He under-  lined the fact that all those working in Germany  felt that a new phase had started in recent months,  not only because of the establishment of a Federal  Government and the handing over to it of control  of German life, but also because a generation of  young people, too young to have been consciously  involved in the war and still less in the Nazi  regime, were now reaching maturity; the upheaval  had been so great that a dark curtain covered the  past and youth looked to the future: it was there  that they needed guidance from Unesco.  With regard to Unesco’s functions in the  immediate future, its aims should be the same as  those of the three Allied Occupying Powers,  namely to develop education, science and culture  on a genuine international basis. Unesco, as a  free international body, had even greater possi-  bilities than the occupation Authorities, and its  ultimate aim should be to bring to Germany  exactly the same benefits as it brought to other  countries, not considering Germany as a special  case, but as a country which, although it needed  to receive outside benefits, would in the near  future have something to contribute to other  countries, and indeed already had something to  contribute. To bridge the great gulf caused by  the past, there should be clear focal points in  Germany to which Germans could turn to learn  about Unesco and to contribute to its work.  Professor Marshall therefore particularly welcomed  the project to establish Unesco centres.  [10] Mr. SARGEANT (United States of America)  had listened with great pleasure to the remarks  made by the Chairman of the Executive Board and  was delighted that the Board had decided to  authorize the Director-General to continue con-  sultations with German circles and with experts  on Germany. He warmly supported Professor  Marshall's remark that Unesco should not consider  its German programme with the feeling that it was  dealing with a special case, but should try to  promote the same activities in Germany as in  other countries; that was the very sense of the  United States resolution. His delegation felt that  Unesco’s programme in Germany had hitherto  lacked a sufficiently penetrating quality, and the  United States resolution therefore aimed at  administratively strengthening and intensifying  the Executive Board's programme. An example of  what Unesco could bring to Germany was the  opportunity for self-education, which meant that  it must create the conditions which made that  possible. It had a great role to play with regard to  youth, and the main purpose of the United States  resolution was to plant the seeds of freedom and  peace in the minds of the young.  Mr. Sargeant announced that his delegation  accepted the Executive Board’s amendment to the  second resolution of document 5C/55. He form-  ally moved the adoption of the United States  resolutions, with the inclusion of the Board's  amendment.  [11] Mr. NANSEN (Norway) spoke of the  desperate plight of a great number of children and  young people in Germany, many of whom were  refugees from the Eastern Zone and had no home  or normal family life: all their efforts to obtain  educational possibilities and vocational training  were in vain and ihey were reduced to living  dishonestly. There were criminals of all ages, and  children of 12 or 13 years were convicted for          robbery, murder and even white slavery. Juvenile  delinquents would exceed one million in 1951 if  nothing were done, and Unesco’s main activity in  Germany should therefore be focused on youth.  [12] Speaking of the appalling refugee problem,  Mr. Nansen recalled that Germany was divided into  two conflicting parts, between which there was  increasing tension. Of the 12,000,000 refugees,  only a portion had been assimilated. Germany  was obliged to accept all the German and non-  German refugees who poured in at the rate of
1,000 to 1,500 a day; it had not been humanly  possible to refuse refuge to the 600,000 refugees  from the Soviet Zone who, in desperate straits,  made a brave fight against heavy odds. Two  things were clear: that many of the refugees  would not have the physical or spiritual strength  to survive, and that their fate would be of decisive  importance to Germany. If they were left to  their fate, it would lead to a total disaster  in Germany which would affect all Western  civilization, of which Germany was an integral  economical, social and geographical part.  All reconstruction work carried out in Germany  would be vain if the importance of the refugee  problem were not faced. Mr. Nansen explained  that he had suffered in German concentration  camps during the war, and that he was not  pleading the cause of Germany, but the cause of  humanity, of Europe and of the future of Western  civilization. He was appealing to all the delegates  present to do everything in their power to prevent  a recurrence of the horrors experienced in the  concentration camps, where he had learned  something of the destruction of human beings and  had seen how quickly human minds could be  destroyed and a whole community transformed  into a lawless hell.  [13] Appealing to Unesco to take action before  it was too late and to check the spiritual  destruction which was proceeding in Germany at  the present time to an extent unparalleled in the  history of mankind, Mr. Nansen called for action  instead of promises and speeches. Unesco could  take a leading role in the spiritual and cultural  rehabilitation of Germany by increasing oppor-  tunities for professional training and humanistic  education. The Bonn Goverment was attempting  to find a solution by constructive activity in many  fields, and rehabilitation work was being carried  out by German and non-German non-governmental  organizations, but there was an urgent need to  co-ordinate all the dispersed efforts, and it was  there that Unesco should play a leading part.  Mr. Nansen sincerely hoped that Unesco would  carry out the plan proposed in the Director-  General’s report to establish a body in Germany to
472  co-ordinate the work of all the non-governmental  organizations and to ensure that all support  received the utmost assistance from the Bonn  Government and the German authorities in gen-  eral.  In conclusion, Mr. Nansen requested his fellow  delegates to insist that activities in Germany  should be courageously conceived and executed, so  that Unesco might contribute to the solution of the  tragic situation existing in that country.  [14] Mr. DELBOS (France) urged the need for  continuity in Unesco’s work, and noted with  approval that the draft resolutions presented by  the Executive Board extended and amplified the  resolutions previously adopted in this field. He  emphasized the wisdom of making a modest  beginning with active organizations, which had  proved most valuable in introducing Unesco to  Germany. The need of the moment was to  consider how to enlarge the field of action and to  develop further methods. He hoped that the  means of financing projects then being con-  templated was an exceptional measure, and that  the greatest possible number of States would offer  their contribution to enable the existing bodies to  be developed and a National Commission to be set  up on a democratic basis.  [15] Dr. KRUYT (Netherlands) recalled how, at  the first Conference in Paris in 1946, the Nether-  lands delegation had proposed a resolution  advocating that Unesco should try to teach the  democratic way of life to the youth of Germany.  He was therefore very glad to see that the United  States delegation was now supporting the action  proposed by the Netherlands at the First Confer-  ence. He recalled how much his country had  suffered under the Germans during the war, which  explained why it so much desired to see Germany  re-educated. The Germans were a nation which  had never understood the principle of mutual  respect, but there were a few Germans who  respected men of other countries and races, and  Unesco should support that minority in the hope  that it would become a majority in the near future.  He therefore supported the United States proposal.  [16] Miss PEDERSEN (Denmark) stated that the  situation in Germany was of great concern to  Denmark, as a neigbouring country. Her del-  egation agreed with the United States delegation  that Unesco’s activities there should be intensified.  Two major difficulties faced Unesco in Germany,  the first being the refugee problem. The Danish  delegation could not accept Mr. Nansen’s proposal  that Unesco should try to solve that problem, as  the Constitution did not justify this. The Dir-  ector-General’s Report [document 5C/31] recom-                                              mended (page 110) that the problem should be  brought to the attention of the appropriate  authorities of the United Nations, and that view  was supported by the Danish Delegation.  [17] The second difficulty was that of contacting  the proper organizations and individuals in Ger-  many. The Danish delegation wished to stress  the importance of contacting German women’s  organizations, such as the National Council of  Women, a branch of the International Council of  Women, which was carrying out work in accord-  ance with Unesco’s aims. Miss Pedersen recalled  that women formed 65 per cent of the voting body  in Germany, and that their co-operation was there-  fore essential to Unesco.  [18] In view of the financial difficulties involved  in the German programme, the Danish delegation  supported the United States resolution and was of  the opinion that practical work should be given  priority over research and studies throughout the  programme. Thus, on page 110 of the Director-  General’s Report [document 5C/3], the activities  listed under (c), (d) and (e) should be given  priority over those under (a) and (b). Similarly  cn page 62 and 63 of the Programme of the Execu-  tive Board [document 5C/5 (I) Rev.] under  Cultural Activities, the projects relating to the  preparations of lists of recorded music, works of  art, etc., and to copyright, should not be given  priority, but should be carried out under the 1951  programme resolutions as part of Unesco’s ordinary  programme.  [19] Dr. OPRECHT (Switzerland) felt that the  question of Germany should be among Unesco’s  foremost concerns, and he hoped that adequate  resources might be forthcoming through the  adoption of the methods then being considered.  He pointed out that in nearly all Member States.  the National Commissions had already established  relations with Germany, and he referred especially  to the Swiss National Commission, which had set  Up a special committee for relations with Germany.  [20] Professor CARNEIRO (Brazil) reminded  the meeting that from its earliest days Unesco had  not only offered Germany material assistance, but  had given its activities in that country a high  moral significance. He emphasized that the first  experiments had been made at the suggestion of  the Netherlands delegation. If Unesco was now  In a position to strengthen its material assistance.  it was necessary that the moral significance of  such work should continue to be uppermost in the  minds of the German people. He was in favour  of the projects presented by the Executive Board  and of the proposal of the United States delegation,  and hoped that the suggested activities might in
473  the future be extended to the whole of Germany.  He felt that Germany would be preparing to  become a Member State of Unesco if it accepted  the present programme.  [21] Mr. SARAJCIC (Yugoslavia) was of the  opinion that the proposed activities were likely to  promote the maintenance of world peace and to  combat any fascist tendencies that still existed, as  well as the re-emergence of imperialism. Accord-  ingly, the Yugoslav delegation supported the  Executive Board’s proposals and the resolution of  the United States delegation. The speaker also  urged the need for further expansion of the  suggested programme and its extension to Ger-  many as a whole.  [22] Dr. BERNAL JIMENEZ (Colombia) dwelt  on the very keen interest Colombia was taking in  Unesco’s work in Germany, and expressed his  support of the proposal put forward by the del-  egation of the United States of America.  [23] Dr. KOHN (Israel) hoped that the gravity  of the German problem would not be under-  estimated. The Jews had suffered more than any  other people from the Germans and no nation  more greatly desired a change of heart in  Germany, but his delegation had doubts regarding  the methods proposed in the Board’s Resolutions:  the democratic outlook was not acquired from  textbooks, but required a moral effort on the part  of the Germans and a sincere discarding of the  Nazi aggressive spirit. The Israeli delegation  would not oppose Unesco’s efforts to guide the  German people, provided the methods used were  effective and not harmful, and it would like to see  the German authorities bear the major part of the  cost of the programme.  [24] Dr. Kohn issued a strong warning against  allowing Germany to enter Unesco as an active  factor in the near future and against giving her  leadership; so long as she was not admitted to the  United Nations, she should not be allowed to  exercise an active influence within Unesco.  [25] With regard to the Exchange of Persons Pro-  gramme, Dr. Kohn agreed that mature professors  could be sent to Germany, but expressed grave  doubts as to the wisdom of sending young persons  with unformed minds. He pointed out that  Nazism had not yet been eradicated and that young  foreigners might be infected with that doctrine.  It was easy to undermine democracy and Unesco  should beware of undue optimism.  [26] Dr. ZOOK (United States of America)  recalled that frequent references had been made to  Unesco’s central objective of peace and security.     [t was clear to the United States delegation that  the situation in Germany constituted one of the  greatest threats to peace and security: he believed  that all other delegations shared that apprehen-  sion, and he appreciated the fact that representa-  tives of other countries, such as the Netherlands,  had taken the leadership in calling the attention  of successive Conferences to the situation in  Germany. Some progress had been made since  the close of hostilities, and after a long debate at  the Paris Conference in 1949, funds for the  German Programme had been increased. The  total sum of $115,000 for the Programmes in  Germany and Japan however represented only
1.5 per cent of Unesco’s Budget and bore  very little relation to the seriousness of the  situation: the United States delegation had  therefore taken the liberty of proposing a resolu-  tion which would enable additional funds to be  found.  [27] One of the most outstanding contributions  made by Unesco’s small staff in Germany had been  to assist in the organization, on a voluntary and  democratic basis, of the German National Com-  mittee for Unesco activities. Unesco could hence-  forth count on the vigorous co-operation of the  German representatives and also on that of the  Allied Occupation Authorities.  Three fields of activities had been mentioned by  the Executive Board; education, youth leadership  and social science development. In the first field,  Unesco could help to eliminate the causes of past  friction and excessive nationalism by encouraging  the revision of textbooks, bv research in com-  parative education and by the introduction of  ideas about human rights into school curricula.  With regard to youth leadership, Unesco could  undertake a variety of projects, such as the  training of youth leaders, the extension of pro-  fessional training and the establishment of inter-  national houses for young people. In the field of  social sciences, it was important to inspire public  opinion in Germany with a desire to co-operate  with other nations. Social sciences were practical-  ly unknown in Germany, so that there was a great  need for basic research, including research in the  field of tensions. Dr. Zook believed that the three  foregoing areas were excellent examples of fields  in which co-operation could be achieved by  Unesco with the Allied and German Authorities.  [28] The United States delegation considered that  the resolutions of the Executive Board were quite  adequate for the situation, but that a substantial  amount of concentration could make the pro-  gramme more effective. It further believed that  an increased appropriation by Unesco for staff in  Germany was necessary to the exception of an
474  effective programme. Unesco should try to estab-  lish a more effective relationship between its pro-  gramme departments and its staff in Germany. If  supplementary funds were found for the German  programme, they should be made available for the  development of the Unesco centres or institutes.  They should also be used for the establishment of  a central office in Germany to co-ordinate all  reconstruction activities. Germany must be pre-  pared to enter the family of nations and it was for  Unesco to prepare a situation which would enable  her to do so. Her reintegration in the family of  nations was essential to the peace and security of  the world.  Draft resolutions presented by the Executive Board  [document 5C/5 (I) Rev., Part IV].  PREAMBLE.
