(4.1) Mr. SOBAKIN (Union of Soviet Socinlist Republics) (Translation from thé Russian):  Mr. President; distinguished delegates, When we discuss whether or not a particular item  should be included in the Agenda, we clearly have to determine, in the first place, whether con-  sideration of the item in quebtion is useful and necessary, and secondly whether its inclusion is  possible from the legal and procedural standpoints. ’
(4.2) In yesterday's proceedings some delegations stated that the General Conference could not  decide the issue of the admission of the German Democratic Republic to Unesco in the absence  of a favourable recommendation by the Executive Board. This argument merely goes to show that  the present session of the General Conference cannot reach a decision on the substance of the  matter, but by no means proves that the meiter cannot be included in the Agenda, The fact is  that discussion of this matter by the General Conference is in no sense an academic or useless  exercise; on the contrary, in our view it is absolutely essential} and is not only useful but vitalto  the successful work of our Organization. . -
(4.3) According to its Constitution, Unesco was created "for the purpose of advancing, through  the educational and scientific and cultural relations of the peoples of the world," - I stress the  phrase "the peoples of tha world" - "the objectives of international peace and of the common wel-  fare of mankind", In other words, Unesco is bullt on the principle of universality, and in the  admission of new members there should be no disçrimination against any country that wishes to  join the Organization and has undertaken to observe its Constitution, , ¥
(4.4) Unfortunately, however, -just such a discriminatory attitude was displayed by a group of  members of the Executive Board when the Board discussed the application of the German Demo-  cratic Republic, As we all know, many members of the Board spoke out in favour of admitting  the German Democratic Republic and practically no one at all denies that in principle the German  Democratic Republic has the right to become a member of Unesco and that it is able and willing  to take part in implementing the programmes of our Organization. In the course of the meeting,  however, a group of Board members objected to the admission of the German Democratic  Republic and sought to justify their attitude by criteria which are utterly at variance with the  Constitution and practice of Unesco.
(4.5) To begin with, they attempted to prove that the question of admitting the German Demo-  cratic Republic to Unesco was of a political character and could therefore be settled only after  some sort of discussion of the matter within the United Nations or even only after the admission  of the German Democratic Republic to the United Nations,
(4.6) In our view, it is quite obvious that this argument contradicts Article II of Unesco's Con-  stitution since paragraph 2 of that Article is specifically designed to enable Unesco to admit to  its membership States which are not yet members of the United Nations. In this respect Unesco  is a fully independent organization. And if every time the question of admitting new members  arises Unesco has to await a ruling by the United Nations, then paragraph 2 of Article II of the  Constitution ceases to have any meaning. What is more, both the United Nations and the Unesco  General Conference have specially emphasized that Unesco has an entirely free hand inthe matter  of admitting new members.  {4.7) 1 should like to recall that at its twelfth session the General Conference of Unesco, in full  agreement with the I/nited Nations and, incidentally, on the proposal of the delegation of the United  States of America, rescinded the rule about preliminary consultations with the United Nations |  regarding the admission of new members. This once again emphasizes the entire independence  of Unesco in this matter and the inadmissibility of making admission to the Organization condi-  tional on prior consultations with the United Nations. This, by the way, was mentioned yesterday  by a number of representatives, and in particular the representative of Frence when he spoke  from this rostrum. Furthermore, account should be taken of the fact that Unesco has already on  several occasions decided such matters - over twenty States have been admitted which were not  or still are not members of the United Nations. In particular, the admission of the Federal Re-  public of Germany to Unesco in 1951 demonstrates the blatant inconsistency and partiality of the  representatives of those States which now assert that the question of admitting the German Demo-  cratic Ropublic cannot even be discussed.
(4.8) In 1951 the representatives of those countries, and also the Executive Board, which issued  a recommendation in favour of admitting the Federal Republic of Germany, took the view that  Unesco could decide to admit one cf the German States before the matter had been discussed and  settled by the United Nations. This being so, the Executive Board of our Organization should  clearly follow those precedents and not use the allegedly political character of the question as a  pretext for refusing to admit the German Democratic Republic.
(4.9) The second main argument of the delegations which opposed admission was that the German  Democratic Republic could not be admitted io Unesco until the bilateral relations between the  German Democratic Republic and the Federal Republic of Germany had been regularized. Those  delegates also claimed that admission of the German Democratic Republic to Unesco would con-  stitute an interference in those bilateral relaifons. But in 1951, when the question of admitting  the Federal Republic of Germany to Unesco was being decided, the relations between the two  countries had not then been regularized either. But this in no way prevented the Bcard and sub-  sequently the General Conference from accepting the Federal Republic of Germany as a member  of Unesco. Why is this argument advanced now? Why are different criteria applied to these two  States? Why in the case of the German Democratic Republic are additional requirements put  forward? This is unfair on the German Demccratic Republic and corresponds neither to the Con-  stitution nor to the practice of Unesco. The use of the absence of regularized relations between  the Federal Republic of Germany and the German Democratic Republic as an argument against  admission of the latter has yet another extremely far-reaching consequence,
(4.10) If Uñesco, heving admitted one of these States to membership, refuses even to discuss  the question of admitting the other State, this will mean that Unesco favours one State and dis-  criminates against the other. In other words, Unesco will thereby demonstrate that it is not an  impartial educational,‘ scientific and cultural organization, but that in political problems between  two States it sides with one State against the other; that is, it interferes in the settlement of bi-  lateral political problems. Therefore it is not the admission of the German Democratic Republic
60   in Unesco but, on the contrary, the refusal to admit that country which constitutes interference  in bilateral relations,
(4.11) To put it objectively, those who actively oppose discussion of the admission ofthe German  Democratic Republic to Unesco are dragging our Organization into a political game, and making  the question of admission to Unesco a means of putting pressure on the German Democratic Re-  public in order to force concessions from it in other matters that have nothing to do with Unesco.
(4.12) Thus you see, gentlemen, that differences of opinion have appeared in the Executive Board  regarding the very criteria which should govern the admission of new members to Unesco.
(4.13) We feei that in these circumstances it is essential that a more authoritative and more rep-  resentative body - namely the General Conference - should hold a discussion on this question so  as to enable all members of Unesco who so desire to define their attitude to the practical appli-  cation of the principle of universality underlying the Constitution of Unesco,
(4.14) Given a situation in which a group of members of the Executive Board is applying patently  discriminatory criteria not provided for in Unesco's Constitution, the General Conference should,  in our view, draw up definite instructions of a general nature for the Executive Board, based on  the principle that when there is a discussion about admission of new members, the Executive  Board should base its approach on the provisions of the Organization's Constitution, and not on  any other extraneous and arbitrary criteria,
(4.15) We therefore consider that discussion of this question is necessary. But is it legally and  procedurally possible?
(4.16) At the last session of the Board, Unesco's legal adviser, Mr. Saba, explained with his  wonted clarity and precision that inclusion of the item on the admission of the German Democra-  tic Republic in the Agenda of the General Conference was quite possible and in no way contra-  dicted either Unesco's Constitution or the Rules of Procedure of the General Conference. In that  connexion it was suggested that since it was the first time that Unesco would be dealing with such  a question, it could, perhaps, by analogy, apply the procedure followed by the United Nations  General Assembly and the Security Council. Rule 138 of the Rules of Procedure of the General  Assembly reads: "If the Security Council does not recommend the applicant State for member-  ship ..., the General Assembly may, after full consideration of the special report of the Security  Council, send back the application to the Security Council, together with a full record of the dis-  cussion in the General Assembly, for further consideration and recommendation or report”. We  feel that what can be done in the United Nations can also be done in our Organization,
(4.17) Therefore, Mr. President, we consider that inclysion of the question of the admission of  the German Democratic Republic in the Agenda 13 not only possible but necessary. So the dele-  gation of the Soviet Union will quite naturally be voting against the proposal of the delegation of  the United States of America to omit this item from the Agenda.
(4.18) Now, Mr. President, I should also like to say a few words about some other proposals  which were made yesterday. Our delegation supports the proposal of a number.of Latin American  States to include in the Agenda the question of the duration of the term of office of members of  the Executive Board. Inasmuch as a number of delegates consider this question useful and worthy  of attention, we consider that it should be discussed by the General Conference.
