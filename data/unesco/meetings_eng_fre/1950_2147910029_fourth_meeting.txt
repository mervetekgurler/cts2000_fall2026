[1] The CHAIRMAN read a motion submitted  by the Belgian delegation to amend item C.2 as  follows:  Unesco shall contribute to researches bearing on the  social problems of our time liable to influence the  international understanding and social equilibrium of  the nations. (2) “Social tensions that threaten peace”, was  the phrase adopted throughout two years of work.  It would be unfortunate if such accredited  terms were extended to include other problems. [70] Professor PIAGET (Switzerland) said he  had no intention of discussing the question of  principle: his draft amendment was merely con-  cerned with the wording of item D. 26. Neverthe-  less, since the question of principle had been  raised, he urged that Unesco’s sole object should  be to ensure the best possible protection for  authors; the question was whether the con-  clusion of a universal convention would really be  an improvement. At the moment there were  wide discrepancies in the legislation of different  countries and the International Bureau of  Intellectual Property at Berne had solved the prob-  lem by adopting a reciprocal system, whereby  maximum rights were granted wherever possible  and minimum rights where the maximum could  not be obtained. In view of the differences in  prevailing practices, a universal convention should  be limited to the granting of minimum rights and  its result would be to restrict authors’ rights in  a number of countries, particularly in those where  those rights mattered to authors most. He there-  fore thought it essential to consult authors’  societies on the subject. That was why he had  suggested deferring discussion of that problem  until the time came to consider the Programme  for 1951.  [71] Mr. THOMAS (Director, Department of  Cultural Activities) emphasized that the problem  had been most carefully considered for several  years. In point of fact, it had been first raised  at the Preparatory Conference of Unesco. He  pointed out that item D.26 of the Basic Pro-  gramme was the outcome of full consultations  with governmental and non-governmental organ-
1zations and of the findings of an international  committee of experts set up in accordance with  the recommendation of the General Conference.  Mr. Thomas felt that the deletion suggested by  Professor Piaget might give the impression that  Unesco was taking a step backwards and thus  would disappoint all who were following its work  in that field.
