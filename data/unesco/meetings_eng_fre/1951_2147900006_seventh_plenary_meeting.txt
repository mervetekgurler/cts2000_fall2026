ZEISSL (Austria) — Mr. President, ladies and gentlemen, fellow delegates, we all know that such gatherings as ours have an atmosphere of their own—something imponderable, something not easily definable, yet in undisputable symptom of success or failure. As one who has had the honour and privilege to attend five of the six General Conferences of Unesco, I may venture to state that a happy change of atmosphere has been taking place. In days gone by there was much doubt and heart-searching and experimenting, some- times followed by a feeling of frustration, while now optimism, however cautious and restrained by certain provisos and warnings from our Director- General, is making itself felt. [3] Let us first look at the less favourable side of the situation. The Director-General has pointed out the financial difficulties and the restrictions that the arrears of some Member States have imposed upon the proper execution of the programme. But, in the financial situation of our Organization, is there not still another ambiguous feature requiring our full attention? TI think of the rising prices and the rising cost of living all over the world, resulting from certain politico-economic developments, the end of which cannot yet be foreseen. Was this not a factor of financial strain as early as 1950? Are there what I should call some “silent reserves” behind the figures of our budget, enabling us to maintain an equilibrium between programme and budget in case of a further rise of prices and wages? I think we should all be grateful if the Director- General could dispel misgiving on this score. Now let me say a few words about our own National Commission, not because I consider it so important for this Organization but because, I believe, its difficulties are the same as those of its sister- organizations in many lands. [5] It consists of 100 members, representative of all classes and shades of public opinion interested in the work of Unesco. We have tried to gather the most eminent people within their fold. But all of them have a heavy burden of professional or other work of their own upon their shoulders. Very often, therefore, it is not easy to reach the quorum when meetings are called, to receive important reports of members in due time, and so on. Then there is the question of the division of labour between Ministry and Commission, and the danger of overlapping work. The other faith—those convictions which must pre- cede deeds—is possessed by few. We must hold firmly to that faith if we are to do those deeds which the dictates of conscience and history tell us are our duty, in order to establish the perennial power of the spirit even in a world which, like our own, is so much ruled by force and haste.
