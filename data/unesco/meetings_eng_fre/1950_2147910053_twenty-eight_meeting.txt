5C/59 and 5C/56|.  [1] Mgr. HOCHWALT (United States of Amer-  ica), introducing the two above resolutions jointly  with Dr. Zook, dealt with them first from the  general point of view. Believing that the Declara-  tion of Human Rights was an extraordinary  project which required extraordinary attention  from Unesco, the United States delegation thought  that additional steps should be taken to bring if  to the knowledge of the whole world. In doc-  ument 5C/97, the United States delegation had  listed the advancement of the teaching about  human rights as the third major task of Unesco.  Unesco should begin by publicizing the Universal  Declaration, and the Director-General should  intensify his programme to make it known to the  world. The United States delegation therefore  proposed that a plan of action should be under-  taken, whose details would be the responsibility  of the Secretariat, but on which it might be  possible to indicate certain lines of action which  would ensure that the project was applied on a  Unesco-wide basis. Mgr. Hochwalt concluded by  outlining in general terms the types of activity  which might fall under that heading.  [2] Dr. ZOOK (United States of America) said  the delegates had all been very deeply impressed  at the afternoon meeting by the importance of  bearing in mind at all times the central objective  of peace and security. The United States delega-  tion was doing all it could to bring that about,  and the proposed programme had been drawn up  to that end. It was worth while realizing that  peace and security were problems for all the  Member States, not only in 1950, but also for  many years to come, and indeed at all times. The  Conference should also remember that it was a  group representing fifty-nine nations, and that it  had to proceed in a democratic manner. The  United States delegation had felt on occasion that  the Unesco Programme was too diffuse, and  Dr. Zook was sure that others had occasionally  felt the same about the proposals of the United
447  States delegation. It would be strange if the  Unesco Programme were ever really satisfactory  to any one nation or to all of them together, and  it therefore seemed to him that their task required  that they should all work together and never be  discouraged.  [3] The United States had identified certain  areas which they believed to be of particular  importance in that respect, notably Germany,  because they felt that peace and security were  threatened in those areas. ‘They believed that  respect for human rights was the basis for attain-  ing peace and security, and they therefore felt that  unusual steps should be taken to implement what  was in fact the basic element of Unesco’s Pro-  gramme.  [4] The United Nations were to be congratulated  on having formulated the Declaration which  Unesco had been privileged to approve at the  Beirut Conference. For his part, he almost  regretted that it was not Unesco which had taken  the lead in the matter, but at least Unesco could  extend its application. In that connexion, certain  resolutions had already been passed, in particular,  resolutions 1.35, 1.352 and 6.31. But that was not  vet enough. The Organization must not cease its  efforts to go forward with its Programme, which  was of such vital concern to peace and security.  [5] Dr. Zook wished to suggest, in the first  place, two amendments, originally proposed by  the United States delegation [document 5C/66],  but temporarily withdrawn at the time the re-  levant part of the Programme had been under  discussion. The first concerned item 1.313, deal-  ing with the preparation in 1951 of a seminar to  be held in 1952, and would have a budgetary im-  plication of between $3,000 and $4,000. The  second amendment was to insert in item 1.321  the words: “and the teaching about Human  \ights” after “the teaching of living languages”.  [6] In addition, his delegation put forward the  resolution contained in document 5C/56, which  proposed the planning of a long-range pro-  gramme designed to make the Universal Declara-  tion more widely known in the world. [10] Professor VERNIERS (Vice-Chairman, Ex-  ecutive Board) regretted that he was unable to say  what attitude would be taken by the Chairman of  the Executive Board with regard to those impor-  tant and complex proposals. As they had not  been submitted to the Executive Board, the latter  had not had to take a decision concerning either  the principles embodied in them or their bud-  getary implications. He therefore would be grate-  ful if the United States delegate would clarify  certain points of his proposal so that the Executive  Board might examine it methodically.  [11] The DEPUTY DIRECTOR-GENERAL thought  the United States proposal was undoubtedly one  of the most important which had come before  the Conference or the Commission. As both  Mgr. Hochwalt and Dr. Zook had indicated, the  matter at issue was one on which Unesco could  appeal to those parts of the population to whom  its ordinary programme made no appeal; it was  also a basic concept fundamental to the work of  the Organization. He agreed that Unesco could  not achieve its purposes until human rights were  recognized throughout the world. The Director-  General and the Secretariat were continually  searching for types of activity which would  appeal to the mass of the people, and for a large  and bold programme which might have a real  impact on world affairs.  The Deputy Director-General considered the  proposal in question a welcome addition to the  programme already under consideration. It re-  mained for the Commission to determine whether  they would refer this matter for examination to  the Executive Board in relation to the total Pro-  gramme, or whether they would decide on it  themselves. In any event, however, it was clear  that if it was to be carried out at all, it must be  done on a substantial basis and with adequate  funds.  The Deputy Director-General therefore sugges-  ted that the proposals should be referred to the  Budget Committee, so that the budgetary implica-  tions might be discovered at the earliest possible  date. It was clear that the proposal would be  among those items which would definitely carry  costs above the ceiling tentatively set by the Com-  mission at the outset of its deliberations, but he  felt it was a project which should not be under-  taken unless really adequate funds were available.  [12] Professor PIAGET (Switzerland) empha-  sized the value of the proposals, which would help  to strengthen contacts between Unesco and public  opinion in a field of considerable social and polit-  ical value. But he would like to go further than  the United States delegate and speak of education  in human rights in general. Certainly it was  useful and interesting to teach about human  rights, but that was an intellectual operation  which did not transform the conscience of school-                     children. It was necessary to go further and  educate them methodically by means of active  experience gained in school or out of school,  through team-work, so that mutual understanding  and tolerance might become a part of their daily  lives.  [13] Professor PHOTIADES (Greece) enthusias-  tically supported those proposals, which would be  an inspiration to the Conference in that they  restored to the Universal Declaration of Human  Rights its true meaning, which should be the  pivot of Unesco’s main educational activities. But  those rights implied obligations which could not  be overlooked by the Executive Board. However,  he was convinced that the additional expenditure  would be justified by the results. Increases in the  number of seminars and in cultural activities  were well-advised proposals which should lead the  members of the Commission to support the  request for additional financial aid outside the  budget.  [14] Dr. WALKER (Australia) thought the pro-  posals amounted to a shift in the balance of the  Organization, that was to say, a concentration on  certain major projects, including the one under  consideration. He personally was prepared to  accept such a shift, but it would mean sacri-  ficing other things, and delegates would have to  decide, at the late stage the Conference had  reached, whether to accept that shift and, if so,  what items to sacrifice.  The proposals were of two types: programme  resolutions and suggestions concerning working  methods and the structure of the Secretariat.  Questions concerning the sending of experts and  field teams and the appointment of a co-ordinator  were administrative points which should not  figure in programme resolutions. The elabora-  tion of a work plan was very important, and  Dr. Walker was glad to see that the representative  of the Secretary-General of the United Nations  was present and would be able to give the Com-  mission the probable reaction and comment of  that organization to the suggested programme  and to the ways in which it might or might not  fit in with the information activities of the United  Nations. It was exceedingly important that co-  ordination should be maintained with the United  Nations, with regard to both the programme and  the working methods proposed.  [16] The Australian delegation did not believe  that it would be possible to make any real  progress in the dissemination of an understanding  of the Declaration of Human Rights, unless it  were done through the Member States and  National Commissions. Unesco could agree on  resolutions, but their implementation was neces-
449  sarily a matter for the individual countries. Un-  less the national authorities in each Member  State accepted the burden of that task, money  would merely be spent without any positive  results, since the presentation of the question de-  pended entirely on local circumstances and condi-  tions. It was a question on which the Australian  delegation held very strong views, and their  attitude to the budget vote would be affected by  the way in which it was decided to carry out the  proposals.  [16] Referring to the remarks made by Pro-  fessor Piaget, Dr. Walker added that Unesco  should prepare, through the normal channels of  the Secretariat, a series of systematic recommen-  dations to Member States concerning teaching  about the Declaration of Human Rights. Those re-  commendations should include indications regard-  ing the stage of school education at which such  teaching would be profitable. It would be necessary  to take into account political tensions, and the  struggles and obstacles which stood in the way of  the dissemination of the Declaration, and also to  consider in what way schoolchildren should be  informed of the reasons for the lack of unanimity  in signing the Declaration. Such a series of re-  commendations could be presented to the 1951  Conference for its consideration, and might  constitute the first pilot model for such a series,  which did not yet exist in Unesco. [19] As to the concrete activities required, he  submitted that it was hardly possible for the Com-  mission to decide at that point what should be  involved, but he thought it might be useful to  consider it at the General Conference. [21] Dr. CHEDIAK (Cuba) said that the attitude  of his delegation was clear: the first draft of the  Universal Declaration of Human Rights had been  worked out by the Cuban delegation. At the  Third Session of the General Conference in Beirut,  Dr. Chediak had seconded a similar proposal from  the United States, on the grounds that it con-  stituted a further step towards world peace. The  Universal Declaration had been accepted by the  majority of Member States. That was a con-  siderable advance, but it was still necessary to  make that fundamental text a vital force at the  present time, when right-thinking people through-  out the world were demanding ever more  insistently respect for human freedoms. A new  stage had been reached. Strenuous and unceas-  ing efforts would be needed, which should not be  confined to supplying information to the press  but should result in definite measures that could  be effectively applied. In that connexion,  Dr. Chediak wished to submit an amendment to  the American proposal. There were a large  number of international organizations receiving  financial help from Unesco, and it was only right  that, in return, they should make an effective con-  tribution to the task of inculcating Unesco’s  fundamental ideals in the masses.  Those were the reasons that had prompted the  Cuban delegation to present a supplementary pro-  posal to be included in the 1951 programme as  new draft resolution 1.353:  The Director-General is instructed to collaborate with  international governmental organizations, and partic-  ularly the Inter-American Academy of International  Comparative Law, with a view to organizing, at their  joint cost, a seminar for studying the mission of the  United Nations and Unesco and, in particular, the  Universal Declaration of Human Rights. This seminar  would be attended by university students and by  regional public officials responsible for maintaining  contact with international institutions.  The Cuban delegation asked the Commission to  refer the above proposal to the Executive Board  for consideration, so that the Secretariat might  put it into execution.  [22] Mr. LARREA (Ecuador) also emphasized  that the main objectives of education, science and  culture could not be achieved unless the Universal  Declaration of Human Rights were applied. He  therefore proposed the following amendment to  the third paragraph of the United States draft  resolution [document 5C/56]:  The Director-General is authorized: to take steps for  the establishment in universities and colleges of special  Chairs for the study and explanation of the Universal  Declaration of Human Rights, with a view to the  incorporation of its principles in the legislation of  those Member States that have not yet fully recognized  them.  [23] Mr. KAYSER (France) realized the neces-  sity and urgency of disseminating those prin-  ciples, for that was the natural and normal task  of Unesco, whose work programme included in-  numerable references to the Universal Declaration  of Human Rights. The moment had come to  inculcate that fundamental theme in the minds of  all men, down to the very humblest, and Unesco  should encourage the work of the National Com-  missions in that direction. He had moreover  noted in France and in certain Central American  Republics results that were extremely encour-  aging.  [24] With regard to the budgetary implications,  it seemed useless to anticipate that the applica-  tion of the American proposal would involve con-  siderable expenditure. In fact, a fairly large  number of resolutions that had been adopted in  the chapter on Mass Communication opened the  door to activities directly relating to that pro-  posal. Mr. Kayser pointed out in particular that,  among the measures to be taken in 1951, the bud-  get for Mass Communication included an item  [document 5C/5 (IT), p. 243, paragraph xiv],  providing a sum of $17,000, under Travel on  Official Business, to enable Heads of Divisions and  senior Programme Specialists to visit North and  South America, India and South-East Asia, the  Middle East, and as many as possible of the coun-  tries of Europe, to strengthen personal contacts  with the National Commissions and with the  heads of press, film and radio groups. That item  would enable the proposals for the dissemination  of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights to  be implemented in all countries without any  danger of upsetting the equilibrium of the budget  by the establishment of new posts.  [25] Dr. JAMALI (Iraq) expressed his appreci-  ation for the United States proposals. He hoped  that, as suggested by Professor Piaget, the  dissemination of knowledge about the Universal  Declaration of Human Rights would take the form  of active experience, and not be confined to mere  theorizing. Progressive education on the question  was necessary, but it should also be borne in mind  that the issue was a practical one. There was a  grave disparity throughout the world between the  actual realities of life and the principles contained  in the Declaration. His United States colleagues  lived in a highly developed society in which it  was easy to practise what they preached; but  there were other corners of the world where
450                                                           certain rights were not accepted, or at least not  applied, and where practice was so much at  variance with the Declaration, that the latter  could not be disseminated in a simple and direct  manner. It therefore had to be adapted to each  particular situation, and at the same time be  progressively applied. Dr. Jamali therefore en-  tirely supported Dr. Walker's suggestion that the  National Commissions should be invited to co-  operate with Unesco in this field.  [26] There was also the practical difficulty of  whether instruction about the Declaration of  Human Rights should be given separately or as an  integral part of civics. The Iraqi delegation be-  lieved that it should be given side by side with  teaching about the United Nations and the United  Nations Charter. They did not believe that spe-  cial field-workers were necessary, since any  specialist in civics or sociology could include  teaching on the subject in the course of his  normal work. Unesco would not, therefore,  require any additional experts.  With regard to timing, Dr. Jamali felt that the  proposal had definitely come too late in the Con-  ference; but if it were really considered to be of  prime urgency, his delegation would always be  willing to give time to a revaluation of the Com-  mission's previous decisions, to give priority to  those which were of fundamental importance to  world peace and to Unesco’s objectives.  [127] Dr. PLAZA (Venezuela) also supported the  proposal, as it concerned one of Unesco’s essential  aims: the maintenance of peace. It was in the  minds of men that peace had its root. The basic  programme had frequently been criticized as being  too dispersed. The proposal under discussion  provided an opportunity of concentrating Unesco’s  efforts on the Universal Declaration of Human  Rights. It was necessary to make an immediate  choice of those main points in the American pro-  posal which could be usefully examined without  delay, and particularly those concerning the  inclusion in school curricula of teaching about  the Declaration.  [28] Mr. DURAN (United Nations) recalled what  had already been said in plenary meeting at the  beginning of the Conference, of the importance  attached by the Secretary-General of the United  Nations to Unesco’s activities in that direction.  He was certain the Secretary-General would be  most gratified at the unanimous and enthusiastic  response given by the Commission to the United  States proposal. Any programme prepared by the Ex-  ecutive Board in co-operation with the Secretariat  would be examined by the Administrative Com-  mittee on Co-ordination and would be the sub-  ject of constant consultation, so that no duplica-  tion would arise.  [30] Dr. ZOOK (United States of America) ex-  pressed his delegation’s appreciation of the  support given to their proposal by so many mem-  bers of the Commission.
