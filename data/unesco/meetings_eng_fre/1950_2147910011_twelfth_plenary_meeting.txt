[2] Mr. Ali Asghar HEKMAT (Persia) — Mr. Pre-  sident, dear colleagues, we are gathered here to-  night to render homage to five great men. It 1s,  indeed, a very agreeable duty, since these leaders  of human civilization, who lived in the four corners  of the world, have accomplished by their deeds  and works the same task that Unesco, following  in their path, means to accomplish. One of the  best results achieved by our Organization is the  fact that fifty-nine nations of the world have given  up their narrow national outlook and have, in  this meeting, chosen these great men of different  nationalities as citizens of the world. This 1s  indeed strong proof of the point that the human  mind is in search of human unity. For the same  reason the Persian delegation has the honour to  salute the memory of these great men and to thank  the Fifth Session of the Conference for organizing  this commemoration. If the aim of our Organ-
1zation is to strengthen the spiritual ties between  the different nations and to fortify the foundations  of peace in the world by intellectual understanding,  the same noble ambition moved these great men  in olden times. Confucius, with his laws and  morals, Avicenna with his philosophy and me-  dicine, Bach with his musical compositions, Bal-  zac with his great novels, Muratori with his works  on history, all of them have created the same ties  and have been likewise advocates of peace and  brotherhood. We are, therefore, paying tribute  to our pioneers.  [3] Since most of my colleagues know the life  and work of these great men better than myself,  I will limit my contribution to a few words on  Avicenna. The philosophical school and medical  system of Avicenna were a link connecting the  East with Hellenistic philosophy and medicine  and, after him, they reflected back again from the  East to the West, where his masterpieces continued  to rank among the classics taught in the great  universities up to the seventeenth century. Where  could be found a greater factor for drawing the  East closer to the West?  [4] A thousand years ago Avicenna put the  corner-stone of his lofty philosophy at the foot  of the Elburz Mountains, and it was much higher  and stronger than the mountains themselves.  What are the Elburz but a dense body always  rooted to the same place, while the refined philo-  sophy of Avicenna was a spiritual essence spread-  ing from its birthplace to the whole world. No  doubt the Middle Eastern countries owe a lot to  this great man because through him they got to  know each other better. Not only the Middle  Eastern countries, however, but the whole eof  mankind has been enriched by his work.  [5] In Avicenna’s Treatise on the Ascension of  the Prophet, which I happen to have with me  here, there is a beautiful passage which, with your  permission, I am going to quote, as it alludes to  the great men who accepted his teachings and can  be rightly applied to this gathering of learned  men from different nations. It may be translated  thus:  And any word uttered by someone, even a noble one,  if not accepted by the High Assembly, will not acquire  refinement and savour; because its acceptance gives  the world a soul, and a body without a soul is without  value. And not every word uttered would be accepted  by that Assembly, but only that which is pure and  without blemish in its meaning and in its sound, so  that it can be heard by the Assembly because its ears  are celestial and no gross matter can reach them.  Words must be spiritual in order to reach that high  summit.             [6] As you may know, Mr. President, the Persian  nation is about to glorify this great philosopher  and has taken several measures to celebrate his  millennium. We are erecting a monument on his  tomb in Hamadan, a mausoleum in the archi-  tectural style of the tenth century. We are  publishing some fourteen of the studies he wrote  in Persian. We are issuing five series of com-  memorative postage stamps, from 1947 to 1951.  But the most important of all these initiatives 1s  the organization of an international congress In  Teheran to which many scholars and learned men  of different countries will be invited in order that  they may contribute by their research work to our  knowledge of this great thinker. The purpose of  the Persian nation is not so much to glorify the  nationality of this great man, but rather to express  our appreciation of him in whose thoughts and  words we have already found the best means of  arriving at a good understanding with other  nations. I hope that many of you who are com-  memorating him tonight will join us in our com-  memoration in Persia in the near future. May  “service to science’ and “honour to savants” be  the motto of Unesco! [7]  [8] Dr. Pinhas KOHN (Israel) — Mr. President,  I have much pleasure in associating myself, on  behalf of the delegation of Israel, with the tribute  which has just been paid by the representative  of Persia to one of the giants of mediaeval science  and philosophy.  [9] Avicenna’s fame as a codifier of medicine,  as a commentator of Aristotle and as a philosopher  of wide and original range was outstanding  throughout the Middle Ages and beyond. He  brought to bear a mind of great originality and a  rare capacity for systematic treatment upon the  problems of logic, physics, mathematics, meta-  physics and medicine. His Canon of Medicine  was a standard textbook in the European uni-  versities until the middle of the seventeenth cen-  tury. It was translated into Hebrew and then  into Latin and it is recorded that in some univ-  ersities of Europe students of medicine were re-  quired to know Hebrew in order to be able to study  Avicenna’s basic treatise. Among the earliest  Hebrew prints in the Library of the University of  Jerusalem there is the Hebrew translation of  Avicenna’s Canon of Medicine.  [10] No less profound was Avicenna’s influence  in the sphere of philosophical thought. His Book  of Theorems was one of the classics of mediaeval  philosophy. He became known as the Aristotle of  the East and his powers of codification put him  indeed in the same category as his great Hellenic  prototype. Avicenna received the Aristotelian  heritage from the Nestorian Christians, but it was  in the form in which he presented and commented  upon the works of Aristotle that he influenced all  subsequent philosophical development of Arab,  Jewish and Christian thinkers in the Middle Ages  and during the era of the Renaissance. But Avi-  cenna was far more than a codifier and a com-  mentator of Aristotelian thought. He added to it  a rich contribution of his own. His system of  logic covers the whole range of philosophic  thought. In metaphysics he was influenced in  significant measure by Neo-Platonic ideas, which  he sought to bring into accord with the basic teach-  ings of his great Hellenic predecessor.  [11] The influence of Avicenna was long felt in  every sphere of mediaeval literature. Here, in the  city of Dante, attention may be drawn to the fact  that the cosmogenic system of the Divina Comedia  embodies a conception of the universe which is  based on the philosophic system developed by  Avicenna and his Arabic and Jewish successors,  and which was accepted at that time throughout  Europe as absolute truth.  [12] I should like to refer here in particular to  the close ties that connected Avicenna with the  great Hebrew philosophers of his age. He was  himself influenced by the work of a great Jewish  philosopher who preceded him, Isaac Israeli of  Kairouan, known to the scholastics as Isaac Judaeus.  In turn, Avicenna’s writings exercised a profound  influence on Jewish philosophers and scientists.  It was pre-eminently through the works of the  Jewish scholars and translators who lived and  worked in Spain, Italy and Southern France, that  Avicenna's ideas reached Christian Europe. Solo-  mon Ibn Gabirol, the great Hebrew poet and  philosopher, known to the Arabs and Christians  by the name of Avicebron, and Moses Maimonides,  most profound of Jewish philosophers, codifiers and  physicians, drew much of their knowledge of  Aristotle from Avicenna’s commentaries. Their  writings in turn exercised a strong influence on  the subsequent development of Arab philosophy  and on Christian scholasticism. It was a fruitful  give and take, and it was this co-operation which  kept the flame of learning alive during the long  centuries which elsewhere came to be known as  the Dark Ages.  [13] It seems appropriate that we should at this  hour and in this assembly recall the memory of  this great effort of intellectual search and co-            operation. The concepts evolved by Avicenna and  his successors reflect a view of life and of the  world which is still a permanent element of  Western civilization. To Avicenna and Maimo-  nides science and philosophy were not water-tight  compartments. They formed integral parts of a  comprehensive view of the universe. We have  moved a long way from that view and it  may be that the ills of our present age are in  significant measure due to that fundamental cleav-  age. Our scientists are becoming Increasingly  aware of the urgency of a new comprehensive  approach to the totality of spiritual and physical  phenomena. In that great endeavour they may  well derive inspiration from the stupendous  achievement of the great Persian thinker whose  anniversary is being celebrated this year. [17] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] We now come to the commemoration  of Johann Sebastian Bach. The delegation of  Egypt will again speak.  [18] Dr. Hussein FAWZY (Egypt) — [Transla-  tion from the French] Mr. President, fellow-  delegates, ladies and gentlemen, it is only right  that the Egyptian delegation should, along with  other countries, join in the solemn tributes that  Unesco is paying to the memory of Johann Sebas-  tian Bach. Not that Egypt has in the past played  any part in the development of Western music;  but by joining in this tribute, Egypt, with its  different historical background, gives striking  proof of the universality of Bach’s genius; for  both my delegation and Unesco this gesture sym-  bolizes the unity of peoples. Egyptian music  belongs to another current of civilization; it still  preserves its own modes and means of expression,  so unlike those uséd in Western music. Nothing  is more foreign to the taste of my compatriots, to  whom music still means a single line of melody,  than the harmonies of Western music. Yet,  owing to the evolution of modern Egypt and its  rapid assimilation of Western culture, wé have  among us a small band of amateurs and pro-  fessionals, for whom Bach stands as the same  towering, many-sided genius as for music-lovers  of the West.  [19] There is one striking fact that I have noticed  among my countrymen: Egyptian audiences, not  trained to value the importance of harmony in  music and with ears attuned to melody only, do  not seem to grasp the harmonic accompaniment,  which they almost entirely disregard, to con-  centrate on the line of melody. But when it  comes to polyphony, where there is no disregarding  the antiphonal melodies, interwoven in a clear,  close-knit sound pattern, the Egyptian listener is  struck by the architectural character of the music.  Hence the dominant part played by Bach in initiat-  ing the general public to the music of the  Western masters. I tell you this to stress the  educational value of the composer whose anni-  versary we are honouring; that value 1s of course  recognized in your colleges of music and con-  servatoires, but it takes on a special meaning from  the fact that I have mentioned. For us it is  not merely a question of studying the fugue as a  musical form and of penetrating its harmonic and  contrapuntal subtleties; what we find is a quite  exceptional means of helping the public to        appreciate great music. In my opinion, this  aspect deserves special consideration by Unesco  since it is our aim to discover the methods best  calculated to bring about an inter-penetration of  cultures to strengthen understanding between the  peoples.  [20] Following another train of thought, it 1s  pleasant to recall that, some time before Bach was  born to carry on the work of his predecessors,  Pierluigi da Palestrina, Olseghem and Josquin des  Prés and others, to whom we owe the Golden Age  of polyphony, it was in Florence itself that Count  Bardi and his music-loving friends gathered at the  opening of the seventeenth century to decide the  fate of the musical forms that were to usher in the  age of monody. Bach was born at a time when  music was turning to new ideas, whose source was  in Florence itself. He was to follow the older cur-  rent of ideas and to enrich it with his genius;  with him, one of the most glorious periods of  music came to a close. This accounts for the  eclipse of Johann Sebastian Bach in the period  immediately following his death. But the cantor  of St. Thomas in Leipzig was not a genius destined  to remain of mere academic interest: the Passions  according to St. John and St. Matthew, the B  Minor Mass, the chorales, toccatas, fugues, passa-  caglias, and the forty-eight preludes and fugues  of the “Well-Tempered Clavier” were not the kind  of works to lie buried under the dust of the past.  One of Bach’s sons, Karl Philipp Emmanuel, was  to divert the current by laying the foundations of  the sonata, a subtle and novel form more suited to  dramatic expression in pure music, but by reason  of its very subtlety far from attaining the arch-  itectural perfection of the fugue; men of genius  of the calibre of Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven  would be needed to bring the sonata to its ultimate  perfection of form and profundity of expression.  It was in the best days of the Viennese school that  the monodic current, which had its origin in  Florence, and the polyphonic current traceable  to Bach were re-united to inaugurate the richest  period of European music.  [21] At this time, it must be admitted, there  had yet been no general Bach revival, although  a few collections of his works were still used by  music teachers. Our present knowledge of Bach  is due to the toil and clearsightedness of Felix Men-  delssohn and also to the patient research and  learning of Spitta, whose biography of the com-  poser is a monument worthy of the cantor and a  landmark in the annals of musicology. We should  in all fairness associate in our tribute both Spitta  and Mendelssohn—these two men who have made  their mark in the history of music and musical  scholarship—for they have every claim to our  gratitude. Mr. President, ladies and gentlemen,
194  in celebrating the anniversary of Bach, Unesco is  fulfilling a pious duty directly related to its mis-  sion in seeking to build the city of Mankind;  Bach is one of its greatest sons. The Egyptian  delegation is proud to associate itself in the com-  memoration of the bicentenary of the death of  Johann Sebastian Bach. [23] Mr. WEN YUAN NING (China) — Mr. Pre-  sident, fellow delegates, ladies and gentlemen, do  you remember what Voltaire wrote when Vauve-  nargues praised Racine at the expense of Corneille?  Voltaire’s words to Vauvenargues deserve quota-  tion. This is what Voltaire said to young Vau-  venargues: ““A man of culture like you ought to  have preferences, but no exclusions.” We who  are gathered here now have come to do honour  to the memory of five great men of the past. And  if I speak of only one of them, this must not be  construed to mean that I exclude four of them  from my homage and admiration. I prefer to  speak only of Confucius, because I know that  others can speak with greater knowledge and  propriety of Avicenna, Bach, Muratori and Balzac.  [24] Confucius was a great humanist. When  asked by one of his disciples about death and the  after-life, his answer may be paraphrased thus:  “We know so little about life, why bother about  death and the after-life? Learn to live well, and  the rest will take care of itself.” This answer  reflects the typical Chinese attitude towards life,  and it is a humanistic attitude. To concern one-  self wholly with the problem of living, to learn  how to behave towards one’s parents, elders,  superiors, wives, children and friends, this is the  alpha and omega of Confucian teaching. Confu-  cius accepts man as he is, with all his appetites, his  desires, his vices even, his goodness, his ambitions.
1. See Annex, p. 201.          The Latin saying that “nothing which is human  is alien to me” is very Confucian. To Confucius  the ascetic, who denies himself the good things  of life, the hermit who eschews the society of  his fellow-beings, the saint who forsakes wife  and children and home in order to follow God,  they are not the men that Confucius would like  to see in Chinese society. The good man in Con-  fucian eyes 1s one who lives a full live of the  senses, the heart and the mind. But the appetites,  the emotions, the intellect and the body must not  be indulged to the extent of impairing the harmony  which should exist in any well-balanced life.  Hence the great Confucian doctrine of Chung  Yung Tse Tao, which can be translated as ‘“‘the  doctrine of the golden mean’. The appetites are  good and necessary to man’s happiness: they are  evil only when control is lost over them. Kind-  ness in man is good and desirable, but in excess  it is also harmful. Even truth and nothing but  the truth cannot be good for man, without re-  spect to time and place. In other words, accord-  ing to Confucian teaching, nothing is good in  itself: it is only good if it promotes the well-being  of human society as a whole. Confucius does not  conceive of a good human society as something  static. Being the great individualist that he was,  he expected every man to be different. But in  a well-ordered society, differences among men  should not be a cause of discord; differences in  points of view and in personalities should be  resolved in a harmony which is dynamic, like  the harmony in music, ever striving towards a  new equilibrium.  [25] To Confucius, the world, and not the partic-  ular state of Lu in which he happened to be  born and bred, was his home. He wanted har-  mony in the home extended to the State, and  from the State to the whole world of living men  and women. Hence his doctrine of the unity of  the world, of universal harmony. To bring this  about it is sufficient if every man were to behave  as a reasonable human being, observing the rule  of the golden mean, or in the words of the  Greek saying: in nothing too much.  [26] In the time of Confucius, China was in  intellectual, moral, material and spiritual chaos,  just like the world of today. Political philo-  sophers and sophists proposed all kinds of  nostrums to cure the ills of life. Have we not the  same situation today? What cure did Confucius  propose? Something very simple and yet pro-  found. The first step that men must take to  restore order out of chaos must be what he calls  the rectification of names. By this, Confucius  meant that words must be made to mean what  they mean. In times of moral degeneracy, he  knew that key words which expressed great ideas
195  were the first to suffer. Love, benevolence, filial  piety, respect, honesty, etc., meant very different  things from what they ought to mean. Murder  was never called murder, but something else.  Do we not find the same thing happening in our  time? What multitudes of sin and crime lurk at  this very moment behind the words democracy,  freedom, peace-loving, and equality? These words  are like oranges sucked dry, empty of all meaning.  They must be made flesh and blood again before  men and women can ever hope to see a better  world arising from the present sad chaos. Con-  fucius was a great leader who did restore life to  the great words of his time. Is it not the func-  tion of leaders now, 1s it not the function of  Unesco, to make the half-dead words such as  democracy, freedom, peace-loving and equality,  take life and fire once again, so that men and  women can be inspired to give even their lives,  if need be, in order that those great ideas may be  a living reality today?  [27] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] Fellow delegates, this closes the series  of tributes. In compliance with the decision of  the Executive Board, I call on Mrs. Montessori.  [28] Mrs. MONTESSORI (Italy) — [Translation  from the Italian] 1 think that, if we are to put  into practice Unesco’s great ideals, to which we  all whole-heartedly subscribe, we must enlist the  co-operation not only of those here present, but  of another category of beings who can help us  actively to achieve our aims. I refer to children.  For all men have, of course, at first been children.  It has been said by the poets that the child is  father to the man; but in speaking of children  I would like to emphasize a particular fact. From  the point of view with which I am concerned,  we must really take the small child, from birth  to the age of six, for that is the “‘formative”  period. Children, at that stage, are very unlike  ourselves, and we fail to understand them: we  leave them, so to speak, on one side, as if they  were of secondary importance. I would stress,  however, that this is the age at which most atten-  tion should be devoted to these children, not  merely if we want to produce better balanced  individuals, but also if we want to help civilization  to progress. The child is not only “father to the  man”; he is also an essential aid to the construc-  tion and advancement of civilization itself. This
1s generally overlooked, so that I feel it necessary  to mention it. Without the help of children we  could make no sustained and regular progress;  we should merely have to do what others had  done before us and, if heredity were all that  counted, that alone would make any real progress            impossible. Here lies our problem; this is the  most important point. It is, in the final analysis,  only the child who can absorb the characteristics  of the age into which he is born, incarnating  them so as to produce, or at least mould, con-  temporary man, as he develops.  [29] If it is one of our objects to bring about  understanding between men who are different in  character, our work must begin with the children,  not with the adults. Unesco’s ideals must be  applied in the education of the child from the  moment he enters this world. Is it not surpris-  ing, for instance, that within their first two years  of life children learn to speak their own native  tongue? At the outset, they know nothing; they  have no “awareness’; they can achieve nothing by  the exercise of the intellect, memory or observation  and concentration; but bv some higher faculty  they learn to speak their own language—language,  if we analyse it, being something really created  by man, man who thinks, man who evolves. How,  then, has the child been able to acquire it? Here  we are confronted with a number of factors to  which we have not paid sufficient attention, and  which show the importance of protecting this stage  of children’s existence, for it is the treasure-house  of the future. Children, however, possess not  only this particular psychic power, but other  psychic powers as well.  [30] We regard the child as a being that can have  no share in culture. Yet long experience has  proved that this is not so. The child, just as  he absorbs his environment, can of his own  accord absorb the rudiments of culture; and he  does this in so singular a manner that for us  it is a veritable lesson, a veritable revelation. In  absorbing culture he does not tire; indeed, he  feeds and fortifies his own intellect; he can work  but, in working, he strengthens himself. Children  have succeeded in combining freedom and disci-  pline, and a number of other lessons imparted  by education now applied in almost all schools  throughout the world have in fact been learnt from  children themselves. We should, then, pay great  attention to this particular period of childhood,  which also contains the seeds of a number of  undesirable developments, some of them, as we  know, resulting in a “deficient” type of man.  We need not elaborate on this fact, which is  common knowledge. It would however be well to  remember that, since children have these great  powers for good as well as the power to develop  in an undesirable direction, a great responsibility  rests upon society to protect and help them. They  should not be left to the care of their mothers  alone, as if we were still in the cave-dwelling age.  They need an environment in which all the  resources of modern science are employed;  mothers should be trained with that end in view;  there should be schools for teachers and for  parents. In that way the plant of “humanity”  would be watered as it should be.  [31] We have noted an interesting fact, and  that is that very small children can absorb a  large amount of culture. I will not mention all  the things they can learn, but will merely refer  to mathematics, geography, grammar and so forth.  The curious thing is that such accomplishments  are common not only to children of one race—to  those of Europe, let us say, or America—but  also to children of every race on earth, even the  most backward. We have examples from all over  world, concerning children in Central Africa and  in the South Sea Islands, as well as those in  more civilized countries like Japan, China, India  and all the nations of Europe and elsewhere. This  shows that there are common laws applying to  all children, which enable them to absorb a great  part of their environment in that particular way  and at that particular age; and those laws are  something uniting all the inhabitants of the earth,  giving them all the same introduction to life,  and making them all subject to the provisions of  those laws. Why, therefore, in order to correct  what we think is wrong, should we not begin  where, in fact, everything has its beginning?  [32] A curious fact has been noted in connexion  with writing at this period of childhood. We  can say with mathematical accuracy that it is  at the age of four that the child can learn to  write and thus acquire the basic tool of culture.  Why the age of four? One explanation 1s  physiological; this is the period at which the sense  of language naturally develops and at which at  is very easy, not only to learn other, foreign,  languages, but to learn how to express thoughts in  writing. If, therefore, for instance, there were  any plans for developing backward peoples (e.g.,  the great literacy campaigns), the problem to be  tackled would be that of the age at which it 1s  best to begin to teach the art of reading and  writing. The reply would undoubtedly be that  the best age was that of early childhood, that is  the creative period, from four to five years, and  even earlier. This is a very interesting point, since  the instruction given to adults often misses the  mark, whereas instruction imparted to children  is certain to be absorbed.  [33] If, on the other hand, it were desired to  provide for a short period of preparation, what  would the proper age for this be? The reply 1s  that an even earlier age would have to be selected.  The representative of Israel has confirmed to me  that, in the case of the immigrants to his country,  this problem arose in regard to the many children,  of all ages, that were involved. The question
196         considered most important was that of how to deal  with the small children under six years of age, and  the first thing done was to institute schools and  instruction for them. This is the real solution  that should be considered in cases of “emergency”.  [34] Not merely infants, but young people as a  whole are undoubtedly full of energy and resources,  to which we have paid insufficient attention. No doubt many of the reasons for his  renown in the Middle Ages no longer hold good.  Although Avicenna was for several centuries the  leading authority on medicine and enjoyed so
201  wide a reputation that Dante himself in his  Divine Comedy had no hesitation in placing him  alongside Socrates and Plato among the greatest  thinkers of mankind, today, as a result of the  progress of science since the Renaissance, his  findings have to a large extent been superseded  and the influence he exerted is of interest mainly  to the historian. The same considerations apply  to the humanities. Avicenna made a tremendous  contribution to the spread of Peripatetic ideas in  the mediaeval world and it is to him that many  of the most influential thinkers of this period  owed their acquaintance with the writings of  Aristotle. But the Renaissance, by introducing  scientific philological methods and seeking out  the authentic texts, brought modern thought into  direct touch with the very sources and is re-  sponsible for the neglect of so many thinkers who,  like Avicenna, had for several centuries served as  the main interpreters of, and links with, those  sources. On the other hand, Avicenna even  today is a living force in the world of philosophy.  He is a master acknowledged in Canada by all who  are influenced by scholastic thought and Thomist  philosophy. In Canada, students of philosophy  are aware of the important part he played in the  formulation of the major scholastic creeds. Their  masters teach them that the great schoolmen  drew largely on Avicenna in regard to such  matters as the classification of sciences. Several  of their main tenets on the hierarchy of beings,  the concept of “good”, etc., are traceable to their  acquaintance with Avicenna’s philosophy.  [66] For generations of Canadian intellectuals,  the name of Avicenna has been a richly instructive  symbol. Through him, our students have learned  that history records more than the names of  great conquerors and powerful monarchs who all  too often claim attention only because they were  able to ride roughshod over others; they have  learnt that an honoured place is set aside in  history for those who have dedicated their lives  to the pursuit of truth and the liberation of the  mind. The name of Avicenna also suggests to  them that in the world of the mind, abundant  lessons may be learned from conflicting ideas that  may be a source of progress. Scholastic thought  and, through it, the philosophic thought of  Canada, has been enriched by the intellectual  wealth of Avicenna. In one sense, Canada may  be said to be the direct heir of certain aspects of  the truth perceived by Avicenna. But his  influence does not stop there. For such is the  power of truth that even in a distorted and  weakened form, it passes on to other minds who  complete it, purify it and set it free; and so  Avicenna, by his influence on scholastic thought,  the impetus he imparted to it and the limitations  he imposed on it, is the indirect author of certain       trends whose repercussions are still discernible  in Canada.  [67] In commemorating the anniversary of  Avicenna, Canada wishes to pay tribute above all  to his pursuit of truth as a means of enriching  the lives of peoples and drawing them closer  together. The teachings of Avicenna contain a  special message for all who have Unesco’s mis-  ston at heart. For 900 years, he has served as a  proof that truth knows no frontiers and that the  progress of ideas depends on an intellectual  exchange that close collaboration between all  peoples alone makes possible. In their earlier days, these arts  knew many vicissitudes—periods of renown and  periods of eclipse—but never before were they so  widely appreciated as they are now, in our time.  We are astonished to note to what a degree  earlier generations—which for centuries past have  argued with considerable accuracy and profundity  about art and beauty—failed to realize how many  different forms might be taken by that same  beauty, which each civilization interpreted in its  own way, so that each could seldom or never  appreciate the interpretations of the other. 202  notion of beauty has been sufficiently generous  and all-embracing to include sculptures from the  Pacific Islands and the works of Praxiteles,  Angelico and Picasso, the Brandenburg concertos  and the “Sacre du Printemps’. It then transcends the form and  makes it as boundless as life itself.  [62] It is not true that the artist seeks beauty  as his immediate aim. The Romanticists sought  beauty in this way and, when they did so, beauty  always eluded them. Rodin, who was less of a  Romanticist than is generally believed, well under-  stood that fact: “I am not a dreamer, but a  mathematician, and my sculpture is good because  it is geometrical.” He also said: “Beauty is not  a starting point but a finishing point.” The  impact of a work on the spectator, reader or  listener, is through the radiations of its form, that  is to say through the substance that informs it.  He may, then, easily be mistaken and think that  the artist, in creating that work, was moved by  the same feelings as himself. But the artist's  mission is not to assemble familiar and accepted  sentiments, for which he has merely to provide  an outward symbol. I do not think that I am  profaning the deeply religious work of Bach or  Giotto or of the Romanesque fresco painters by  seriously doubting that their thoughts, when some  work was taking shape under their hands, were  specifically religious. When a conscious effort  was made to create religious art, it was too late;  the result was like colour-prints. And this was  because the artists had lost all religiousness. Had  they retained a deep feeling for religion, they  would not have been obliged to direct to it their  conscious thoughts.  [63] Bach, who liked to consider himself as a  mere artisan, tells us that we must not consciously  seek after noble feelings. The possessor of such  feelings has no need to seek them: Bach was  actuated above all by a “will to create”, as Valéry  has pointed out, and it is to this will to create  that we owe his art. Bach tackled his works by  way of the “façade”, to borrow an architectural  term, confident that if he produced a balanced  and coherent elevation the rest would fall into  place automatically, according to the principle  inherent in the design; and in so doing he pro-  duced a concentrated expression of his on person-  ality.
