2. Le PRESIDENT :  Je vous rappelle qu'à la suite du vote d'hier après-midi, il nous reste  à élire le cinquième Etat membre du Groupe électoral I qui fera partie du Groupe.  de rédaction et de négociation. Je donne la parole au représentant de la  Finlande, président du Groupe électoral I.
3.1 Mr SALMI (Finland):  Thank you. Mr President, distinguished colleagues, when I on Wednesday  and again yesterday asked for postponement of the election of the representatives  of our group in the Drafting and Negotiation Group I did so in order to gain some  time to find a consensus solution. A serious and sincere effort was being made  by one member of our group, the representative of the Netherlands, who was trying  to receive authorization from his government to withdraw his candidature and so  avoid the vote. Unfortunately, due to difficulties in communication, the auth-  orization finally came too late, shortly after the vote had already taken place.
3.2 Electoral Group I met this morning; at this meeting Ambassador  van Loosdrecht once again confirmed his offer to withdraw. On the other hand,  Ambassador Hummel of Switzerland also offered to withdraw, explaining that the  General Conference had in the vote indicated its preference concerning the two  remaining candidates, and stating that he was prepared to respect the will of the  General Conference. After a thorough discussion, the group finally decided that  in the spirit of the rules governing the composition and the work of the Drafting  and Negotiation Group, it was the preferences established by the electoral group  itself in an indicative vote that had to be respected. Accordingly, Group I  requested Ambassador Hummel not to insist on his withdrawal, which he accepted.  NOW, therefore, Group I has only one candidate for the remaining seat in the  Drafting and Negotiation Group. I wish to thank on behalf of the group both  Ambassador van Loosdrecht and Ambassador Hummel for the constructive spirit and  the generosity that they have shown in order to solve this question for the  benefit of us all. Thank you.

10.1 Mr BRATANOV (World Federation of Scientific Workers) (Statement made in  Bulgarian; English text supplied by the delegation):  Mr President, ladies and gentlemen, dear colleagues, it is a genuine  pleasure for me to convey to you, the participants in the twenty-third session of  Unesco General Conference, the most heartful and warm greeetings on behalf of the  World Federation of Scientific Workers, which is a member of Unesco according to  the statute of non-governmental organizations - category A. We approve of and  congratulate you on your highly noble and useful activity reflected in detail and  very constructively in the report of Mr Amadou-Mahtar M’Bow, your Director-  General, and at the same time we are looking forward to your new initiatives in  the difficult tasks of safeguarding the spiritual wealth and the cultural monu-  ments on our planet, of bringing literacy to millions of people, of diffusing  knowledge, education, science - all this to mould the new man, the creator of  cultural wealth.
10.2 The World Federation of Scientific Workers was founded in 1948 by a  group of progressive scientists among whom we have to mention the names of Pro-  fessors Frederic Joliot-Curie, John Bernal, Cecil Powel, Pierre Biquard, Eric  Burhop, Academicians Ivan Ivanovitch Artobolevski and Alexander Ivanovich Oparin  and others. For nearly 40 years now the Federation has been gaining strength and  today unifies more than 550,000 scientists from 47 countries. And for these 40  years it has remained one of the world organizations most dedicated to the cause  that science - this great creative power - should be used to improve the life of  people, should serve the progress and well-being of humanity.
10.3 At the same time, our Federation is struggling to preserve peace and  peaceful coexistence among peoples on our planet. All of its activities consist  of initiatives and events relating to the topics of how to use scientific  achievements for peaceful purposes, how to spread culture and science in the  thousands and millions of households and how scientific achievements could  penetrate in each house and warm with its rays the life of people.
10.4 Your rich, useful .and all-embracing activities for the development of  human culture and science, for the literacy of millions of young people takes a  very important place in and is closely related to the activity of our Federation  of Scientists. On many occasions our aims coincide and we work together on the  same front. For instance, our Federation has lately treated the problem of the  outflow of grey matter from the developing countries. This grey matter is a  precious capital, especially for small African and Asian countries. They must  keep it to themselves. As I said, our activities coincide in many areas. Thus,  all the 14 programmes of Unesco are built on scientific principles. Major Pro-  grammes VI, IX, X and XII1 are specially involved with science and our Federation  is ready to co-operate in their realization.
10.5 Dear colleagues, the world scientific community is today greatly con-  cerned by the fact that the most important achievements in the fields of  science - physics, chemistry, biology, genetics, immunology - can be used by some  war-mongering countries for the infernal aims of war. We scientists struggle  against such use of scientific result,s and believe that they should be used to  bring happiness and well-being to people.
10.6 The achievements of physics, especially nuclear physics are of special  importance in our century. Atomic energy should bring warmth and light into the  houses of millions of families, should set in motion the turbines and engines in  the factories, should be the motive power of human progress. What is the reality  which is troubling US today? The war bases have piled up a great amount of bombs  and missiles with atomic war heads. Only a small part of them is enough to  destroy the whole life on the earth. Imagine, dear colleagues, that these war-  heads were taken off and used for energy in the atomic power stations, then the  light which they will produce would enlighten our planet and it would be bright  forever.
10.7 The world scientific community is today greatly concerned by the fact  that if some warlike countries dare to use the achievements of chemistry and  biology - the science of life, which today has reached unprecedented summits  especially in the field of molecular biology, genetic and cell engineering - for  war aims, that would be a deadly danger for humanity.
10.8 Dear colleagues, the scientists unified in their Federation will con-  tinue to co-operate with the highly noble and useful activity of Unesco, which  contributes to the development of hurhan culture, science and education, to the  creation of a world without wars, in which culture and science penetrate every  house. On behalf of the thousands of members of our Federation, on behalf of its  Executive Council, may I wish success to Unesco in its future activities. The  speeches we have heard so far at this twenty-third session show that Unesco is a  vital necessity, that it should live, should grow, should develop for the welfare  of humanity. We promise you a still closer co-operation in your activity, which  is also ours - in the diffusion of knowleäge, science and culture in order to  safeguard world peace and to create a world without threats and wars. I wish you  new achievements in your difficult but noble dedication. Thank you for your  attention.

12.1 Mr NDOMAHINA (Sierra Leone) :  Mr President, Mr Chairman of the Executive Board, Mr Director-General,  distinguished delegates and observers, ladies and gentlemen, at the outset permit  me, Mr President, to associate myself with my distinguished colleagues in con-  gratulating Mr Todorov on his unanimous election to the high office of President  of this session. His rich background and experience, coupled with his qualities  as a stateman and your personal integrity, give me every confidence that he will  continue to guide the deliberations of this Conference to a successful end.
12.2 Let me also congratulate the other members of the General Committee who  will surely provide the necessary boost and support for ensuring the smooth run-  ning of the Conference. I should also like to convey my delegation's thanks to  the Chairman and members of the Executive Board for the work they have done, and  its high appreciation of the excellent report of the Director-General.
12.3 To the outgoing President, I would like to express our deep appreciation  of the grace and wisdom with which he carried out his onerous responsibilities at  the twenty-second session of the General Conference.
12.4 May I, Mr President, through you offer my delegation's sincere appreci-  ation to the Government and people of the Republic of Bulgaria for inviting us to  meet here in the colourful and historic surroundings of the city of Sofia and for  the wonderful welcome and hospitality accorded us so far.
12.5 Mr President, this twenty-third session of the General Conference of our  Organization is taking place at a crucial and perhaps critical time in its 40-  year history. Since the twenty-second session in 1983, Unesco has had calls from  a number of its members - including several founding members - for reforms.  These calls have been on occasion accompanied by threats of withdrawal of member-  ship from the Organization. Indeed, unfortunate as it is, one State has already  ceased to be a member of the Organization. Granted, it is enjoying an observer  status, but surely this is not an ideal situation. I would therefore like to  join in the numerous appeals to the government of that country to kindly review  the situation and rejoin this happy family in the interest of international co-  operation. We definitely miss you and will be more than happy to have you back  taking you rightful place.
12.6 Mr President, 40 years in the life of any body or organization is indeed  a long time. It covers the period of infancy, through childhood to adolescence  and approaching middle age. Going through these successive stages invariably  involves growth in many facets. And with this growth a number of changes. There  is therefore no denying of the fact that Unesco in its fortieth year must be in  need of changes and reforms. But surely these changes and reforms should not  necessarily mean its demise or destruction. Reforms, Mr President, can be  effected from within. It is in this vein that we earnestly appeal to all Member  States to stay in the Organization and, wlth the co-operative efforts of all -  the Member States, the Executive Board and the Secretariat - to secure the  reforms needed to revitalize the Organization.
12.7 It must be conceded that much work has been done to meet the calls for  reforms. The Temporary Committee established by the Executive Board at its 119th  session has d6ne an excellent job for which my delegation wishes to record its  appreciation. We would like to support the Committee's plea for a 'scrupulous  respect for Unesco's Constitution and faithful adherence to the decisions and  undertakings of the General Conference'. The principles underlying the creation  and functioning of the Organization should not be jeopardized. We welcome the  concrete measures for reform, in particular the strengthening of the role of  Unesco's governing bodies, the decentralization of activities, programme concen-  tration and evaluation, the improvement of budgeting techniques and staff  recruitment procedures.
12.8 Mr President, we have read with great interest the numerous documents  prepared for this Conference. We shall be making detailed comments on them in  the appropriate Commissions, but let me be permitted to offer some general com-  ments here.
12.9 The Sierra Leone delegation is in a large measure in agreement with the  preparation of this Conference. It shows, as usual, the hard work and dedication  put into it by all concerned. The consultation carried out with Member States,  Associate Members, international governmental and non-governmental organizations  provides a valuable range of opinions and suggestions, as enriching as they are  varied. We believe that somehow these ideas can be collated, condensed and  reduced to general principles. This in our view should lea5 to greater  concentration.
12.10 We note with satisfaction the giant strides already made in this  direction with the reduction in the number of suhprogrammes in document 23 C/5  (from 187 in document 22 C/5 to the present 151). We are of the opinion that  this move towards concentration should, as a by-product, lead to the concen-  tration of funds and resources, whose rational utilization should create the  widest possible impact. It is against this background that we note with satis-  faction the increase in the total amount of appropriations allocated to major  programmes as compared with currrent programme and budget.
12.11 Mr President, permit me to offer my delegation's appreciation of the  continuing efforts at decentralization. Side by side with this process goes the  restructuring of the Secretariat which continues to be of great interest to us.  What we are concerned with above all is for the Member States to actually be seen  to feel the benefit of the international co-operative efforts of the Organiz-  ation. A primary concern here is for Unesco to be 'in the field' where actual  contributions to education, science, culture and communication, we imagine, might  usefully be made. I venture to say, Mr President, that sometimes for this 'feel-  ing' of being in the field, speed is of the essence. As in many other fields of  activity, a lot of frustration can be felt at having to wait for a long period  between planning and actual execution of a project. We realize that certain  activities, by their nature, evoke a worldwide interest and so cannot possibly be  exécuted on a regional basis. Nevertheless, we contend that a good beginning is  being made in the plan to-transfer a number of posts from Headquarters to the  established Regional Offices, especially those for operational projects.
12.12 Of paramount importance to many developing countries in general, and to  Sierra Leone in particular, is Major Programme II. The Government of Sierra  Leone is committed, if not the total eradication of illiteracy (because of short-  age of adequate financial, technical and material resources), then to the gradual  and systematic elimination of this scourge. Illiteracy is estimated at 80 per  cent of our total population. We would wish to have it considerably reduced.  That is why we warmly support this major programme especially with the provision  that a greater proportion of the resources available be allocated for the train-  ing of literacy and primary education personnel. Needless to say, therefore, we  fervently support the Regional Programme for the Eradication of Illiteracy in  Africa, which is co-ordinated by the Unesco Regional Office for Education in  Africa (RREDA) in Dakar.
12.13 In all these programmes, prominence should be given to training activi-  ties. We hold the view, Mr President, that operational activities combined with  training programmes should enhance the ability of developing countries to improve  their overall development.
12.14 Mr President, together with our commitment to eradicate illiteracy if  possible in Sierra Leone goes our ardent desire to develop communication. The  dissemination of news and programmes is vital to the achievement of our develop-  ment goals. The Major Programme on Communication in the service of man is  designed 'to contribute to the establishment of a more equitable situation  regarding communication and media by encouraging the strengthening of the devel-
19  _oping countries' own capacities and by promoting a free flow and wider and better  balanced dissemination of information'. This aim is laudable. It implies that  national efforts have to be intensified and then complemented.
12.15 Sierra Leone, in cognizance of this, wholly supports the emphasis on  activities for strengthening communication capacities in general and in particu-  lar the International Programme for the Development of Communication (IPDC),  which has so €ar been doing a commendable job with the resources at its disposal.  It certainly deserves more generous support. The prerequisites for a proper dis-  semination of news include, as well as programmes and books, both the appropriate  infrastructures and equipment and the personnel. There is also the component of  adequate training necessary for manning and maintaining the activity.
12.16 We also appreciate, in particular, Unesco's programme for the creation  of regional networks to exchange information and ideas and to pool resources in  key areas such as educational innovation and development, educational adminis-  tration and management and science and technology. We welcome the renewed stress  on programmes relating to marine and terrestrial resources, the human environ-  ment, the International Hydrological Programme, the Man and the Biosphere pro-  gramme and the International Geological Correlation Programme, in which Sierra  Leone has been actively involved through her national committees.
12.17 Overall development, I dare say, could have many inputs among which  culture is a major element. There is no gainsaying the fact that every culture  could make a significant contribution to development. I believe, Mr President,  in the plurality of cultures enriching the lives of individuals and societies.  What I am advocating is the importance of recognizing the values of different  cultures. An interplay between them could lead to mutual understanding. With  the recognition of the positive values in each culture, the breaking down of  cultural barriers and all its consequences may soon be realized. We therefore  appreciate the fact that the Draft Programme and Budget for 1986-1987 calls upon  Unesco to promote knowledge of culture and to encourage greater awareness and  expression of cultural identity. It is also gratifying to note that Unesco has  taken up a range of activities highlighting the specificity and the universality  of the values of various cultures.
12.18 Let me reiterate yet again Sierra Leone's oft-stated determination to  associate her women and youth in all the cadres of decision-making and develop-  ment programmes. These two groups constitute a large proportion of the popu-  lation, so it is only proper for them to be actively involved not only in the  planning processes but also in the implementing and evaluation stages of the  nation's development plans. Questions relating to women affect in practice every  aspect of life in Sierra Leone, so a conscious effort is made to integrate the  role of women in the nation's affairs.
12.19 Our young people enjoy the same consideration. The rationale here is  that with a careful marriage of the old and the young, there will be a happy  blending of the two, with the young benefiting richly from the wisdom and experi-  ence of the former and the old gaining from the vigour and enthusiasm of the  latter. Indeed, even in the celebrations of International Youth Year, youth and  non-youth alike were actively involved. Government has always been aware of the  need to draw the youth movements closer to the centre of power. A clear mani-  festation of this is the appointment of a Youth Presidenta1 Aide to keep the Head  of State constantly in touch with youth affairs.
12.20 My delegation therefore appreciates the attention so far paid by Unesco  to youth-related programmes, especially in the context of the current Inter-  national Youth Year. We believe the youth is central to education and culture,  even as education and culture are central to youth. This reciprocal relationship  is very well brought out in various programmes at present being undertaken as and  outlined in document 23 C/5.
12.21 Mr President, the Government and people of Sierra Leone have taken note  of the comprehensive recommendations of the Executive Board concerning the Draft  Programme and Budget for 1986-1987 adopted at its 122nd session. My delegation  fully supports these recommendations. We believe that at a time like this, when  the Strengthening of the human spirit for the defence of peace is more necessary  than ever before and when the needs of Member States in the fields of education,  science, culture and communication are more urgent than ever before, it is in the  interest of the world community as a whole to reinforce Unesco in every way poss-  ible so that our Organization in its turn can help us more effectively and there-  by enable us to help ourselves in meeting .the challenges of national development  and world peace through international co-operation and peaceful coexistence.
12.22 It is in this spirit, Mr President, distinguished colleagues and  delegates all, that I would like to appeal to all Member States whose represen-
695    tatives are assembled here to adopt by consensus the Draft Programme and Budget  for 1986-1987,as a measure of solidarity and international co-operation in the  urgent tasks that lie ahead of us all. I thank you.

20.1 Mr MSIMANG (African National Congress):  Mr President, Mr Director-General of Unesco, honourable members of the  Executive Board, distinguished delegates, comrades and friends, it is my  privilege to represent the African National Congress at this twenty-third session  of the General Conference of Unesco. It is also an honour for me to congratulate  you, Mr President and the members of your General Committee on your election.  Having followed the business of the conference with great interest since the  beginning, we are satisfied that, given your wisdom and experience, solutions  will at the end emerge so that this Organization may continue to carry out with  increased vigour and motivation the functions for which it was established.
20.2 Through you, Mr President, the African National Congress would like to  convey its deepest appreciation to the Party, Government and people of the  People's Republic of Bulgaria for the warm hospitality extended to us and for the  excellent facilties placed at the disposal of the Conference. We are truly happy  and satisfied.
20.3 Mr President, in his introduction to the general policy debate, the  Director-General politely reminded this session of the circumstances that  hampered the smooth execution of his responsibilities. We could not help but  recall that, shortly after the submission of notice of withdrawal from Unesco by  the United States of America in December 1983, the Division on National Liber-  ation Movements at Unesco was singled out for destruction by a cowardly act of  .arson in March 1985. Whoever committed that criminal act of arson did so, in our  view, Mr President, tö further embarrass the Director-General. The cynical  desire to accuse Unêsco of promoting so-called terrorist organizations failed  lamentably, however, thanks to the Director-General's foresightedness in ensuring  that more than one Division kept records relating to the humanitarian work of the  national liberation movements.
20.4 Whilst we reiterate our condemnation of the criminal act, the African  National Congress extends its sympathy to the Director-General and solidarity  with his courageous bearing of this unfortunate incident of arson. We may, like  Shakespeare's Portia, be considered, Mr President, the unhappy cause of this  incident, though such an innocent cause we are.
20.5 Mr President, during the work of Programme Commission II last Monday, we  made the point that the oppressed black people of our country have never had a  say in the formulation of an education policy for South Africa. Different  colonial masters have at different times forced our people to accept various edu-  catjonal systems that had been decided for them. The objective of those edu-  cational systems has, without distinction, been the strengthening of the racist-  colonial oppression.
20.6 Twenty-three years ago, our youth and students rose to challenge the  iniquitous system of Bantu Education. In the last twenty months alone, Mr Presi-  dent, close to a thousand of our young people have been mercilessly killed by the  racist police and army. Scores of hundreds of others are either in detention,  brutally tortured, severly maimed or serving varying terms of imprisonment:  their crime is that they dared to oppose apartheid.
20.7 Mr President, in our troubled times, the word 'crisis' has been used so  much and so frequently that one sometimes suspects that it is losing its meaning.  And yet there is no word other than 'crisis' to describe the situation pertaining  to education in our country. In the current and ongoing political turmoil in  South Africa, our students and youth have been at the forefront of the struggle  against apartheid and Bantu Education in particular. Not a day goes past when  the public mass media do not report acts of resistance by our youth and students  against Bantu Education; against discrimination and against oppression and  exploitation. Rarely a day goes past when newspapers and television screens the  world over do not carry reports of unbridled violence committed by the racist  South African police and army against our unarmed people.
20.8 Many of the distinguished delegates to this Conference will have seen  television shots of the Fascist-army and police brutally whipping South African  students, using savage dogs against them and shooting, seemingly at random, at  groups of our young people. It is incredible but true, Mr President, that in one  instance the racist South African police were seen throwing tear-gas cannisters  into a preschool classroom of forty children aged from two to four years in  Soweto. All of them had to receive emergency hospital treatment.
20.9 Mr President, the African National Congress, the organization which I am  proud to represent here today, has opposed the system of Bantu Education from the  outset. We recognized then that the regime was using education as much as a  weapon to keep our people in subjugation as it uses its police, army and all  other institutions of national oppression. We firmly hold, in line with the prom-  ises of the Freedom Charter, the only constitutional document in South African  history which has the support of the mass of the people, that the doors of learn-  ing and culture shall be open to all. In the words of our President, Comrade  Oliver Tambo, this simple statement, Mr President, 'represents a cohitment to  move away from a state of ignorance, away from a condition of unknowing. It con-  stitutes an undertaking that, as a people, we shall seek to absorb the accumu-  lated wisdom of all civilization and, basing ourselves on that knowledge, strive  to contribute what we can to push back the frontiers of darkness'.
20.10 Thanks to the generosity of the Government of the United Republic of  Tanzania on the one hand, and the technical'assistance of Unesco, on the other,  the African National Congress was able to establish its first ever educational  institution at Morogoro in Tanzania in 1978. The institution known as the  Solomon Mahlangu Freedom College (or SOMAFCO as we refer to it) has three edu-  cational sectors comprising the nursery school, the primary school and the  secondary division. With effect from January 1986, adult education will be  formally introduced as the fourth sector. Some sixty kilometres to the North of  Morogoro there is established an Education Orientation Centre where our new  students are upgraded in mathematics, English and the natural sciences. It is  pertinent to note, Mr. President, that most of our students arrive from South  Africa with no exposure to these subjects. We are also in the process of estab-

It per-  sonifies the united commitment of the world community to end the crim-  inal apartheid system. It is a demonstration of the confidence of the  world community in the ability of the African National Congress and the  people of South Africa to bring into being a society that will be  governed by the noble ideals enshrined in the Freedom Charter.  Ever!? brick and every chair at SOMAFCO is the product of the common  labour of millions of people across the face of the globe: South  Africans, Tanzanians, Nigerians, the Danes, Zambians, Swedes,  Norwegians, Finns, the Soviets, the Dutch, Germans both from the German  Democratic Republic and the Federal Republic of Germany, the British,  Indians and many other peoples. To all these, we say thank you. We  also look forward to the further strengthening of this fraternity of  nations which underlies this co-operative venture.'  Mr President, inside racist South Africa, our people have not only been  in demonstrations against the system of Bantu Education. Strenuous  continue to be made, notably by organizations such as the Congress of  South African Students to work towards  the formulation of an Education Charter  for South Africa. Chief among its provisions is free compulsory education for  all. This initiative, Mr President, reflects the broad perspectives of Major  Programme II under consideration in one of the sub-organs of this Conference.
20.13 Thus can be seen, Mr President, a notable ferment taking place in and  outside South Africa in regard to education. The world is itself engaged on a  major campaign to eliminate illiteracy so as to provide a better quality of life  to its citizens. We also see our effort in this field of human endeavour as part  and parcel of this mighty current of change. As a people themselves with a  vision of a liberated people, we are determined that our country too will be rid  of the evil system of racism and apartheid; of superstitution and other backward  practices. With knowledge and learning, we shall be able to advance to new  heights the well-being of our people, including the interests of humanity as a  whole.
20.14 It is with this understanding that we support Major Programmes XII  and XIII, both of which signal a call to action against war and sources of con-  flict in the world. We are convinced that the accomplishment of these programmes  will significantly remove the threat to world peace.
20.15 Mr President, we are by no means s'O naive as to think that apartheid can  be removed purely by peaceful means. The apartheid regime is by definition a  violent regime. It maintains its rule over our people by force of arms. Large  communities of the Black people are uprooted from their traditional homes and  dumped in barren lands at the point of the gun and bayonet. It enforces the  system of Bantu education by sheer brute force.
20.16 Mr President, this morning we heard with an intense feeling of revulsion  and anger the news that the Pretoria racist regime had once again committed the  despicable act of judicial murder. Comrade Benjamin Moloise has been executed  for a crime he never committed. The worldwide pleas for the stay of execution  have fallen on deaf ears in Pretoria, largely because of South Africa's history  of naked violence. South Africa's brazen act of defiance in not heeding world  public opinion must be stopped. The world community, especially Member States of  Unesco, must ceaselessly work for the imposition of mandatory sanctions, includ-  ing the isolation of the racist South African regime in the fields of education  and culture. For our part, Mr President, we shall not let this crime go un-  punished. We can assure you that our people will not fail to demonstrate their  anger and hatred for the apartheid regime of terror.
20.17 As we pointed out earlier, we are dealing with a regime that finds  itself in a crisis. The growing strength of the national liberation movement in  our country has stunned the regime into bewilderment. The barbaric acts of  invasion and destabilization against all the Front-line States in Southern Africa  serve to underline Pretoria's strategy of desperation, when the struggle of the  oppressed people of South Africa is being waged within the borders of that  country.
20.18 Of late there have been contacts between my organization, the African  National Congress, the various strata of the South African white community,  mainly the intellectuals, industrialists and the main official opposition party,  the Progressive Federal Party. These contacts, as we see it, have served to  underline certain qualitative positions, namely: that the African National Con-  gress remains a real factor in the political life of South Africa; and, secondly,  that there is a growing awareness and acceptance of the validity of the policies  of the ANC, such as the establishment of a non-racial democratic form of society  in a future South Africa. The implications, Mr President, are that there is  developing inside South Africa a broadening'of the anti-apartheid base, signal-  ling a sharpening of contradictions within the ruling circles. The death-knell,  however, to apartheid oppression and exploitation will be ushered in by our  gallant fighters of Umkhonto We Sizwe. We therefore still need and will continue  to value all acts of solidarity and support from the progressive world.
20.19 We wish your Conference a successful outcome. Thank you, Sir.

28.11 ' S i  l'action n'a pas suivi le discours, c'est que toutes les conditions  n'étaient pas réunies : la masse critique de chercheurs n'est pas encore  atteinte, les équipements ne sont pas toujours disponibles et les fonds ne sont  pas suffisants dans beaucoup de cas.
28.12 Quant à la formation de cadres, notre Association en a fait son cheval  de bataille. Nous nous réjouissons des prévisions figurant dans le document
23 Cl5 au titre de la formation universitaire et postuniversitaire et de celle  des techniciens de laboratoire. De plus, la poursuite de l'effort en vue de  résoudre le problème de l'exode des compétences est saluée avec soulagement par  notre Association.
28.13 Les universités africaines sont interpellées en permanence pour ce qui  est de leur contribution au développement de l'Afrique. En février dernier  l'Association des universités africaines et la Commission économique pour  l'Afrique (CEA) ont organisé une conférence à Mbabane (Swaziland) à 1' intention  des responsables d'institutions d'enseignement supérieur en Afrique. Le thème de  ces assises était : "La réponse des institutions d'enseignement supérieur à la  détérioration des conditions sociales et économiques de l'Afrique''. Les problèmes  concernant la méthodologie de l'enseignement et de la recherche, les programmes  et les manuels qui ont été soulevés au cours de cette conférence constituent une  préoccupation permanente des res onsables des universités africaines. Nous nous  réjouissons des initiatives de 1 Unesco dans ces domaines et nous les soutenons  pleinement.
