[28] The Director-General feared that item 7 in section 1 might endanger one of the advantages of the adoption of the principle of biennial sessions: that of allowing the Director-General to give a wider view of the Organization's activities in his Report,
735 РООл as that Report would cover a two-year period, and of thus lightening the work of the Secretariat, for whom the production of an annual report was a very heavy burden. If the Committee and the General Conference considered an annual report necessary, the Director-General hoped it would be stated that the latter should be a mimeographed—not a printed —document, giving only a brief factual survey, instead of a complete and detailed account, as the Report of the Director-General had so far done. [29] With regard to section 5, concerning the Reports of - Member States, the Director-General hoped that the same policy would be followed as for “his own report—i.e. that only the reports for the Conference years would include comments and be designed for publication, while shorter documents would be prepared in the alternate years. [30] The Director-General thought it impossible for the work plans to indicate intentions regarding the timing of the implementation of the programme, in respect of the first and second year periods (item 3 of section 1). It was already difficult for the Ex- ecutive Board and the Secretariat to say at what exact time of the year a seminar, a committee of experts or a conference could be organized; it would be even more difficult when there might be a lapse of 40 months between the dates of drawing up a project and carrying it out. If the exact time for implementing any particular item had to be stated, the principle of biennial sessions might lose the ad- vantage of enabling some projects to be prepared in greater detail and with less haste. That would amount to a reversion to the method of annual pro- grammes. The budget would also run the risk of being badly balanced, as expenditure in 1953 might appear small compared with expenditure in 1954. Thus the sole advantage remaining in the principle of biennial sessions would be a lightening of the expenses incurred by Member States in sending their delegates to sessions of the General Conference; it would not allow the Executive Board and the Director-General greater latitude in carrying out their work.
