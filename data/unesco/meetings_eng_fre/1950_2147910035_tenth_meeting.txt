[1] The CHAIRMAN pointed out that at the end  of the previous meeting the Commission had  decided to establish a drafting sub-committee to  revise the English text of the preambles to the  Basic Programme. The sub-committee would  consist of three English-speaking members and  one French-speaking member.  He said that he was not qualified to serve on  the sub-committee, as his duty as Chairman was  to be impartial in the work of the Commission  and of its committees. He invited the Head of  the United Kingdom delegation to designate three  English-speaking persons whom he considered  qualified to form part of the drafting sub-  committee.  [2] Mr. HARDMAN (United Kingdom) put for-  ward the following names: Professor Armfelt  (United Kingdom), Dr. Stoddard (United States  of America) and Dr. Beaglehole (New Zealand).  He would support the Chairman’s proposal as to  the French member of the sub-committee.  [3] The CHAIRMAN would leave it to the  French delegation to designate a delegate or an  adviser who would collaborate with the three  English-speaking members. 18] and recalled that, the previous day, the  Director-General had emphasized the danger to  the Commission of taking an immediate decision  on the total budget figure: as certain countries  (whose contributions represented an important  sum) did not pay their contributions, the sums  voted would be purely theoretical and the  Director-General would not dispose in fact of suf-  ficient sums to carry out a normal programme. 321  Mr. Roger Seypoux (France)  reading document 5C/ADM/18, with which he had  not been familiar on the previous day, his misgiv-  ings had increased. Commenting on paragraphs 4  and 5, he said that, although the “priority activ-  ities” included in Part I of the 1951 Programme  should be entirely covered by a budget of  $8,205,000 (which, owing to the non-payment  of certain contributions, would be reduced to  $7,500,000), it was rather ironical to qualify the  activities of Part II as “priority” activities, for  their realization seemed unlikely. In his opinion,  the problem could not be raised in a more dif-  ficult fashion. The immediate fixing of the total  figure for the budget (it seemed unlikely that a  figure higher than $8,205,000 could be envisaged  at the present stage) would at once oblige the  Conference to cut down the programme in accord-  ance with the solution advocated by the Com-  mittee of the Administrative Commission. In fact,  the estimates would have to be reduced to  $7,600,000. But certain expenses which could  not be reduced would be higher in 1951 than they  had been in 1950. Increases in salaries, for  instance, would amount to $300,000. Moreover,  the number of Member States would probably be  increased to 59 during 1951, and that would  involve an increase in overheads such as doc-  uments, travelling, etc. The funds available for  the execution of the programme would have to  be reduced to about $7,200,000. It therefore  seemed indispensable that the Programme and  Budget Commission should endeavour to fix  higher budget ceiling. The Secretariat had done all it could in order  not to submit exaggerated budget estimates to the  Conference. The problem had arisen as to how  they should meet the deficit due to the non-  payment of contributions. The Administrative  Commission was inviting the Programme and  Budget Commission to answer that it intended,  not to find compensation, but to reduce Unesco’s  activities. [7] Mr. STEWART (Union of South Africa)  thought it essential that delegates should endeav-  our to obtain at once a clear idea of the budget  that could be expected in 1951. From experience  in 1950, it had seemed reasonable to expect that  about $8,000,000 might be collected; but, in  actual fact, this seemed an optimistic forecast.  There was striking evidence that the limit to  which Member States could be expected to con-  tinue their contributions had been overreached.  While the needs were admittedly great, the prime  responsibility of Member States was their national  sphere. The Executive Board and the Director-  General had urged them to increase their activities  on the national front, and these must be financed  by the governments themselves.  He hoped it would not be necessary to reduce  the budget, but he was prepared to support only  those proposals which would keep the budget  within reasonable limits in 1950 and enable  Member States to meet their obligations to the full.  His delegation was making appropriate recom-  mendations in the relevant commission. He  quoted, in support of his argument, the report  of the United Nations Advisory Committee on  Administrative and Budgetary Questions (doc-  ument 5C/OXR/4 - Annex I, page 2), and stressed  his anxiety that a suitable system of priorities  should be set up in Unesco. How-  ever, he thought a motion to this effect should  come rather from the delegations of countries hav-  ing a major share in financing Unesco.  [8] Mr. MASSAQUOI (Liberia) proposed de-  ferring the discussion until the following week,  when the Commission could be put in possession  of further data.  [9] Mr. HARDMAN (United Kingdom) summed  up the existing position. The Commission had  before it: firstly, the annual Programme and  Budget (assessed at $8,200,000); secondly, a  series of new resolutions (many of which,  although they had looked innocent to their  initiators, were found to involve financial provi-  sions by Unesco assessed at $600,000 to  $700,000); thirdly, the report of the Committee  of the Administrative Commission (according  to which the Organization could not reasonably  expect to receive more than $7,500,000, if the  $8,200,000 budget was accepted).  His delegation believed that the Conference  must arrive at a realistic budget total which  specified a series of programmes and activities,  ¢ 322  and contained no unappropriated money, but  which also included a reserve within whatever  total was adopted.  [10] Mr. HARDMAN therefore proposed that the  upper limit of the Budget should be fixed within  the $8,200,000, and he stated categorically that  his delegation would not vote for more. This  sum should also be used for purposes of assessing  contributions.  He supported the suggestion of the Admini-  strative Commission, contained in document 5C/  ADM /18 paragraph 5, to divide the Budget into  two parts, the first containing items of high  priority with a total of not more than $7,500,000,  and the second with the low priority items which  would bring the total to $8,200,000.  He further suggested that the Director-General  and the Executive Board should be requested to  regulate the level of expenditure in 1951, as well  as in 1950, so as not to exceed income in that  year.  [11] Professor PIAGET (Switzerland) hoped that  the problem would be put more realistically.  According to the estimates of the Swiss delega-  tion, each Swiss citizen paid 15 Swiss centimes  annually to Unesco's budget. Estimating the  population of the United States at 120,000,000, an  American citizen paid an amount equal to 10 Swiss  centimes. It was difficult to take too seriously the  figures being discussed by the Commission, as  they represented a mere trifle in the total ex-  penditure of each State.  The Swiss delegate confessed how disappointed  he was on reading the report contained in doc-  ument 5C/ADM/18. As it was the rôle of the  Administrative Commission to administer, the  latter, after qualifying the figure of $8,205,000  as a fictitious one, should have proposed an ad-  ministrative solution for meeting the deficit. But  it simply referred the problem back to the Pro-  gramme and Budget Commission. The deficit  due to the delay in payments was perhaps tem-  porary. Could not a provisional solution be  found, in the form of a loan, for instance?  [12] He therefore proposed the following pro-  cedure:
(1) To refer the matter back to the Admini-  strative Commission with the request that it  should examine one or more specific projects for  meeting the deficit;
(2) To pass on immediately to the examina-  tion of the Annual Programme, in order to be  able to adjust the budget estimates to the deci-  sions taken concerning that Programme.  [13] Mr. SEDAR SENGHOR (France) also  thought that the Conference must be realistic.                       The Director-General had submitted to the Ex-  ecutive Board a draft budget, the total figure of  which amounted to $9,500,000. The Executive  Board had reduced those estimates to $8,205,000,  a figure which it considered to be the minimum  for executing the Programme. Since then, how-  ever, new projects had been submitted to the Con-  ference and their execution would involve ad-  ditional expenditure. Moreover, owing to the  delay in the payment of contributions, it was  to be anticipated that the receipts would not  exceed $7,500,000.  [14] A very clear problem therefore arose: would  it be necessary to reduce Unesco's activities as  from 1951? No one could accept such a prospect,  and he felt the same disappointment as Professor  Piaget at the suggestions of the Administrative  Commission. He therefore proposed that the  Commission should refer the question back to the  Administrative Commission with a formal re-  quest to re-examine the matter and find means of  financing the programme. It might be possible  to consider shortening the time-limit for the pay-  ment of contributions, or else to work out estimates  for the payment of certain arrears, or to adopt  lhe idea of establishing a reserve lund.  Finally, account must be taken of such hard  realities as the urgent needs of humanity in the  fields of education, science and culture.  [15] The CHAIRMAN said that it was not the  Director-General who had originally presented a  draft budget of $9,600,000; that figure had been  established by the Secretariat after estimating the  expenditure which would be necessary for the  draft programme for 1951.  [16] Dr. JAMALI (Iraq) expressed his delega-  lion's appreciation of the Director-General’s objec-  tivity. The financial problem which faced them  arose from the difficulty experienced by certain  States in promptly providing the money required.  If Unesco attempted to carry out all the possible  programmes ahead of it, there would be no ceil-  ing to the budget. He therefore thought they  should work on two lines: firstly, as suggested  by Professor Piaget, the Administrative Commis-  sion should be asked to make further proposals  and secondly, a sub-committee should be set up  on the lines proposed by the delegate of the  Union of South Africa.
117] Dr. Jamali emphasized that the present  programme had been drawn up as a result of  long experience. Admittedly, some of the ad-  ditional resolutions might have been adopted  under particularly favourable conditions; but he  believed that, in the circumstances, the whole  basis of Unesco’s activities should be reorganized  and three sets of priorities recognized: (a) the  reconstruction of the human spirit; (b) the ex-  change of ideas between nations and the implemen-  tation of the United Nations ideals; (c) creative  work, scientific research, the arts and humanities.  In the view of his delegation, this latter group  had a low priority, since the activities involved  would be continued even without Unesco’s inter-  vention. It was therefore on this group that  economies could be made.  Dr. Jamali supported the proposal for the  appointment of a sub-committee to evaluate and  revalue Unesco’s 1951 Programme along those  lines.  [18] Dr. WALKER (Australia) stated that, in  the view of his delegation, they should press on  and try to reach a decision at once, because it  was unlikely that the Administrative Commission  could produce any different ideas if the matter  were referred back to it. Besides, the whole work  of the Conference would be paralysed if the size  of the budget were not determined at the earliest  possible moment.  [19] Those delegates who had referred to the  danger of reducing the 1951 Budget, as compared  to that of 1950, were under a misunderstanding.  It was unlikely that the maximum income of  $7,600,000 estimated by the Administrative Com-  mission for 1950 would be exceeded: but it was  also unlikely, in view of the assurances received  by the Executive Board from the Director-General,  that the budget of the current Financial Year  would reach $7,500,000.  [20] The opinion of the Australian delegation  was that the time had come when the budgets of  the various Specialized Agencies of the United  Nations should be stabilized. This was not to  place a limit on their work. Indeed, they knew  that the Technical Assistance Programme would  call for a supreme effort from them during the  next years; but, more than anything else, it  should establish Unesco as a force at the present  time. Therefore, if they pressed for a stabiliza-  tion of the budget, it did not mean that it  could not be expanded. However, his instructions  would not permit him to vote for a budget in  excess of $8,000,000. They believed that Unesco  could nevertheless continue to flourish and de-  velop, provided Member States themselves  shouldered some of the responsibilities and under-  took Unesco’s activities wherever possible, and  provided that they concentrated their pro-  grammes. The 1951 Programme should be  centred on three major problems, apart from the  established routine: fundamental education, tech-  nical aid and education in relation to the Uni-  versal Declaration of Human Rights.              The Australian delegation could neither support  programmes involving extra expenditure, nor  make snap decisions during a Conference, since  they had no time to consult their Governments  adequately on the new proposals.  Dr. Walker therefore proposed adopting the  budget of $8,000,000 for 1951, including a reserve  for the non-payment of contributions.  [21] The CHAIRMAN remarked that the Chair-  man of the Administrative Commission would no  doubt be happy to answer the criticism levelled  at his Commission’s report. He therefore pro-  posed inviting him to attend the meeting. Agreed.  [22] The DIRECTOR-GENERAL wished to give  additional information on several points raised by  the Australian delegate.  The budget of $8,000,000 was not a stabilized  budget. It was impossible to conceive of a  stabilized budget, considering that, in 1951,  $300,000 would have to be devoted to the increase  in the salaries of the personnel and would there-  fore be deducted from the funds set aside for the  activities of the programme departments.  In February 1950, the Director-General had  promised to slow down the execution of the 1950  programme while awaiting a decision by the Flor-  ence Conference with regard to the covering of the  deficit. But that slowing down had been partic-  ularly disastrous for the Secretariat, as it had  mainly concerned the programme departments  (frequent changes in the structure of the admini-  strative bureaux would be impossible).  [23] The Director-General therefore asked the  Conference to grant him for 1951 a real budget,  in keeping with a real programme.  He also stated that the concentration of the  Programme asked of Unesco would not be possible  without a suitable budget. If Unesco had to  devote itself exclusively to two, three or {four  activities, it would be impossible to devote to  them the same budget as in the case of a balanced  programme. It was altogether too simple a solu-  tion to reduce the field of work at the same time  as the budget.  The tragic facts of life were more important  than budgetary realities. If Unesco did not keep  its promises, it would not be taken seriously and  the world would never believe that it found such  great difficulties in obtaining funds in the present  circumstances, with its 59 Member States. [32] As regarded 1951, it was for the Pro-  gramme and Budget Commission to suggest the  ceiling.
