1. The PRESIDENT:  I declare open the seventh plenary meeting, and I give the floor to the first speaker on our  list today, Mr Bertel Haarder, Minister of Education, head of the delegation of Denmark.
2.1 Mr HAARDER (Denmark):  Mr President, it must have been a tremendous task for the Director-General to prepare the  Draft Medium-Term Plan. It has been his duty to try to harmonize the many different trends in,  and attitudes of, Member States, and we commend him warmly on his efforts. We realize that  there are limits to what the Director-General is prepared to desighate as activities that are  obsolete or have proved less useful than had been expected. Bul, even so, we see it as a special  obligation for the Director-General, with his unique insight and experience, to present to us in  an impartial manner options for the concentration of activities and lo suggest an order of  priorities.
2.2 Many targets have indeed been set for Unesco's activities in the next six years, and ! cannot  help feeling that there is a lack of harmony between ambitions and resources in the Draft Medium-  Term Plan. Even if all the themes mentioned can be accepted as relevant to Unesco's role, one  has to ask whether it will be possible to make a substantial impact in such a large number of  fields. It seems rather like watering 1, 000 plants with one drop of water for each of them. I  hope, therefore, that we will be able to limit further the proposed fields of action when we take  a position on the first specific two-year programme under the Plan.
2.3 Unesco is an organization in which a great deal of reflection on problems and trends in the  international community is carried out. But it is also an organization from which the inter-  national community demands practical and down-to-earth action. It sometimes seems to be quite  difficult for Unesco to meet these demands.
2.4 1 am concerned that many of the studies proposed are described in such general and  imprecise terms that it seems doubtful whether they would add anything new to what we already  know. I do not underestimate the merits of all Unesco's studies. However, Denmark would have  preferred much stronger emphasis on action-oriented programmes. I wish, in particular, to  stress the utility of pilot projects. Such projects give us the opportunity to test the practical  value of theories, and the lessons to be drawn from them should subsequently be made available  to all interested Member States.
2.5 The world of today is marked by unacceptable inequalities, some of which fall within  Unesco's field of competence. The Danish Government is satisfied to note that one of the guiding  principles in the Draft Medium-Term Plan is that of attempting to reduce these inequalities. I  am thinking of access to education and training, access to knowledge, access for nations to the  use of their own national resources. I also have in mind information and ideas which should flow  within and across national frontiers. We welcome the suggestion on the way in which inequalities  between the sexes might be done away with, especially with regard to access to education and  training. However, Denmark shares the view of the other Nordic countries that these questions  deserve a more prominent place in the Plan. Tne Nordic countries have tabled a draft resolution  to that effect.
2.6 Mr President, Denmark has always believed that it has obligation to take part in Unesco's  debates of global scope. We have learned a great deal from doing so, and acquired a better  understanding of the conditions and preferences of other countries and other cultures. We have  also seen how much can be achieved by international co-operation when intentions and ideas are
194   merged into a consensus. On the other hand, we have also realized that there can and must be  limits to consensus --when a consensus conceals more than it reveals.
2.7 We experienced this most clearly in the debate which, more than any other debate in recent  years, brought Unesco into the limelight: the debate on the role of the mass media. My Govern-  ment has been encouraged to see how Unesco has been able to increase its assistance to develop-  ing countries wishing to build up a communication structure. Denmark remains prepared to  engage, in collaboration with Unesco, in further well-defined and well-prepared communication  projects in the Third World. Our attitude was also expressed in the former Danish Governments  letter to the Director-General in June this year on the significant work that is being carried out  under the International Programme for the Development of Communication.
2.8 Mr President, we very much appreciate the Director-General's observations and explana-  tory remarks at the autumn session of the Executive Board during the discussion on Major Pro-  gramme III, But we still have a number of reservations on the formulations and proposals set  out in that programme. I am referring in particular to the proposed activities of a theoretical  nature--activities which might engender renewed attempts to set standards for the work of the  media. We would also have liked to have seen stronger emphasis placed on initiatives of a  practical nature.
2.9 For my Government, the demand for intellectual freedom, freedom of opinion, of con-  science, of speech and of the press is the very foundation of democracy and the first essential  for upholding other human rights. If there is no freedom of travel or of written and oral  expression, all other rights can be suppressed without anybody knowing it outside the country in  question. On the other hand, where fundamental civil rights are enjoyed, protests against exploi-  tation can always be made and the struggle for social and human rights can constantly be carried  on.
2.10 Il is therefore no use equating all sorts of social rights with rights to freedoms. For if  there were no rights Lo freedoms, nobody would be able to verify whether the other rights were  respected, and this would create a fertile soil for the springing up of the tyranny latent in every  place where human beings live together.
2.11 Unfortunately, we cannot create the perfect society. But we can restrict encroachments on  human rights and freedoms by those who mistakenly believe they can do this--if we defend the  right to protest and to resist. Freedom of speech~-which in practice ne ans the right to oppose  and gainsay those in power--is not a freedom reserved for the media. It is a fundamental human  right, which it is the duty--and privilege--of Unesco to defend everywhere, regardless of poli-  tical and social orders.
2.12 Let us never forget that Unesco was founded at a point in history when the world had seen  the tragedy which will always occur when the individual is oppressed by totalitarian regimes--  which only recognize the right to wheedle those in power. To Unesco, the defence of human  rights, and in particular fundamental human rights, must be the most important means of  obtaining the supreme aim of mankind: peace.
2.13 Mr President, in the view of my Government, peace and human rights are inseparable.  There can be no peace without respect for the rights of the individual. Peace begins in the minds  of men. There is nothing to indicate today that there are any grounds for reducing or weakening  that defence. On the contrary. It is therefore most unfortunate that the period between the first  and second Medium-Term Plans has seen, implicitly at least, a degradation and weakening of  the rights of the individual.
2.14 The rights of the individual are defined in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and  in other human rights instruments. My Government believes it imperative that the concepts of  human rights be described with intellectual clarity and logic and in conformity with Unesco's  Constitution, We will work to that end during this session.
2.15 We note with some regret that the references in the Draft Plan to the teaching of human  rights are far from sufficient. For instance, there is no reference to the very important seven-  year programme for the development of human rights teaching adopted by the General Con-  ference at its twenty-first session.
2.16 We support the efforts to counteract all forms of intolerance. The most abominable form  of intolerance is racism. But let us not underrate the dangers inherent in religious, philosophi-  cal and ideological intolerance.
2.17 There are other important areas in which Unesco should endeavour to safeguard con-  victions and values. I have in mind, particularly, education and cultural life. With regard to  education, we would be well advised to remember that historical traditions and economic and  political conditions differ widely from country to country.
2.18 The Draft Medium-Term Plan contains many views and recommendations which my  country can readily endorse. But we would also have liked to see a distinct reference to the  values that may lie in a pluralistic education system in individual countries. The government  has its role to play. But it is equally important that parents too should have the opportunity to  create or superintend the schools which they want their children to attend. Also, in those areas  where the school is following ideas that differ from those favoured by the majority, itis a  matter of minority rights. Freedom in educational policy is simply a question of freedom in  society. How can there be freedom if children are divorced from their parents’ values and  convictions?
2.19 The same freedom to be different from the majority must also apply to cultural life.  Denmark has therefore noted with the greatest satisfaction that the Mexico City Declaration  emphasizes the great importance of securing freedom and pluralism in cultural self-expression.  The Nordic countries will present a draft resolution on that subject designed specifically to  safeguard the cultural rights of ethnic and cultural minorities in individual countries.
2.20 Mr President, Unesco, to an even higher degree than before, should serve as a forum in  which peoples from all over the world can meet to present their own cultural values and experi-  ences. Other peoples can thus freely draw on those inspirations which they feel they can use in  their own conditions. Now and then it might be desirable, or perhaps even necessary, to leave  it to Unesco to formulate some common guidelines which might stimulate and facilitate the  cultural relations between our countries. Denmark will always be ready to participate in a  positive spirit in common deliberations and decision-taking on that question.
2.21 But our search for consensus should never be allowed to override values which are held  to be essential by one specific country or one specific culture. Let us not forget that the  differences in our cultures make up our collective cultural wealth, a wealth we may use  according to our different needs and wishes. It is only if we are allowed to cherish our own  values that we will be able to understand that others should be allowed to cherish theirs.
2.22 Mr President, we cannot ignore the fact that the world is going through a period in which  there seems to be no prospect of a speedy improvement of its economy. It would therefore be  unrealistic to expect a marked growth in Unesco's resources during the next few years. In the  circumstances, it is more necessary than ever that Unesco should demonstrate the greatest  sense of efficiency, economy and rationality.
2.23 The Draft Plan includes a projection regarding the expansion of Headquarters staff. My  Government cannot support such an expansion. We find it unnecessary and, in the longer term,  potentially inadvisable. Instead of this expansion we had hoped to see a much bolder plan for the  decentralization of the Organization's work and its decision-making procedures. Rather than  expanding Headquarters, further steps should have been taken to strengthen the Regional Offices  so that a much larger number of decisions could be taken as close as possible to where Unesco's  activities are undertaken. With the present structure of the Organization, we see no reason to  fear that increased decentralization would weaken the central management. It would rather  strengthen it.
2.24 We have before us a Draft Plan which outlines Unesco's activities for the next six years.  But let us not overlook the fact that these activities will extend far beyond the scope of the  regular programme and budget. A great number of international and regional organizations are,  in various ways, channelling financial resources to developing countries through Unesco. The  confidence thereby placed in Unesco is of crucial importance to the Organization's reputation,  not only in the developing countries but also, and very much so, in the countries which contrib=  ute these funds for development.
2.25 Like the other Nordic countries, Denmark will support initiatives that serve to strengthen  Unesco's role in this action-oriented work in the Third World.
2.26 Mr President, finally, as a supplement to my prepared statement, allow me to add the  following words: I have learned from my delegation that a decision on a major procedural matter  has been adopted in haste. The consequence of this decision might be that my delegation will  have to confine itself to taking note of the Draft Plan. I am hopeful, however, that the General  Conference will be able to adopt a Plan on which we can agree. Thank you, Mr President.
3. THE PRESIDENT:  I thank the head of the delegation of Denmark. I give the floor to The Hon. Datuk Sulaiman  bin Haji Daud, Minister of Education, head of the delegation of Malaysia.
4.1 Datuk SULAIMAN BIN HAJI DAUD (Malaysia):  Thank you, Mr President. Firstly, 1 would like to congratulate you on being selected to  chair this meeting this morning, Mr President, and at the same time to record the Malaysian  delegation's congratulations to the President of the General Conference, Mr Ivo Margan, on his  re-election to this high office. The unanimity of our choice is indeed testimony to his wise and  able leadership.
4.2 For the next two weeks, we shall be gathered here in a meeting of minds to disucss the  Draft Medium-Term Plan for 1984-1989, a document which is the product of Unesco's diligent  study and of meticulous consultations with Member States and experts. The Draft Plan is indeed  à compendium of global problems which will greatly assist us in our deliberations in the search  for their alleviation, and, if possible, solution. It is, therefore, a document of paramount im-  portance, intended to serve as a guideline for our Organization's activities for the remainder of  this decade. At this juncture, I would be failing in my duty if I did not publicly record and  express my delegation's appreciation and congratulations to the Director-General, the Sec-  retariat and all involved for their excellent work and contributions towards the preparation of  the Draft Medium-Term Plan.
4.3 Mr President, it is indeed heartening to know that our Organization has grown in the past  three and a half decades from a membership of 20 to its present strength of 158, The increase  has come mainly from developing and newly independent nations, whose aspirations and pri-  orities are necessarily directed towards meeting basic needs such as education, the elimination  of illiteracy, food, nutrition, health, housing and the development of infrastructures for rapid  socio-economic development. Consequently, it may be necessary now for our Organization to  shift its emphasis more firmly in the direction of action-oriented programmes that serve the  needs of the vast majority of its Member States, in keeping with the changing character of its  membership. In fact, the recent influx of newly independent nations from developing regions is  a timely reminder of the urgent need to make such appropriate shifts in Unesco's programme  orientations.
4.4 Activities involving philosophical reflection and co-operation on the intellectual plane are  perhaps of relatively less priority and may not be of immediate and urgent relevance to many  of us in the developing countries. What we require now, I feel, is more assistance in the formu-  lation of policies and strategies with a view to finding practical solutions to problems and in the  implementation of practical programmes to help in our development process. In fact, the lucid  statement of the world's problems contained in the First Part of the Draft Medium-Term Plan  are ample evidence that our Organization has already accumulated a vast store of knowledge and  experience regarding research into world problems.
4.5 I am of the view, Mr President, that we are now at a stage in our Organization's history  where we could perhaps afford to place less emphasis on philosophical reflections and studies  and concentrate more on action-oriented programmes. In the light of these considerations, I  would like to suggest that Major Programme I, 'Reflection on world problems and future-  oriented studies', should be assimilated with the ongoing programmes in the different areas of  Unesco's competence, as has been the practice up to now. To do otherwise would not only mean  a diversion of our already limited resources away from more important areas, but might also  take Unesco beyond its fields of competence, as well as having implications as regards finance  and personnel. As it is, the major part of our Organization's resources has of necessity been  devoted to staff costs, and I believe that we should be wary of allowing this item of expenditure  to increase further.
4.6 Notwithstanding these observations, Mr President, we consider that the Draft Medium-Term  Plan is a generally well-conceived document, thanks to the wisdom of the Director-General and  his staff, who have worked assiduously in its preparation. While Uresco obviously has a promi-  nent role to play in securing international collaboration and co-operation to ensure its success-  ful implementation, it is also important and imperative for Member States, both developed and  developing, to strive harder to assist one another in all fields of endeavour and to extend aid  and assistance to those who are in need of them. Assistance of th:s kind need not necessarily  come mainly from the developed nations; many developing nations are now also in a position to  provide such help. In this context, we in Malaysia--a developing nation ourselves--have for  some years been rendering assistance through a Programme of Technical Co-operation to a  number of newly independent states, especially in Asia and the Pacific, and also in Africa. Not  only will we continue to render such assistance, Mr President, but we intend to increase its  scope, believing that couniries which have the necessary expertise, e::perience and facilities,  and which are in a position to share them with others, should step forward readily to do so in  the cause of humanity and progress.
4.7 It is our hope that in due course a network of such programmes of arsistance will be built  up among developing nations throughout the world, thus contributing towards the realization of  the goal of making ourselves more self-reliant.
4.8 Besides this type of assistance, the developing nations should also pool their resources and  expertise in the pursuit of commen goals. A recent example is to be found in the collaboration  between Islamic nations on the proposed establishment of an International Islamic University in  Malaysia, which will help to provide more opportunities for higher education. This proposal has  received unanimous endorsement and strong support from member countries of the Organization  of Islamic Countries, some of which have already given firm commitments. We are most grates  ful for such strong support and encouragement. My country, Malaysia, is indeed very proud to  have been given the honour of hosting this university, the establishment of which will--I believe--  constitute a positive step in enhancing international understanding and goodwill.
4.9 Mr President, increased international co-operation of this kind is urgently needed in view  of the staggering problems faced by the world today, as brought out so clearly in the First Part  of the Draft Medium-Term Plan. In mobilizing our Organization's efforts and resources to meet  the challenges ahead, I am pleased that strategies have been proposed in the Draft Plan for  closer collaboration between Unesco and international aud regional organizations. The advan-  tages of such collaboration are self-evident, helping as it will to further Unesco's objectives by  the most economical means. The international and regional organizations in question have been  established with functions in specific fields and have accumulated a wealth of experience and  expertise over the years, not to mention the facilities they have built up. Many of them, such  as the South-East Asian Ministers of Education Organization (SEAMEO), are engaged in activi-  ties within Unesco's spheres of concern; collaboration and co-operation between them and Unesco  would be mutually beneficial. 'n the field of educational research, SEAMEO has undertaken some  outstanding research in the development of the most econcmical and effective delivery systems,  especially at the primary level. The development of such systems would not only ensure optimum  utilization of the limited and scarce resources available to the developing countries in coping  with ever-increasing demands but would also go a long way to help solve the problems of  illiteracy.
4.10 Mr President, I have no doubt that the findings of research conducted by SEAMEO and  other international organizations would be applicable to developing countries in other regions of  the world. Moreover, Unesco could facilitate the transfer of this know-how with hardly any  expense to itself and yet to the greatest benefit of the countries concerned.
4.11 In view of the value of such co-operation, Unesco might usefully help to strengthen certain  existing international and regional organizations and their institutions in terms of manpower  planning and facilities, and thus enable them not only better to serve their own respective  regions but also to assist Unesco itself in the implementation of its programmes. Assistance of  this kind would obviously also be in furtherance of the Organization's objectives of developing  endogenous expertise and activities in its various fields of interest. Needless to say, this would  also result in economies for the Organization, as there already exists a readily accessible pool  of expertise which could be utilized on a limited and temporary basis.
4.12 Mr President, I have dwelt extensively on the subject of co-operation and collaboration  between Unesco and the international and regional organizations because I firmly believe that
198   it would result in tremendous benefits for all concerned, and I would like to urge that this matter  be given more prominent emphasis in the Draft Medium-Term Plan.
4.13 Turning now to Major Programme VIII, concerning 'Principles. methods and strategies  of action for development', I welcome the proposed activities. I would, however, like to comment  on two of them. On the proposed activity pertaining to the development process, I would urge  that the promotion of studies on different theories be toned down in favour of greater emphasis on  practical development undertakings; what to my mind our Organization lacks is not such theor-  etical studies but the translation of their results into action. Much thought has been given to this  concept of endogenous development, and greater efforts should now be made to direct the results  of research over the years towards the realization of actual endogenous development.
4.14 My second observation, Mr President, concerns the training of national personnel  required for development planning and evaluation. Here is an opportunity for Unesco to make a  real and concrete contribution in response to one of the most urgent needs of developing  countries. Such a programme would help alleviate the shortage of trained manpower faced by  the developing nations, and here I would urge the Organization to give greater prominence to  this activity, as compared with other programmes in Major Programme VIII.
4.15 The training of personnel in all Unesco's fields of competence should be stepped up, and  substantially more so in the field of education where--as ! see it--there is a definite shortage of  competent educational planners and administrators to cope with the ever-increasing demands  made on education and educational facilities. Education is a field that is undergoing rapid ex-  pansion in the developing nations. Not only have content and methodologies changed in character  to include new areas and means of learning, but facilities themselves have expanded tremen-  dously, and governments have been required to spend vast sums of money to cater for this  development. It is thus essential that such resources be put to optimum use as well as ef-  ficiently managed. This calls for specialized manpower, which requires training to undertake  this important task. In the provision of such training, our Organization should not only implement  specific programmes but should also collaborate with regional and international organizations in  the training of more Unesco-sponsored candidates.
4.16 Mr President, as we look at the programmes relating to the elimination of prejudice,  intolerance, racism and apartheid and those concerned with peace and international understanding,  the freedom of the peoples and human rights, it is indeed appalling to recall the carnage per-  petrated recently on the Palestinian people in Lebanon. The scale of those atrocities is a grim  reminder to the world that we are still far from our goals of peace and international understanding,  and the event itself bears witness to the prejudice and intolerance which still prevail in the world  today. Such affronts to humanity merit the strongest condemnation and underline the urgent need  for our Organization to step up activities to spread peace and international understanding among  the peoples of the world.
4.17 Mr President, the promotion of international understanding is closely linked with the  universalization of education. International goodwill cannot be nurtured in the desert of  illiteracy. Consequently, the success of the programmes proposed under Major Programme XIII  will no doubt depend in great measure on the rate of success in eradicating illiteracy, which is  constantly on the increase in absolute terms.
4.18 Access to education is among the basic human rights, and it is to be deeply regretted that  this right has not been respected in some countries. Here, we painfully recall that the Arab  communities in the Occupied Arab Territories are being deprived of this right to the education  of their choice. Such an act runs counter to the concept of the universalization of education as  propounded by our Organization, and we call upon Unesco and the international community to  demand the immediate cessation of this injustice.
4.19 So much, Mr President for my views and observations on Major Programmes I, VIII and  XIII of the Second Part of the Draft Medium-Term Plan.
4.20 Mr President, when our Organization was conceived some three and a half decades ago our  founding fathers envisioned the creation of a world free from injustice, intolerance, mistrust  and apprehensions, through the fostering of intellectual collaboration and co-operation. In  pursuit of this noble and indeed ageless quest, they were inspired and guided by the stirring  clause in the Preamble to our Constitution which states ' That since wars begin in the minds of  men, it is in the minds of men that the defences of peace must be constructed'. Written many
7   decades ago, this phrase has been a source of inspiration to all of us to strive harder in the  continual or ceaseless search for universal respect for justice, for the rule of law and for  human rights and fundamental freedoms. Although, at this moment in our history, we may not  appear to be anywhere near these goals, despite the many efforts made by various states, and  in different bodies, forums and meetings, it is must gratifying to witness, during this waek, the  earnestness of our desire to collaborate and to seek in the recesses of our minds that common  destiny for all mankind, and this irrespective of the fact that we come from varied cultural,  ideological and social backgrounds.
4.21 Tam truly convinced and confident, Mr President, that our deliberations during this fourth  extraordinary session of the General Conference will make a significant contribution to the fuller  realization of our Organization's goals and ideals. Thank you, Mr President.
5. The PRESIDENT:  I thank the head of the delegation of Malaysia. I give the floor to H. E. the Hon. M. L.  Wellington, Minister of Education, head of the delegation of New Zealand.
6.1 Mr WELLINGTON (New Zealand):  Mr President, may I join with previous speakers in congratulating Mr Ivo Margan on his  election to this important position at this particular conference. I should like to begin by  extending my congratulations to the Director-General and the members of his Secretariat for  the detailed work that has gone into the preparation of the Draft Medium-Term Plan. Although,  in the view of the Government of New Zealand, the Draft Plan falls short in a number of im-  portant respects it does represent a further significant step forward in the Organization's  efforts to plan more effectively. In the interests of brevity, much of what I shall say will focus  on the reservations New Zealand has about the Plan in its present draft form. Consequently I  may do less than justice to many of ihe useful initiatives it contains. In a short intervention,  however, it is inevitable that that should be so. The New Zealand Government believes that it  is in the best interests of the Organization that we should all speak our minds clearly and in a  spirit of co-operation and commitment to the important objectives of Unesco's Constitution.
6.2 It is disappointing that the Secretariat has been unable to comply with the requirement of
21 C/Resolution 100 regarding the length and presentation of the Draft Plan. It is not,  Mr President, a concise document. And it is not 'so written and presented that all those who  participate or take an interest in Unesco's activities can readily understand it'. Admittedly, the  General Conference set the Secretariat a very tall order. But I cannot escape the conclusion that  the text has been written by specialists for specialists, not for people who, like so many of us,  wish to take an interest in Unesco's activities and want to be told, in plain language, what the  Draft Plan proposes for the next six years. The language of the Draft and the abstract nature of  many of its ideas are thus serious stumbling-blocks. This has made it difficult for my Govern-  ment and its advisers to identify the major programmes it cau most readily support. Our  endorsement of the Plan must, therefore, at this stage be less th n comprehensive.
6.3 There is, however, much in Major Programmes II, IV and V on education, III and VII on  communication and information, VI and IX on science and in Major Programme X on ‘The human  environment and terrestrial and marine resources', with which we very much agree. In the  main these programmes are in accord with the objectives of the Organization: they are the result  of practical experience in the field and, as programmes, they are 'action-oriented, with a view  to development and in favour of the developing countries in particular’.
6.4 On the major programme relating to 'Communication in the service of man', I fully  appreciate the sensitive nature of statements on the role of the media. New Zealand has noted  the changes proposed in document 4 XC/4 Annex II. I think the Organization is well advised not  to depart from the phraseology of the 1978 Media Declaration in its analyses and programme  statements. That declaration, Mr President, was the outcome of the closest scrutiny and a  fragile consensus. I! commend the revised wording of the title of Programme II. 2. This brings  it into line with the phraseology of the Media Declaration, thereby reflecting our concern that  activities achieve 'a free flow and a wider and better balanced dissemination of information’. I  commend particularly those parts of the major programme that will assist developing countries  in building up media structures.
6.5 I have one reservation relating to the major programme on science. I believe that the third  subprogramme cf Prograrame VI. 3 on energy requires further study to ensure that there is no  duplication with programmes of other agencies and that the subprogramme when finally
200   developed is carried out with inter-agency co-operation as necessary. In our interventions in 7  Commission I, we will be more specific in drawing attention to the quite extensive overlap of  activities that already exists in the field of energy.
6.6 1 welcome Major Programme X on 'The human environment and terrestrial and marine  resources’ for two reasons. Firstly, its scope has been widened to include marine resources,  which will be of particular significance for the Asia and Pacific region. Secondly, the programme  itself has a larger significance in the work of the Organization. In its present form, itis a  result of a decade's work that has grown out of the Man and the Biosphere programme. It is a  demonstration that practical, multidisciplinary programmes can be developed, and it is a re-  minder that the process is one that takes time,
6.7 Indeed, the practicality and coherence of this major programme stands in marked contrast  to Major Programmes land VIII. These programmes have in my view not yet reached the point  where they are ready for the endorsement of the General Conference. Mr President, New  Zealand could support a modest programme which, if carried out during the next six years,  might well identify some specific world problems which were within Unesco's mandate and  which could provide a basis for the orientation of the third Medium-Term Plan. For the immedi-  ate future, however, we would prefer to see more effort put into the evaluation of Unesco's own  experience in the field and, if this could be done in co-operation with other United Nations  agencies, a basis might be established for a consideration of the principles, methods and stra-  tegies of action for development that would have a greater sense of realism than I find in Major  Programme VIII and, in particular, in Programme VIII. 1, ‘Study and planning of development’.  It is our view, furthermore, that the first subprogramme of Programme VIII. 3 (paragraph 8041)  on the 'Mobilization of financial resources’, is outside Unesco's mandate and should be omitted.  Such activities seem to me to come more appropriately within the scope of international agencies  primarily concerned with economic matters.
6.8 1 believe too that Major Programmes XII on 'The elimination of prejudice, intolerance,  racism and apartheid’ and XIII on 'Peace, international understanding, the freedom of the  peoples and human rights' require careful scrutiny. Their subjects are of course fundamental  to the aims of the United Nations. As a consequence they have been the subject of deep thought  and study by the world community. We need to ask ourselves whether the kinds of subprogrammes  proposed will not merely confirm what is already very well known; or even, when proposing  specific action, will not duplicate the activities of other organs of the United Nations system. The  subprogrammes should be more selectively formulated to pick out areas where new efforts and  attention will be productive. Full account should also be taken of the responsibilities already  placed and accepted elsewhere.
6.9 May I emphasize that New Zealand regards Unesco's commitment to uphold human rights  in the broader sense asa corner-stone of its very existence. Any trend to dilute its efforts or  even more the concept so firmly enshrined in the International Bill of Human Rights would  deprive Unesco of its basic conceptual framework. In a wider sense, Unesco's role in asserting  the freedom of man extends to human rights in every sphere of its competence; the right to  education, science, culture and communication, as well as the right to live in peace and freedom  from persecution. Seen in this light, Major Programmes XII and XIII are laudable in their  general aims but I question whether they justify, in their own right and as they now stand, a  larger investment of scarce resources, as is proposed.
6.10 Mr President, I want now to turn to Major Programme XV, 'Programme support’, and to  general policy considerations relating to resources and priorities. It is clear that, whatever  policies are adopted on the size and rate of growth of the budget, the Organization will never  have enough money to do everything that member countries would wish it to do. It is inevitable  that priorities will have to be decided and it is equally inevitable that that task will be difficult.
1 therefore congratulate the Director-General on the inclusion of the Appendix on resource  indications. That is a necessary reminder to us all that the financial cake, whatever its size,  will be finite and that if higher priority is to be given to some major programmes others may  have to be accorded a lower priority. There is, however, one tendency that we should guard  against. We should look very critically at proposals which, based on growth rates in the past,  extrapolate continuing growth into the next six year planning period. I am referring in par-  ticular to Programme XV. 7, 'Personnel', paragraph 15042. The New Zealand Government  welcomes the substantial changes made to the original text and in particular the new wording of  paragraphs 15041 to 15046 in 4 XC/4 Annex II. Staffing levels should be determined in the light  of the programme to be carried out and the resources to be made available. Staffing levels, the
201   kind of personnel to be employed, their location, and more clearly defined personnel policies  appropriate to the total scheme of Unesco's activities are all matters to be related to the  requirements of 22 C/5 and subsequent biennia. My reference to locations is of course a  reference to the issue of decentralization. The New Zealand Government sees decentralization  as an absolute imperative for Unesco's future programme policy, and I congratulate the Director-  General on the active steps he is taking to give effect to this policy. I welcome paragraph 15044,  which speaks of increasing the staff strength of units in the field, and the suggestion that this  could be effected by transferring certain staff members and activities from Headquarters. We  support this proposal having regard to the much smaller numbers of staff working in the Field  compared with those employed at Headquarters. But I remain unconvinced, given the information  so far available, that Unesco will need to increase staff both at Headquarters and in the Fieid at  the same time. A policy of decentralization should lead to a reduction of Headquarters staff,  especially in the operational field, nct to an increase.
6.11 These remarks on personnel have a direct bearing on future needs in respect of Head-  quarter premises, as outlined in paragraph 15051, which projects future space requirements on  the assumption of a staff increase. The case has not been established, and we would need a lot  of convincing that Unesco's resources would not be better employed in other directions. New  Zealand has been concerned for a long time that Unesco has been attempting too much within its  limited budgetary resources, trying to be all things to all men. We believe that it is better to  concentrate on fewer programmes and do them well, rather than spread resources too thinly.  This makes the definition of priorities a matter of utmost importance.
6.12 The New Zealand Government's priorities are as follows: firstly, we support practical,  action-oriented programmes and therefore give high priority to Major Programmes II, III, IV,  V, VI, VII, IX and X; secondly, we will oppose any proposals for a reduction, in percentage  terms, of the priority to be given to Major Programmes VI and IX; thirdly, we would want the  Organization to give a high priority to evaluation studies of all its programmes during the Medium-  Term Plan; fourthly, we do not consider Major Programmes I, III, XII and XIII to have reached  the stage where the Organization should commit large amounts of resources to them during the  Medium-Term Plan,
6.13 Mr President, New Zealand continues to take the position that, conceptually, the end of  the first Medium-Term Plan should be seen as the terminal date for all programmes carried out  under the first Medium-Term Plan, and that past programmes should be included in the new  Medium-"Term Plan only if their continuation can be justified as contributing towards the im-  plementation of the new Plan. It is clear, however, that this extraordinary session of the General  Conference does not have the kind of information that would enable it to evaluate Lhe effeciiveness  of existing programmes. I know that there are conceptual as well as organizational and practical  difficulties in the way of effective evaluation of Unesco's operations. New Zealand is aware of  the work that the Director-General is doing to include evaluation activities as an integral part of  the process of planning, programming and budgeting. These activities, however, should be  widened in concept to include external evaluation where appropriate. I therefore welcome the  commitment in paragraph 46 of the Foreword to the Draft Plan. I consider the development of  these activities as they relate to programme execution to be a matter of high priority within the  framework of the Medium-Term Plan.
6.14 Finally, Mr President, I wish to make a few comments on budgetary matters. The New  Zealand Government's attitude has consistently been one of support for modest budgetary growth  rates provided these are based on sound programme proposals put forward at General Con-  ference sessions. This position has not changed, except for one important proviso: New Zealand  would not be able to support a budgetary increase for the 1984/85 biennium in view of the inter-  national economic recession which shows no signs of abating in the short term. New Zealand  does not, however, exclude the possibility of modest budgetary growth for the subsequent two  biennia, provided the economic situation returns to some kind of normality. Implicit in this  position is the acknowledgement that some degree of growth over the medium term is desirable  to add impetus to the work of the Organization. We do not, however, see the consideration and  adoption of the Medium-Term Plan as the right time or place to determine future growth rates.  A proper costing of the budget can take place only after the cost of each individual component of  the programme has been worked out and placed in the framework of a total budget, and this of  course can only happen at a General Conference when the C/5 document is being considered. Our  position, then, is that it is premature, in the context of discussion of this planning document, to  determine a growth rate. The proper course is to have that debate at the twenty-second session  of the Unesco General Conference next year.
6.15 In conclusion, Mr President, may | again acknowledge the diligence with which the 7  Director-General has implemented the various resolutions that have provided the policy guide-  lines for the preparation of the Medium-Term Plan. New Zealand has expressed some reser-  vations on a number of matters, but this, I believe, is no more than is to be expected in a debate  ranging over such a wide canvas. However, divergence of views does not in any way diminish our  respect for the work which the Director-General has carried out on behalf of the Organization. (8.1) Mr YORDANOV (Bulgaria) (Translation from the Russian):  Mr President, Mr Director-General, dear colleagues, ladies and gentlemen, I should like most  heartily to congratulate Mr Ivo Margan on his election as President of our Conference.
(8.2) The fourth extraordinary session of the General Conference of Unesco is called upon to discuss  and adopt an important document--the Second Medium-Term Plan of the Organization's activities up to the  end of the 1980s (1984-1989). Hence the delegation of the People's Republic of Bulgaria, mindful as it is of  the great importance of this representative forum, earnestly desires to assist its constructive work, the  aim being to adopt decisions and a Plan which will raise Unesco's activities to new heights.
(8.3) This session of the General Conference is taking place at a crucial juncture for mankind. A grave  threat hangs over the world and the whole of civilization. Militarist and aggressive forces are intent on  aggravating international tension and preparing for a nuclear war. They are generating growing alarm and  are systematically directing increased efforts at reviving not only the spirit but also the practice of the  "Cold War', rejected by time and by the different nations. Reactionary imperialist circles are constantly  endeavouring to exert pressure on the socialist countries, deprive the newly liberated peoples of their  historic gains and oppose the world's progressive and peace-loving forces. We must not remain indiffer-  ent to such attempts. There is today no more pressing and significant issue claiming man's attention than  the concern to strengthen peace on earth. The lessons of the past are too harsh to fade from memory and  the future is too dear to us for anybody to behave towards it irresponsibly.
(8.4) Our Government shares a position of principle, the essence of which is that it is the duty of each  state to protect the peaceful present and future of its people and to assist the strengthening of security in  the world, As has been stated by the Chairman of the State Council of the People's Republic of Bulgaria,  Todor Zhivkov, the world has no reasonable alternative when it comes to the existence of man and the  preservation of life on our planet. This is the chief issue with which the fate of civilization and the life of  man as the pinnacle of nature are bound up.
(8.5) The preservation of world peace and the promotion of détente are in keeping with the vital interests  of all countries and peoples. This is the dictate of our times. A substantial contribution to this human-  istic cause is made by the socialist states and progressive forces the world over, who are resolutely  striving for a halt to the arms race, for détente, for development and for social progress. Noting the  exceptional importance of the decision by the Soviet Union not to be the first to use nuclear weapons, the  Government of Bulraria considers that this noble example of attachment to peace should be followed by  other nuclear weapon states.
(8.6) In the name of justice and truth, Unesco must be given its due for its substantial contribution to the  preservation of peace. In displaying realism and a great sense of responsibility for progress throughout  the world, our Organization is helping to amplify the constructive trends and phenomena in our complex  and contradictory world.
(8.7) Mr President, we all owe a debt of gratitude to the Director-General and the Secretariat of Unesco  for the enormous amount of productive labour that they have put into the preparation of this session of the  General Conference. We have before us the Draft Medium-Term Plan, which is entirely in keeping with  the noble principles and purposes of the Organization as set forth in its Constitution. This document, which  is a new expression of the collective efforts of Member States, helps to confirm Unesco as a factor for  peace, mutual understanding and spiritual co-operation among peoples. The value of the Draft lies in the  fact that the planning and forecasting activities of the Organization have been put on a serious scientific  and methodological basis.
(8.8) We must welcome the efforts directed towards exact definition and in-depth analysis of the processes,  phenomena and trends operating in education, science and culture, and in other fields of Unesco's  competence.
(8.9) Our delegation considers that a great merit of the Draft Plan and the analysis carried out by the  Director-General is the fact that the strengthening of peace, the halting of the arms race and the averting  of a nuclear catastrophe are made central world issues.
(8.10) Perfectly justified and convincing arguments have been adduced to show up the irresponsible  philosophy of 'local' nuclear conflicts, and a note of warning has been sounded regarding the real danger  of using chemical and bacteriological weapons. What reasonable man can in fact dispute the truth that the  danger of a nuclear war is a matter of the utmost concern for nations?
(8.11) The Bulgarian delegation attaches great importance to Major Programme XIII, which, in our  opinion, could be expanded. What I have in mind is the need to voice an attitude regarding the widespread  anti-war movement which has developed in the world as a convincing expression of the desire of peoples  for peace and international security. When discussing problems of the freedom of peoples and human  rights, we cannot fail to condemn the misdeeds of the Israeli aggressors and we cannot fail to raise our  voice in defence of the legitimate rights of the Arab people of Palestine to the establishment of a state of  their own.
(8.12) We stigmatize the policy of apartheid, racism and genocide. Although reference is made to these  matters in Major Programme XII, we consider that still more resolute support should be given to the  struggle against the vestiges of colonialism and against racism and apartheid.
(8.13) It is rightly emphasized under Major Programme III that the mass media should be used in the  interests of peace, friendship and mutual understanding among peoples. At the same time, it is extremely  important that they should also serve the cause of the national originality and culture of the developing  countries. A prime task of our Organization is to render the necessary assistance to those nations so that  they can establish their own effective mass information and communication media. It is logical in this  connection to ask one further question: is it not time to put an end to the calculated slogan-wielding around  the so-called 'freedom of the press’ and 'free flow of information’, which are often used as a cover for  imperialistic cultural expansion vis-à-vis the developing countries.
(8.14) Our delegation is at one with Unesco's efforts to overcome illiteracy and inequality in the edu-  cational and cultural level of the population. It is quite natural that emphasis should be placed in the  relevant programme on the importance of education for the development of a humanism which combines  the ethical values handed down from the past with the sophisticated requirements and criteria of these  fast-moving modern times. Such a humanism would be impossible if education did not help to bring up  the young in a spirit of peace, mutual understanding and co-operation, and of respect for the rights and  cultural achievements of other peoples.
(8.15) It is stated in the Draft Plan that the progress of society is governed both by socio-economic and  by cultural factors. These factors are in a process of continuous interaction, and the role of culture in  modern society is constantly increasing. À major success in the Organization's work was the Second  World Conference on Cultural Policies, held in Mexico City. The Declaration and documents of that  Conference contain important ideas which are reflected in the Draft Medium-Term Plan. Life is full  of examples proving that education, science and culture are decisive factors in the economic and social  development of society, and socio-economic progress is a firm basis and the best pre-condition for its  full spiritual development. Clear confirmation of this is provided by present-day life in socialist  Bulgaria. Within a short span of history and on the basis of a constant improvement in the material and  cultural living standards of the Bulgarian people, the community now enjoys the broad prospect of and  high potential for uninterrupted cultural progress. The achievements of our culture, science and edu-
206   cation are the property of the entire nation. Our fruitful co-operation with the socialist countries, with  the Balkan nations and with other Member States of Unesco provides genuine pre-conditions for profitable  cultural exchange and for enriching the spiritual world of our contemporaries through acquaintance with  the achievements of progressive world culture.
(8.16) Mr President, the present is of value only when it enfolds the hope of a better future and when it  creates the conditions and establishes the prerequisites for constant progress. It would indeed be a inark  of levity and indifference if we were to minimize our responsibility for the fate of the young.
(8.17) It is heartening to note that in recent years Unesco has been giving a great deal of attention to the  problems of young people. We remember the important initiatives taken by the Organization on the  occasion of the International Year of the Child, together with a number of its undertakings concerned with  youth.
(8.18) With deep gratitude I should like to emphasize that as a result of this correct approach to the  future and its culture, the Director-General of Unesco, Mr Amadou-Mahtar M'Bow, and the Chairman of  the State Council of the People's Republic of Bulgaria, Todor Zhivkov, accorded their high patronage to  the International 'Banner of Peace' Assembly. The vigour of its motto 'Unity, Creativity and Beauty' was  attested to in a convincing way in August this year when young people from more than a hundred countries  of the world gathered in Sofia. It was a moving meeting of young talents and pure hearts, thirsting to live,  learn and create beneath a clear and peaceful sky.
(8.19) The 'Banner of Peace' Assembly made a substantial contribution to the development of the creative  abilities of children and young people. That was its chief purpose and its great international value. A  stimulus in this respect was also provided by Recommendation No. 170 adopted by the Second World Con-  ference on Cultural Policies, held in Mexico City.
(8.20) The founder and inspirer of this wonderful venture was that eminent participant in the building of  modern socialist Bulgaria, that tireless champion of peace, friendship and cultural co-operation among  peoples, Lyudmila Zhivkova. We are deeply grateful to Unesco for the attention and support it is giving  to the recently established Lyudmila Zhivkova International Fund, the purpose of which is to promote the  further development of this noble movement. The young participants will be meeting once more for their  Third Assembly in 1985, which has been proclaimed International Youth Year by the United Nations.
(8.21) In view of the topicality and social significance of this range of questions, various aspects of which  compel the attention of the entire world, we consider that it would be advisable to make actual planning  provision in Unesco in the years ahead for an even broader range of undertakings concerned with youth.  This will help to enhance the prestige of the Organization and make its work more effective.
(8.22) Mr President, I should like, in summing up my observations, to state that the delegation of the  People's Republic of Bulgaria approves Unesco's Second Draft Medium-Term Plan. We are convinced that.  this document, with a number of additional ideas and proposals, will assist the noble efforts of Unesco to  develop modern civilization and strengthen peace and security the world over.
(8.23) May the hopes of peoples for a world without wars and a world of justice and common human  progress triumph. Thank you.
9. The PRESIDENT:  I thank the head of the delegation of Bulgaria. I give the floor to Mr Mumtaz Ali Kazi,  Adviser to the President of Pakistan for Science and Technology, head of the delegation of  Pakistan.
10.1 Mr KAZI (Pakistan):  Mr President, Mr Director-General, Your Excellencies, distinguished delegates, ladies  and gentlemen, my delegation considers it a privilege to have been invited to participate in this  fourth extraordinary session of the General Conference, and we sincerely hope that the General  Conference will attain the objective for which it has been convened.
10.2 I wish to express my great satisfaction at seeing Mr Margan presiding again over the  deliberations of this session. We greatly admire the skill and distinction with which he guided  the work of the General Conference at its twenty-first session in Belgrade.
10.3 May I also take this opportunity to express my Government's sincere appreciation of the  Director-General's abiding commitment to the ideals of the United Nations and to Unesco's  Constitution. The imaginative way in which he has steered the Organization and the untiring  efforts that he has made to attain its objectives have won him the well-deserved acclamation and  approbation of the entire world community.
10.4 I sincerely hope that the very moving and forthright statement made by the President of  the General Conference at the opening session as well as the Director-General's eloquent  exposé of the salient features of the Draft Medium-Term Plan will inspire Member States par-
7   ticipating in the session to seek a consensus and agreement on the proposals containz2d therein.  Such a consensus, I am sure, would demonstrate the identity of purpose and the spirit of co-  operation and goodwill that exist amongst the Member States which subscribe to the Constitution.
10.5 The First Part of the Draft Plan which deals with the analysis of world problems is a  faithful and forthright survey of the existing situation in the world. A great deal of ground has  been covered. The areas of conflict, confrontation and concern that humanity as a whole is  facing today, at this stage of its history, have been clearly identified. A searching analysis has  been made of the crucial problems and the major issues that need our consideration. The con-  tribution that Unesco can make in accordance with the provisions of its Constitution and in its  fields of competence have also been indicated in clear and unequivocal terms. What is required  now is a collective will, a deep sense of commitment and willing co-operation in implementing  the programmes that have been proposed in the Draft Plan.
10.6 Mr President, it is a truism that we are today passing through the most difficult period in  human history. There is tension all over the globe. There is conflict and strife everywhere.  There is continuing exploitation of the poor by the rich and the hegemony of the powerful over  the oppressed. The world is not only polarized but sharply divided; one part of mankind is  blessed with undreamt-of affluence and a profusion of material goods, whereas the bulk of  people in the developing world faces absolute poverty and unmitigated hardship. In addition, they  are facing an unprecedented threat to their value systems, cultural identity and economic  viability from the technologically dominant nations. Internal inflation, rising prices, unemploy-«  ment and underemployment have resulted in widespread malnutrition and misery for these already  oppressed peoples. In that context, it may further be mentioned that, on account of the increasing  economic domination of the affluent and industrialized world, all international arrangements,  systems and institutions are designed to meet the needs of that world. Consequently, there is a  disproportionate global burden of readjustment, a lack of international agreement on commodi-  ties, an inadequate transfer of resources and technology and a high degree of instability in the  exchange rates of major currencies, all of which operates to the gross disadvantage of the  developing countries.
10.7 Historically, Mr President, the most important factor in creating and perpetuating  conflicts and a sense of deprivation among peoples has been disregarded for equity and justice  in regulating the affairs of and relations between nations and groups. Although during the past  three decades the developing countries have emancipated themselves from an oppressive and  unjust colonial political order, an iniquitous and unjust international economic order, which is  becoming more and more oppressive with the increasing economic disparities between the North  and South and the extension of the spheres of influence of economic domination, still persists.  The continued stranglehold on the economy and resources of the less developed countries has  perpetuated a state of impoverishment and underdevelopment. The people of the developing  countries, in spite of political liberation, have thus been unable to achieve the much cherished  economic emancipation which is essential to their honourable existence and survival. With such  an iniquitous distribution of available resources and much wasteful use of scarce resources,  unrest and disturbances of the peace are but a natural consequence.
10.8 Another complicating factor that we observe today is the acceleration and intensification  of the power struggle and the arms race. The nuclear and conventional arms race continues  inexorably, adding more and newer weapons to existing stockpiles. The quest for new sophis-  ticated military technologies is adding to the imbalance in security. The interests of the develop-  ing countries have been gravely affected by this ever-increasing and ever-escalating arms race.  Today a sum of nearly 600 billion dollars a year is being spent in the world on armaments,  which works out at nearly two billion dollars a day on nuclear and other weapons of mass de-  struction. If this money were available to the poor countries for development, the world would  be a happier and a healthier place to live in.
10.9 Integral to peace and security in the world is a commitment to peaceful coexistence and  the peaceful settlement of disputes. If succeeding generations are to be spared the scourge of  war and the threat of perpetual confrontation, then the prevailing atmosphere of mistrust and  incipient conflict must be ended and the existing nuclear arsenals must be eliminated com-  pletely. It is only then that we will be able to breathe freely in an atmosphere of peace and  tranquillity.
10.10 Mr President, Pakistan is a developing country. It is passionately devoted to the develop-  ment of its resources and the advancement and welfare of its people. It believes in the acqui-
208   sition and use of modern technology--but it does not subscribe to nuclear proliferation or the use  of nuclear weapons. In pursuance of Pakistan's abiding policy to keep its region free from the  menace of nuclear proliferation, it has already moved two resolutions: the first on the establish  ment of a nuclear-free zone in South Asia and the second on the conclusion of an international  convention to protect non-nuclear-weapon states against the use or threat of use of nuclear  weapons. Pakistan has proved its bona fide intentions to bring about peaceful coexistence and  mutual trust for all on all occasions.
10.11 It seems paradoxical that when we meet at international forums we talk of social justice,  equality, human rights and political liberation, and yet we tolerate the most devastating viol-  ation of these values. Only very recently there was a horrible massacre of innocent and unarmed  persons, including women and children, in Sabra and Chatila. Pakistan deplores the systematic  attempt which is being made by Israel to annex the Palestinian territories and to change their  cultural, historic and demographic character. History knows no instance of such planned and  malicious destruction of an entire culture. This, Mr President, is a sad and glaring example  of a case in which the denial of peoples' rights is indistinguishable from the denial of human  rights.
10.12 In our humble opinion, the first step to take to solve the world problems described in the  Draft Medium-Term Plan is to revive moral and ethical values, to restore human rights, to  share intellectual and material resources between peoples, to end exploitation and to disarm.  For what is cutting across our much cherished aspirations to and ideals of peace in the world  today is the ethical anarchy and moral relativism prevalent in modern societies and the  arrogance and power passion that we find in them. If these could be contained and controlled,  mother earth would be a better place to live in.
10.13 Mr President, in conformity with our ideology and faith, Pakistan is fully committed to  the preservation and restoration of human rights. We have openly and fervently supported the  legitimate rights of individuals and peoples all over the world, and have expressed this support  in many regional and international forums. We have demonstrated our commitment in deed as  well. We are currently looking after nearly 3 million refugees from Afghanistan purely on  humanitarian grounds and in the spirit of compassion enjoined on us by Islam. In spite of our  meagre resources, we have welcomed these refugees in our midst, and have opened our homes  to them so that they can live in peace. It is not easy for a poor country like ours to take in such  a mass of people, but we have done it in our conviction that we must uphold human rights and  the rights of people to exist in dignity and freedom in this difficult world.
10.14 We believe that human rights and peoples' rights are complementary to each other. All  peoples of the world, whether white or black, yellow or brown, are human beings and should be  treated as such, individually as well as collectively. We endorse the explanation and clarifi-  cation given by the Director-General in this connection in the additional texts that have been  circulated.
10.15 Pakistan has always opposed and raised its voice loudly and clearly against apartheid,  racism, colonialism, prejudice, intolerance and political domination, economic exploitation and  the social and cultural domination of nations over other nations. We have always condemned acts  of aggression, violence and persecution wherever and whenever they have been committed.
10.16 As a developing country, Pakistan is beset with many problems. We have a large popu-  lation and a wide cultural base. We are trying to strengthen our educational system, our scien-  tific and technological potential and the dissemination possibilities of our information system.  We are about to launch our sixth development plan. The main strategy of this plan is to combine  a strong production base with a strong social conscience. We need Unesco's co-operation and  guidance in the fields of its competence. Unesco, along with many friendly states, is already  assisting us in restoring our cultural sites and monuments. Moenjodaro is one such important  project which has attracted considerable attention in the world community as part of the common  heritage of mankind. We are most grateful for the assistance we are receiving from Unesco and  some of its Member States.
10.17 Mr President, I shall now offer some comments on the text and subject-matter of the  Draft Plan in the different sectors. First of all, there was a time when social and economic  progress was not directly linked with the widespread provision of education to the masses. Edu-  cation was the privilege of the rich, the exalted and the élite, and its expansion was due to the  foresight of the chosen few and not the result of the system. Today, as a result of two great
7   revolutions, the industrial and the social revolution, the situation has completely changed. Mass  education has become a major determining factor for national, social and economic growth. It is  an investment for the future and a key input for the development process of any nation. It is very  sad and disconcerting indeed that this right to education has been denied to a large part of  humanity. It is also sad that we have not been able to motivate the conscience of the world or  seek the commitment of richer nations to help the less developed countries achieve universal  literacy and mass education for their teeming millions of illiterates. Last year the United  Nations General Assembly gave a clarion call to take steps to eliminate illiteracy {rom the world.  We are happy that the need for literacy has been greatly emphasized again in the Draft Medium-  Term Plan.
10.18 Mr President, a global approach to culture was endorsed by the General Conference at  its twentieth session. A definition of culture was also evolved. This gave culture a wide conno-  tation and meaning, and includes the entire value system covering a very large area of human  life and activity. This means that the social, moral and spiritual components of culture cannot be  ignored. Seen in this perspective, the emphasis on culture is not adequate in the Draft Plan.  Perhaps Unesco's concept of culture is mainly concentrated on cultural sites and museums. The  relics of all culiures are, of course, the common heritage of mankind whether rich or poor,  strong or weak. If they are allowed to wither or perish it will make all of us poorer. The sooner  we take these things up the better it will be in terms of time, energy and cost. If more time is  lost, further deterioration will occur making preservation efforts much more difficult and  expensive.
10.19 However, while we are trying to protect our cultural heritage on the one hand, we are  losing a great deal on the other. The influence of modernity and modernism is continuously  eroding and changing our life-styles, moral and ethical values and cultural commitments. Today  a new trend is being spread, which finds expression in permissiveness, defiance of authority and  disrespect for tradition and heritage. Morality and religion have no place in this new cultural  complex. We cannot turn a blind eye to this erosion of culture. Monuments, sites and relics are  no doubt very important, but humankind and its future is no less important. This must be saved  and protected. The non-physical cultural heritage is as important as the material heritage of  mankind.
10.20 It is a paradox of the present age that much of our anxiety, as indeed much of our hope,  is centred on science and technology. The phenomenal discoveries made in science and the  spectacular advances in technology have placed terrifying powers in the hands of man which,  exercised unwisely, could bring about his self-destruction. At the same time, only science and  technology can ensure deliverance from the poverty, hunger, disease and deprivation that  afflict most of mankind. The future hinges critically on the use of this great power placed by  science at the disposal of mankind.
10.21 A major factor in the misuse of modern science and technology for destructive and  exploitative ends is its supposedly value-neutral framework. The renaissance of science and  learning in Europe was accompanied by a separation of the religious and the secular, due to the  peculiar conditions then prevailing. Knowledge has therefore increasingly come to perform a  utilitarian function, whose ends are determined largely by the prevalent and dominant distribution  of societal power. Knowledge and science have become a means towards the continued pursuit of  whatever aims are deemed appropriate by those with the greatest influence, prestige and power.  They are a major tool for social manipulation and the service of profit. Modern science,  mistakenly based on the separation of the secular from the religious, has cut man off from his  moral moorings. The world needs to rethink the assumptions that led to the creation of this  value-neutral framework.
10.22 The basic problem which the developing countries, including Pakistan, face today, is the  rapid development of their capability to use science and technology for a comprehensive socio-  economic transformation. What they require is peace and assured security for uninterrupted,  indigenous and self-reliant development. Emerging nations have found it highly difficult to adopt  an imitative style based on imported technology that has been evolved in an entirely alien socio-  cultural environment. They must evolve indigenous systems compatible with their environment  and resources. They do, however, need the wholehearted assistance and support of the devel-  oped world to build sound science and technology systems. If this were to be given, sincerely,  without bias and with a sense of urgency and an understanding of the needs and requirements of  the developing nations, present-day feelings of frustration and discontent would soon be dispelled,  and mankind would proceed to build a better future and an era of peace and goodwill would dawn  and free mankind from the fear of mass destruction.
10.23 Every society has its own pattern of life, evolved through the interaction of a number of  parameters of complex and varied nature such as religious, ethnic, economic, ecologic and the  like. Society is like an organism. As the systems evolve, compatible and incompatible factors  interact and tend to reach an equilibrium. Thus the reaction of society to changing conditions is  interpretable only in terms of its own norms in a special and temporal relationship. It is there-  fore necessary to make a critical examination of various components to characterize certain  trends and the magnitude and nature of the inhibiting influences at work, so that a new process  of life in harmony with the environment can be evolved.
10.24 We are happy that great emphasis has been placed on the social sciences in the Draft  Medium-Term Plan. The social sciences is a very important field covering a very large area of  human endeavour. The social sciences, as opposed to the natural sciences, are essentially man-  made sciences. They are the result of the accumulated and varied experience of human societies  and human cultures. There are many societies in the world with different cultures and traditions.  We must consequently attempt to study the norms of all of them and should not restrict our  studies to the development of the social sciences in the twentieth century.
10.25 Another important question on which Unesco should lay major emphasis is that of  strengthening the marine science capabilities of the developing countries to enable them to  respond to emerging needs resulting from the agreement reached by governments on the Con-  vention on the Law of the Sea. The new ocean regime poses challenges to these countries in the  exercise of their rights to explore and exploit resources for the achievement of national goals  in marine affairs.
10.26 We are happy to note that a full chapter has been devoted to the position and status of  women in society. An excellent analysis and a forthright appraisal of the problems and diffi-  culties of women in different parts of the world has been clearly made. The need for equality,  equal opportunities and consideration for the fair sex has been emphasized. A positive strategy  of action has been suggested in paragraphs 14031 to 14041.
10.27 It has been argued that Unesco has expanded its activities beyond the requirements of its  Constitution and outside its field of competence. This is not correct in our opinion. Unesco covers  a large area of human activity and endeavour. It deals with the social, cultural, educational and  developmental aspects of human societies, and it therefore has to touch many borderline fields  and activities. Unesco has done well to live up to its responsibilities.
10.28 While it is true that Unesco cannot answer all the questions or solve all the problems of  the world, it can do a great deal to help bring them under control. It can provide an effective  forum in which intellectual exercises and in-depth studies can be profitably conducted to under-  stand the nature of these problems and work out possible solutions. In fact Unesco can serve as  a think tank in which a continuous process of reflection can be generated to guide and steer the  world conscience more steadily towards a new international order which will guarantee peaceful  and respectful coexistence to all humankind.
10.29 Finally, Mr President, we fully support both the first and second parts of the Draft Plan,  the analysis of world problems, the reflection on them, the thirteen major programmes, the  lines of emphasis on the status of women and programme support. We once again wholeheartedly  commend the excellent work done by the Secretariat under the able guidance and direction of the  Director-General. I hope and pray that the Plan will succeed and meet the expectations and  aspirations of Member States. We would be happy to support any draft decision that is being  contemplated as a consequence and outcome of this rich debate. Thank you Mr President.

(12.1) Mr NASIR (Palestine Liberation Organization) (Translation from the Arabic):  Mr President, Mr Chairman of the Executive Board, Mr Director-General, distinguished delegates,  ladies and gentlemen, it gives me great pleasure to join with previous speakers in congratulating  Mr Margan on his election as President of this important conference. I am fully confident that he will  lead the Conference, by his wisdom, to the achievement of the objectives for which it has been convened.
(12.2) I should also like to offer the Director-General my thanks and appreciation for the work he has  done in preparing this Medium-Term Plan which we are called upon to discuss. [t contains a serious,  profound analysis of the major problems affecting today's world, starting out from the needs and diffi-  culties of diffei ent states and going on to suggest appropriate solutions, look for means and formulate  programmes. The analysis by the Director-General which is placed at the beginning of the document, ard  which is obviously the product of much reflection and study, shows that work undertaken under Unesco's  auspices cannot be brought to a successful conclusion unless real-life situations are taken into account at  all political, economic and cultural levels, and unless all these factors are reflected in Unesco's action,  so that the Organization will be in a position to keep abreast of this indefatigable movement, this changing
213   rhythm. The international situation inevitably impinges on the work of Unesco, since Unesco is respon-  gible for the most important task of all, namely dealing with issues of education and culture and their  development with a view to mutual understanding among peoples and implanting peace in the hearts and  minds of mankind--a difficult and thorny task indeed.
(12.3) As you are aware, Mr President, we would like to see greater concentration in the plan and the  programme on the particular problems faced by liberation movements throughout the world, as these  problems are among the most serious of all, and those that are most threatening to world peace and  security.
(12.4) The holding of this Conference this year, Mr President, coincides with a massive aggravation of  the problems confronting the Palestinian people, who are still waiting and hoping for the realization of  their national aspirations and the restoration of their legitimate and inalienable rights, which have been  confirmed by all international institutions and organizations. It is important for the international com-  munity never to lose sight of the injustice and aggression to which that people is being subjected, nor of  the particular difficulties confronting it in its efforts to irnplement its educational and cultural plans. I  mention these matters, not by way of supplication nor to arouse emotion, but merely as an honest appeal  to the international community to participate in and contribute to the effort to devise appropriate solutions  with a view to changing the abnormal situation of the Palestinians.
(12.5) This year, in Lebanon, the Palestinian people have been subjected to a concentrated extermination  process, because of the Israeli assault on Lebanon. The result has been the killing and expulsion of  thousands of our own people and of our fellow people, the Lebanese, while thousands more--including  workers, students and teachers--have been herded into concentration camps, and nothing has been heard  of them since their internment some months ago. Hideous butchery has been perpetrated on innocent  children, women and old people in the camps, especially the camps of Sabra and Chatila, which were the  scene of a tragedy which shocked the conscience of the world. Israel must bear full responsibility for  these crimes. The Israeli assault on Lebanon also caused the destruction of the various educational and  cultural establishments which were legally functioning in Lebanon within the context of I.ebanese law and  institutions. The Israeli assault was expressly aimed at these establishments; even children's schools  were destroyed, such as the 'Is‘äd al-tufila (children's happiness) school, the 'Bayt al-sumud  (steadfastness house)’ and the 'Al-Hanän (compassion)' school, all of which were educational establishments  for the children of those who had met martyrs’ deaths. They were places of gecurity for the children,  their only refuge, where they were assured of care and were given hope of a brighter future; and they were  not spared from the deliberate destruction of the institutions providing socidl, economic and health  services, including the hospitals of the Palestinian Red Crescent, which is the organization responsible  for all matters concerning the health of the Palestinian people in Lebanon. With respect to the Palestinian  Research Ceutre, which is an academic, cultural and humanitarian institution specializing in studies,  research and documentation, it was spared from destruction--deliberately spared, but then it was raided  and its entire collection of documents, which constituted a priceless cultural heritage, was confiscated.
(12.6) The Executive Board of Unesco, at its 115th session, adopted a decision condemning this cultural  piracy and calling for the return of all the stolen documents to the institutions that own them. We should  like, at this time, to emphasize the importance of giving effect to that decision.
(12.7) The operations of destruction also included the Palestinian camps and the educational institutions  of UNRWA: everything that that agency had built over a period of thirty years was destroyed by the Israeli  assault in three months. This is a most distressing situation, especially since the school year has now  begun and many schools in Lebanon are still closed.
(12.8) Mr President, I have spoken at considerable length about what has happened to the Palestinian  people outside their country, specifically, in Lebanon. As to the occupied territory of Palestine itself, I  shall not review before you today Israel's continuing practices with respect to education and culture, such  as closing universities, mass arrests, refusing to permit the entry of school books, even those approved  by Unesco, and prohibiting their circulation, and levying heavy taxes on educational materials. All these  practices have been dealt with in earlier reports submitted to your esteemed Organization, and the  necessary resolutions adopted in connection with them. At this Conference I should like to r ention two  new matters which are worthy of attention. The first is Military Order No. 854, which wa. issued by the  occupation authorities in July 1980, This order restricts academic freedoms in the universities and  impedes their functioning, and it prevents students in the Gaza Strip and Jerusalem from attending  Palestinian universities without previous permission from the military authorities. Unesco condemned  this arbitrary order at its Belgrade Conference in 1980. calling on Israucl to cancel it immediately, but  Isr.el refused to comply. Two years after the adoption of Unesco's resolution, at the beginning of this  year, Israel simply suspended the decree, temporarily and for an unspecified time. Such a suspension,  of course, is not acceptable; Military Order No. 854 still exists, and it is in Israel's power to apply it  whenever it wishes. It is a knife held at the throats of the academic institutions of the occupied territories.  It is Unesco's duty now to take all necessary measures to give effect to its previous resolution and to werk  for the complete cancellation of this abominable Israeli decree. The second matter concerns a document  which academics applying for work permits are required to sign. The occupation authorities have recently  isgyed a new document which all foreign members of university teaching staffs are required to sign as a  pre-condition of permission to work at a Palestinian university. This document commits the applicant "not  to perform any work or provide any service constituting assistance or support for the organization known  as the Palestine I “eration Organization or any other hostile organization’. The contents of this document  are inconsistent with the United Nations resolutions recognizing the Palestine Liberation Organization as  the sole legitimate representative of the Palestinian people. In addition, this document is a form of pol-
214   itical blackmail which is incompatible with the most elementary principles observed in academic institu-  tions. Under international law, Israel does not have the right to impose its political point of view on  persons working in Palestinian universities. The instructors concerned have refused to sign the document,  and as a result the occupation authorities, in the course of the past month, have expelled more than 25  teachers, out of a total of 130 who are expected to be expelled after the expiry of the permits or visas  allowing them to reside in the occupied territories. These instructors account for a substantial percentage  of the entire teaching staff, and consequently their expulsion will seriously damage, or even paralyse  altogether, higher education in the occupied territories. The occupation authorities claim that most states  and academic institutions throughout the world impose political undertakings of this kind on foreign uni-  versity teaching staff. In point of fact, this claim is false, since no academic institution anywhere in the  world, including Israel, requires any such political undertaking. It appears that as a result of media  pressure and international pressure, Israel decided to amend the document in question just before the  beginning of this Conference. However, the amendment in question does not affect the wording or the con-  tent cf the document, but only the procedure involved, and consequently it should be noted that this s0-  called amendment is a mere trumped=-up, artificial device designed to mislead and to afford relief from  media Jressure.
(12.9) Unesco is now called upon to act with all speed to develop adequate means of putting an end to this  binding document altogether, in letter, spirit and application, and bringing about the reinstatement of all  those university teachers who have been expelled as a result of it. This duty is, of course, part of the  task of attaining the most elementary human rights and freedoms, in accordance with the demands of  ethics, custom, and international conventions.
(12.10) The situation, Mr President, is very serious, both inside the occupied homeland and outside it.  Israel's continuing pursuit of its hostile policy towards the Palestinian people and their fundamental rights  constitutes a threat to peace--that peace towards which we are all striving, and which is one of the major  axes and objectives for which Unesco stands.
(12.11) Lastly, I should like to convey my thanks and appreciation to those heads of delegations who have  spoken before me in this forum and expressed their solidarity with the Palestinian people and their just  cause. I should also like to thank Unesco for the efforts it is making to strengthen the foundations of peace  and international understanding. Thank you.

14.1 M. NAGY (Hongrie).-  Monsieur le Président, Mesdames et Messieurs, en ce qui concerne le Projet de plan à moyen  terme, j'aimerais souligner que nous partageons entièrement le point de vue du Directeur général  qui, dans le document soumis à l'examen de la Conférence générale, ainsi que dans son discours  d'ouverture, a constaté que l'action de l'Unesco ne saurait être efficace que si elle s'appuyait sur  l'analyse des problèmes cruciaux de notre époque pour procéder ensuite à la définition des me-  sures que l'Organisation aura à adopter afin de contribuer à leur solution par le moyen de l'édu-  cation, des sciences, de la culture et de la communication.
14.2 Nous considérons que le Projet de plan à moyen terme est dans son ensemble conforme aux  directives formulées par la Conférence générale à sa vingt et unième session dans sa résolu-  tion 100. Il s'inspire des principes fondamentaux énoncés par l'Acte constitutif de l'Unesco et  tient dûment compte des résolutions adoptées dans le cadre de différentes réunions internationales  importantes. Je tiens, au nom de mon gouvernement, à en féliciter chaleureusement tous ceux qui  ont collaboré à l'élaboration de ce Projet de plan.
14.3 11 faut, à mon sens, souligner tout spécialement la pertinence de la définition du défi  fondamental qui se pose au monde contemporain, qui est donnée dans la première partie et à  laquelle nous souscrivons totalement : est-il possible "de faire face aux problèmes fondamentaux  qui se posent à l'humanité et en particulier à celui d'un développement envisagé selon une vision  planétaire, si des sociétés humaines sont dressées les unes contre les autres et si pèse sur  l'humanité la menace de la guerre nucléaire, c'est-à-dire de l'anéantissement ?" Face à ce pro-  blème, ne l'oublions surtout pas, aucun effort ne doit être ménagé pour mettre fin aux différentes  manifestations de confrontation et pour atténuer la menace d'un conflit nucléaire. C'est pourquoi  nuue approuvons pleinement le fait que la paix, la compréhension internationale, les droits de  l'homme, entendus dans leur sens le plus large comme signifiant le droit de l'humanité à la vie,  reçoivent toute l'attention voulue dans le Plan à moyen terme. L'aspiration au respect de l'égalité  en droits de tous et de chacun doit ainsi présider à l'ensemble de l'action de l'Organisation. Je  saisis ici l'occasion de dire l'émoi qui m'a étreint en entendant l'intervention du délégué de  l'Organisation de libération de la Palestine tout à l'heure, et de l'assurer de notre entière  solidarité avec les actions menées pour la libération du peuple palestinien et la défense de ses  droits.
14.4 Les Etats et les peuples se rapprochent de plus en plus et l'humanité dispose - en prin-  cipe - aujourd'hui des ressources nécessaires pour résoudre les problèmes qui se posent à elle,
215   pour assurer à chaque être humain les conditions d'une vie digne de l'homme. D'autre part,  l'aggravation de la situation internationale ainsi que les efforts désespérés que déploient les forces  réactionnaires pour rétablir leur suprématie annihilée par le progrès historique de l'humanité  remplissent d'inquiétude et incitent à l'action la communauté internationale, ainsi que tout individu  de bonne volonté.
14.5 Si nous ne parvenons pas à temps à empêcher ces forces de nuire, toute la vie de notre  planète sera en danger. Rien d'étonnant donc à ce que soient de plus en plus nombreuses les  voix qui s'élèvent en faveur d'un désarmement général. Un rôle de plus en plus important incombe  en la matière aux organisations internationales, et par conséquent aussi à l'Unesco.
14,6 Que peut faire l'Unesco dans cette perspective ? Avant tout, elle doit s'employer de manière  conséquente à mettre en oeuvre l'idée directrice contenue dans le Plan à moyen terme. Le déve-  loppement réel de l'éducation, des sciences, de la culture et de la communication est d'ailleurs  inconcevable sans la prise en compte des facteurs sociaux, économiques et culturels ainsi que de  l'évolution générale des sociétés. Une organisation internationale ne peut être réellement efficace,  même dans ses domaines de compétence, si elle ne tient pas compte des réalités politiques d'une  situation donnée.
14.7 De par la noble mission que lui assigne son Acte constitutif, l'Unesco est appelée à repré-  senter la conscience du monde dans les domaines de la culture et des sciences. En s'acquittant de  cette fonction, elle n'a pas à craindre qu'on la soupçonne d'idéalisme exagéré comme on le fait  parfois. Ce qui importe, c'est que dans son action et dans su réflexion, elle soit guidée par  l'analyse approfondie de la situation mondiale ainsi que par les intérêts et les aspirations de ses  Etats membres. L'Unesco est unc organisation intergouvernementale ; qu'elle devienne donc une  instance internationale montrant l'exemple de l'action juste aux gouvernements. L'Unesco devrait  en outre s'efforcer de recueillir les connaissances et les données les plus à jour sur les problèmes  d'ampleur internationale dans les domaines relevant de sa compétence et asseoir sur ces bases ses  analyses théoriques et ses propres hypothèses de travail.
14.8 La politique socialiste du gouvernement de la République populaire hongroise tient elle aussi  compte des contextes sociaux dans lesquels s'inscrivent les questions relatives à la science, à la  culture et à l'éducation. Son point de départ est la conviction que la société socialiste doit  assurer l'épanouissement de la vie de l'individu, de son développement et d'une action culturelle  de caractère universel, bases du progrès de la société tout entière. Dans notre société, la pos-  sibilité pour l'individu de s'épanouir est aussi garantie par le fait que toute personne a accès à  l'éducation et à la culture.
14.9 La République populaire hongroise est prête à faire profiter la communauté internationale de  l'expérience qu'elle a acquise dans ces domaines ; dans le même temps, elle s'attache à étudier les  expériences des autres afin d'enrichir sa propre action en la matière.
14.10 On sait que pendant que se tient cette session extraordinaire de la Conférence générale,  les représentants des pays européens sont réunis à Madrid dans le cadre de la Conférence sur la  sécurité et la coopération en Europe. À ce propos, j'aimerais attirer l'attention sur l'importance de  la coopération régionale européenne dans le domaine culturel et scientifique. L'Unesco devrait  poursuivre et renforcer son action dans ce domaine, d'autant que la mise en valeur des connais-  sances scientifique et des créations culturelles accumulées sur notre continent au cours des siècles  pourrait également contribuer au développement des activités menées par l'Unesco dans les autres  régions.
14.11 Monsieur le Président, j'aimerais soulever quelques questions ayant trait à la structure  audacieusement nouvelle du Plan à moyen terme. Le caractère des problèmes globaux abordés dans  les programmes nécessite naturellement l'adoption d'une approche interdisciplinaire ; il ne serait  d'ailleurs pas possible de les résoudre autrement. Ce qui ne paraît pas clair toutefois, c'est la  manière dont cela se traduira au niveau de l'action. Il serait en outre utile de préciser comment  s'effectuera l'évaluation des programmes et budgets biennaux.
14.12 Après ces remarques générales concernant l'ensemble du document, permettez-moi de dire  quelques mots concernant la substance des programmes. Notre opinion repose sur une étude appro-  fondie du projet, effectuée en collaboration étroite avec nos experts.
14.13 Les trois premiers grands programmes concernant l'éducation ont recueilli une edhésion  générale car ils reflètent fidèlement les interrelations existant entre les divers aspects de la  question et ils se fixent à juste titre pour obiectif principal d'élucider les causes des problèmes et  de formuler des propositions en vue de leur solution.
14.14 L'analphabétisme est effectivement un des problèmes les plus graves de l'humanité, dont la  solution appelle l'adoption d'une approche interdisciplinaire. De nos jours, où savoir lire et écrire  est fondamental, l'analphabétisme est un handicap qui entrave l'exercice des droits fondamentaux  de l'individu. L'éradication de l'analphabétisme non seulement exige une intervention politique,  économique, juridique et pédagogique mais suppose également - en plus de la volonté des gouver-  nements et des institutions officielles d'atteindre cet objectif - que des mesures soient prises et
216   des méthodes globales et coordonnées mises au point pour assurer non seulement l'apprentissage  de l'écriture et de la lecture, mais aussi l'utilisation régulière et active des connaissances  acquises.
14.15 Si nous voulons vraiment rendre efficace l'action d'éducation et d'instruction de demain,  nous devons changer les systèmes éducatifs traditionnels, qui doivent largement s'ouvrir sur le  monde du travail et sur les différents lieux de l'action culturelle. Un accent plus net doit aussi  être mis sur les questions concernant le temps libre et les loisirs.
14.16 L'enseignement des langues et l'enseignement dans la langue maternelle, notamment dans le  cas des langues peu répandues, ne bénéficient pas, à notre avis, de toute l'attention qu'ils mé-  ritent. Les programmes éducatifs et culturels devraient prendre davantage en considération les  aspects interdisciplinaires liés à la situation et aux aspirations des minorités nationales,  ethniques, linguistiques et culturelles.
14.17 Il est juste que l'enseignement primaire occupe une place privilégiée dans le programme  d'éducation, car dans le monde - abstraction faite du problème de l'alphabétisation - c'est cer-  tainement à ce niveau que les efforts les plus sérieux s'imposent. II faut également se réjouir de  ce que le programme de l'Unesco ait fait, au cours de la dernière période, davantage de place à  l'enseignement supérieur. J'aimerais toutefois attirer l'attention sur le fait que dans certains Etats  membres, où le problème de l'enseignement élémentaire peut être considéré comme résolu, c'est  l'enseignement secondaire qui pose toute une série de questions pédagogiques et socio-éducatives  complexes. Ce problème devrait recevoir dans le Projet de plan à moyen terme une attention cor-  respondant à son importance.
14.18 En ce qui concerne les chapitre relatifs aux sciences exactes et naturelles, nos experts  soulignent qu'ils témoignent d'une préparation approfondie et formulent un ensemble d'objectifs  bien conçus et rationnels. Nous avons constaté avec satisfaction que le Projet de plan à moyen  terme prend nettement position pour le développement de la recherche fondamentale, qui vient  sans cesse compléter notre connaissance de l'univers par de nouvelles théories. C'est sur des  bases objectives que le projet souligne l'importance de la recherche spatiole et met en relief la  signification de la biologie dans l'évolution de la société.
14.19 Cependant, il faudrait s'efforcer encore davantage d'accroître le rôle du programme des  sciences exactes et naturelles, car à notre sens la place réservée à ce domaine dans le programme  de l'Orgarisation ne reflète pas suffisamment son importance. Nous nous réjouissons d'autant plus  de constater que le Projet de plan envisage la continuation des programmes intergouvernementaux  qui ont déjà obtenu des succès notables.
