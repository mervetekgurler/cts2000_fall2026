[1] Professor VERNIERS (Belgium) wished to  pay tribute to the authors of the report on the use  of indigenous languages in education [document
6C/OXR/5, Annex 1]. That document was  extremely useful and should be widely dis-  seminated. The Belgian delegation hoped that  Unesco would continue its work in that field. It  wished to draw the Commission’s attention to the  “Journées d’études coloniales” recently held in  Brussels, which might provide the Organization  with useful information on education.  As regards the proposed measures for the  eradication of illiteracy in non-self-governing ter-  ritories [document 5C/OXR/5, Annex II], the  Belgian delegation particularly appreciated the fact  that the authors of the document had taken into  account the variety of aspects of that problem  occurring in the various territories concerned.  There should, in fact, be no uniform campaign;  rather, efforts should be made to safeguard the tra-  ditional values of each of the territories, while  enriching them with the experience of the more  developed countries.  The Belgian delegation expressed its satisfaction  that Unesco was collaborating with the United  Nations, and particularly with the Trusteeship  Council, in seeking a solution to those general  problems.  [2] Shafik GHORBAL Bey (Egypt) associated  himself with the remarks of the delegate of Bel-  gium. He thoroughly approved the Executive  Board’s recommendations for the continuation of  work in the future, but felt that a small clause  might be added at the end of the first or second  paragraphs of the recommendations of the Ex-  ecutive Board on page 2 of document 5C/OXR/5  Add. 1, as follows:  With special reference to conditions under which a  national language has been subordinated to that of  the administrating Power and the effect of such  subordination on national culture and national  institutions.  ‘ He explained that the word “national” did not  refer to any small tribal language, but to a  recognized national language which had been  subordinated to that of an administrating Power.  His delegation did not wish to indicate whether the  effect of such subordination had been good or bad,  but left it to the Secretariat, in its survey, to  ascertain the effect.  [3] Mr. ODEGAARD (United States of America)  recalled that the question of non-self-governing  territories had first been under consideration after  the San Francisco Conference, and it was very  fortunate that the United Nations had been  requested to keep in mind the educational develop-  ment of people in those territories, as well as other  aspects. He welcomed document 5C/OXR/5 -  Annex III as a very important contribution to the  consideration of the whole matter, and hoped that  Unesco would give much attention to the problem.  He asked the Secretariat to give the Commission  some idea of the cost of the study and the length  of time which it was likely to take, and also of the  manner in which it would be related to the entire  fundamental education programme.  [4] Mr. ELVIN (Director, Department of Educa-  tion), replying to the question about the cost of  the study, referred to paragraph I on page 86 ol  the Budget Estimates, where there was an item  of $3.000 for fees for studies of the use of the  mother-tongue in education. The Budget Com-  mittee had pointed out that that item was nol  related to a programme resolution, but it was  related to the study under discussion and the  money could therefore be transferred to the latter.  It was estimated that the cost of the whole project  in 1951, including that $3,000, would be  $34,000.  With regard to a plan of work, it was intended  to use the $3,000 for preparatory studies and  working papers for a conference of experts on  indigenous languages and for the fight against  illiteracy. It was proposed to hold that meeting  in 1951, convening experts representing, not only  the non-self-governing territories and adminis-  trating Powers, but also other Member States. It  was planned that the Conference would divide into  three groups: (1) to study the problem arising  from the presence both of a native language and
398                                      of a second language; (2) to consider the problem  of the fight against illiteracy; (3) to examine  psychological and social questions arising from  the first two problems.  With regard to the question of the time involved,  the Secretariat thought that each of the three  divisions of the Conference might do work which  would lead in later years to authoritative volumes  on each of the problems. The relationship of the  study to fundamental education was very close,  and much of the work already done in funda-  mental education would assist in carrying out the  project under discussion.
