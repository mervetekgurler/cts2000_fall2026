We can recall no more terrible drama than the  bitter struggles between the Cross and Islam :  Godefroy de Bouillon and Richard Cœur de Lion,  gallant Knights, came here with arms in their  hands to fight against men of equal courage and  nobility. Not far from here lie the ashes of the  great Saladin. In our day, we see a change from  the past and it is under the banner of brother-  hood that our work has started in this historic  place. You have shown us, from your mountain  tops, history unfolding century by century in your  great monuments, and we have taken up the thread  of past ages. We feel we are all the joint heirs  of a common past, in which the words of Christ  and the chapters of the Koran both contribute to  the welfare of humanity.  Mr. President, on the day that Unesco was born,  a cry of hope and joy echoed through the world.  The appeal we made to the peoples has a great-  ness that the misery of our time had made us  forget. What did the new Organization mean to  do? To build in the minds of men the defences  of peace; to increase mutual understanding among  peoples, in order to eliminate suspicion and mis-  trust; to reaffirm the ideal of dignity, equality  and respect for the human person; to diffuse  culture and education in the cause of justice,  liberty and peace—a sacred duty to be accom-  plished in a spirit of mutual assistance; to establish  peace on the basis of the intellectual and moral  solidarity of mankind; to ensure full opportunities  for education for all, the unrestricted pursuit of  objective truth and the free exchange of ideas and  knowledge, the free circulation of ideas by word  and image; and to assist the maintenance, increase  and diffusion of knowledge.  We thus thought we were once more in the  presence of the great figures of Confucius and  St. Francis of Assisi, the philosophers Aristotle and  Descartes, and great scientists such as Pasteur and  Pierre Curie. We imagined suddenly a lay cathe-  dral arising out of the ruins and embracing all  religious creeds, all currents of philosophic  thought, all types of reformers and seekers after  truth—all in the cause of preserving and improv-  ing the human race. We hailed the day and the  hour as a renaissance.  Then a certain ambiguity arose, which still  weighs upon the destiny of our Organization. For  accomplishing this task—a missionary task—  what were we offered? A General Conference, an  Executive Board and a Secretariat, all dependent  on the authority of States. The spiritual and the  temporal kingdom cannot compromise; either they  separate and work in their respective fields or, if  they encroach on each other, they produce confu-  sion and chaos. Certainly we have political                directives to follow; we are a Specialized Agency of  the United Nations and it is for the parent body  to design the general framework within which  Unesco must evolve in its own domain of culture,  science and education. But we must have that  minimum autonomy which our universities enjoy,  autonomy in deciding on our programme, auto-  nomy in the choice of our guiding principles,  within the framework set up for us by our Consti-  tution. Ever since then this ambiguity has hin-  dered our work. It is difficult to fix a rigid line of  demarcation; there is a sort of No-Man’s Land  between spiritual and temporal forces, and only  the Catholicism of the Middle Ages was able to  impose on humanity a clear line of distinction.  But the very history of our Organization is a  constant reminder, gentlemen, that unless the  situation is cleared up we shall work in a spirit of  doubt, suspicion and mutual distrust.  Our Organization is only three years old, but it  has experienced enough to prove that our prime  duty is to show the peoples who gave us our  mandate whether Unesco is a non-political organ-  ization or whether, on the other hand, we have  merely opened up, under the aegis of scientists  and educationists, a new battlefield, a fresh oppor-  tunity for national competition  If we recall the moment when we elected our  eminent Director-General, we remember that this  doubtful situation created an anomaly : the man  chosen by the Executive Board to hold office for  six years could accept the post for two years only.  How, in so short a time, can a new world be built.  the structure be erected, or the voice of the Organ-  ization be heard throughout the world, when care-  [ul reflection and energetic combined effort are  required” We have seen the Executive Board  itself faced with similar difficulties in the choice  of its own members. We have seen resolutions  against the promoters of war, which were in  fact inspired solely by a national ideology, put  forward in the guise of an international ideal.  Each time the Executive Board has dealt with an  acute problem (such as Unesco’s work in Ger-  many) needing very careful treatment, the utmost  precaution at the same time, and a broad over-all  view, cleavages of opinion, inspired by purely  national political interests, have nevertheless  appeared.  At Mexico City, politics appeared in various  guises, and the working language, which should  have been a bond among those serving Unesco,  became instead a cause for competition and the  exercice of regional or national influence. The DIRECTOR-GENERAL : Mr. President,  delegates, I welcome this opportunity of making  a few observations on the interesting and often  eloquent speeches which we have heard during  the last several meetings. First of all, I welcome  the opportunity of again adding, to those of all  the speakers in this particular discussion, my own  tribute to the great effort which has been made  and readily accomplished by the Government of  Lebanon to render it possible for the Conference  to be held here in such admirable surrondings.  I note the very pertinent points made by the  delegate of the United States of America, but in a  troubled area such as this much trouble could  be avoided if everyone were to adopt the Unesco  principle of the oneness of mankind. I think if  only fair to say that a great many other types of  trouble also could be, and would be, avoided if  Unesco’s principles were fully implemented by  all the countries of the world.  Then I note the agreement from every quarter  regarding the great cultural interest of this region  in which we are meeting. We have been remin-  ded of the connexions between this country  and India, between this country and Italy, the  Iberian Peninsula, the United States, Mexico,  West Africa and other regions. It is a most  interesting story of conquerors accross the seas.  I think we have all learnt something from  holding this Conference here and from the  speeches we have heard in this discussion about  the contributions made by this region. I would  remind you of the basic fact, basic to any history  of mankind, that all the fundamental discoveries  and inventions on which civilization is based have  come, not from the so-called West, but from the  ancient East, more particularly from the Middle  Fast : agriculture, writing, alphabetic writing,  building in stone, astronomy, the wheel, all these  great inventions and probably others have come  [rom this great region of the Middle East, not to  mention two of the great religions of the world.  This fact—that we have been learning a great  deal about the cultural contacts of this region  and the contributions it has made—justifies  the holding of our Conference in a different  region as often as possible. One delegate said,  if I understood him aright, that I had spoken  with two voices on the question whether the  Conference should be held away from head-  quarters or at headquarters. I do not think  that is wholly correct. What I did my best to  point out in my Report was that there were  advantage and disadvantages in both solutions,  but that they were of a different nature, so that  one could not weigh the advantages of one course  directly against the advantages of the other course. I also hope very much that Unesco will speedily  b able to implement the suggestion of delegates  from the United Kingdom that we co-ordinate our  calendar with that proposed by the United Nations  for all Specialized Agencies, so that we should  hold our Conference in the first instead of the  second half of the year. One of the things that a scientist learns—if  he has not learned it before—when he joins an  organization like Unesco, concerned with all the  activities of the human spirit, is that, although  science is in certain aspects good in itself, and its  advance should be encouraged for its own sake,  yet it is necessary to have a general framework  of ideas, including values, in which science and  technique can operate for the general advantage  of mankind.
