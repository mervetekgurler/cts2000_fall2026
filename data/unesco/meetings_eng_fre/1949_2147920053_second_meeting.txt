Mr. de LACHARRIERE (France) explained that the aim for the time being was merely to establish principles, on the basis of which draft regulations concerning recommendations to Member States and international conventions would be framed by the Secretariat. An international convention was an unusual and important instrument which had to be most carefully prepared. Unesco’s prestige was bound up with the success of the international conventions it sponsored. The con- vention must therefore be effective and must be voted by a large majority. In Beirut, the General Conference had adopted a convention by too slender a majority. The delegate of France agreed with the procedure outlined by his United States colleague. He urged the necessity for exercising extreme caution with regard to international conventions and, in particular, for respecting the constitutional provisions of individual States; above all, care should be taken to have the treaties and conventions ratified by the correct authority. Dr. CHEDIAK (Cuba) shared the views of the previous speakers. He pointed out that recom- mendations differed from conventions only in being less binding, the prelimenary work and technical formalities being the same for both. To illustrate his point, he referred to the work of the Committee of Experts on Copyright, which had met at Unesco in July of that year. An inter- national convention had to be based on detailed investigations by experts. Dr. Chediak suggested the establishment of a permanent legal commission to be responsible for framing draft conventions; he thought such a commission preferable to a meeting of experts delegated by States, for not all States could send experts. Reports on the work of such a commission would be communicated to Member States for their comments. Mr. MULLIKEN (United States of America) summed up the discussions. When a convention was desirable, the Secretariat should prepare a report for submission to the next session of the General Conference, where it would be discussed. It appeared from the discussion that the Secretariat would compile a questionnaire to be sent to Member States. On the basis of the replies received and the opinion of a few experts, a draft convention would be framed; the latter would be despatched to Member States, so that it could be profitably discussed and, if possible, approved by the following session of the General Confer- ence. Accordingly, the Secretariat could submit to one session of the General Conference a report proposing the establishment of a convention and then, after full investigations had been carried out, lay before the following session a draft prepared by experts. The General Conference should only consider drafts that had been carefully prepared and perfected in their technical details by experts. The CHAIRMAN called upon the Legal Adviser to give some technical explanations regarding the definition of recommendations, in compliance with Mr. ANTONIO CASTRO LEAL (Mexico) the request made by the Cuban delegate at the preceding meeting. Dr. SABA (Legal Adviser) referred to the recom- mendations regarding the programme, especially those formulated at Beirut. Instances were the requests to Member States regarding the issue of certain stamps with a surcharge, encouragement to institutions and organizations in their territories to co-operate more closely with Unesco or to take   part in the United Nations and Unesco campaigns for children; those requests had taken the form of recommendations. As an instance of a regulative recommendation, the Legal Adviser briefly described a recom- mendation of the International Labour Organiza- tion regarding vocational guidance. He pointed out that the practice of the International Labour Organization had always been to ask Member States if they deemed it desirable to frame inter- national regulations on some particular problem and whether such regulations should take the form of an international convention or the simpler form of a recommendation. Dr. Saba explained that recommendations did not have the binding force of a convention ratified by States, nor did they involve such strict or specific obligations as an international convention. What the States which had assisted in the framing of the Constitution of Unesco had had in mind when drafting Article IV, paragraph 4, was a recommendation which might have the force of a regulation. The procedure for drafting mere recommenda- tions did not involve any preliminary investigations or the sending of questionnaires to Member States. However, that did not reduce the scope of such recommendations, nor would the fact that special procedure was being contemplated for drafting regulative recommendations necessitate any amend- ment of Article IV, paragraph, 4, of the Constitu- tion, as it was stipulated in Article VIII that every Member State should report to the Organization “on the action taken upon the recommenda- tions and conventions referred to in Article IV, paragraph 4”. Professor EUSTATHIADES (Greece) thought that, as there were not enough lawyers present to deal with the question in detail, it would be preferable for the time being to confine the dis- cussion to general considerations. He noted that, according to document 4C/OXR/14, only eight governments had sent their comments on the draft to the Secretariat, which had consequently been unable to prepare a draft convention in time. He contended that the first necessity was to define the general policy to be followed with regard to inter- national conventions and the procedure to be adopted for voting on recommendations and draft conventions. In the light of these general discus- sions, certain conclusions could be drawn to serve as a basis for well-considered draft regulations to be submitted to the Fifth Session of the General Conference. The delegate of Greece recalled that two pro- posals had been submitted: one, by the delegation of the United States of America, outlining the procedure to be followed, and the other, by the Cuban delegation, advocating the establishmentof a permanent legal commission to study the question. Professor Eustathiades would be in favour of setting up a committee to frame a draft convention on the basis of the general instructions given by the Commission. He also suggested that, if the investigations could not be completed in time for a draft convention to be submitted to the Fifth Session of the Conference, the committee could continue working on the draft while the Confer- ence was in session. It would therefore be desir- able, in his opinion, for each delegation to include a legal expert among its members at the Florence Conference. Returning to the constitutional question raised by the French delegate at the preceding meeting, Professor Eustathiades thought that, since gov- ernments would give full powers to their delega- tions at the General Conference to negotiate a convention, the prerogative of ratification by Heads of States would not be endangered. There would merely be a special procedure for the negotiation of an international convention. Dr. BOOR (Czechoslovakia) remarked that the fact that very few Member States had sent their comments on the draft regulations concerning re- commendations and conventions was a clear indication that the draft was considered undesir- able and inapplicable. Many Member States were disinclined to subscribe to an international con- vention and they would probably resent any moral pressure from Unesco. Dr. Boor thought that mere recommendations would be more effective, as well as more in accordance with the spirit of the Constitution. Mr. de LACHARRIERE (France) thought that the main purpose of the discussion was to show the Secretariat what principles should subsequently guide its action; as matters then stood, it was useless to go into details. He recognized that regulative recommendations, such as those defined by the Secretariat in its memorandum, were extremely important instruments, to which recourse should be had only in serious, if not exceptional, cases. The delegate of France did not think it possible to set up immediately the com- mittee suggested by Professor Eustathiades, as not all the delegations included the necessary legal experts. In conclusion, he emphasized the pre- liminary nature of the discussion, which could be closed as soon as the guiding principles were sufficiently clear to show the Secretariat how it should continue its work. Mr. de BLONAY (Head, Bureau of External Relations) asked that a committee should be entrusted with the task of condensing into a resolution the general principles set forth during       the discussion; it would be borne in mind in framing the final draft for submission to a future session of the General Conference. Mr. de LACHARRIERE (France) would agree to the establishment of a committee, provided that it was merely a drafting committee. Mr. COWELL (United Kingdom) associated himself with M. de Lacharrière’s proposal that a drafting committee should be appointed to report back to the Commission. Mr. MULLIKEN (United States of America) did likewise, but wished to make a few comments on the difference between recommendations and conventions. The United States delegation had carefully studied document 4C/OXR/14-Adden- dum, and recognized that Unesco’s Constitution provided for the adoption of recommendations to governments as well as conventions. The General Conference would often wish to make recommendations to governments, but the United States delegation did not consider that any special regulations were required at the present time in connexion with recommendations to governments. Possibly the Conference, in its resolutions, might wish to emphasize recommendations to govern- ments by using the word “recommend”, as opposed to “invite”. If committees and com- missions of the Conference adopted this method in their resolutions, a step forward could be made; but it seemed inappropriate to the United States delegation to go further at the present time. Professor Eustathiades and Dr. Saba had referred to the procedure of ILO, where a system of con- ventions and recommendations was involved. The question was whether Unesco’s General Conference wished, like ILO, to devote the major part of its time to the consideration of conventions and recommendations; this seemed unlikely and the parallels drawn between the methods of ILO and of Unesco were therefore less close than had been suggested. Mr. NICHOLAS (Australia) was in general agreement with the remarks made by Mr. Mulliken. With regard to the distinction between recom- mendations and conventions, the meaning of “con- vention” was not the same in Unesco’s Constitu- tion as it was in the Charter of ILO : in Unesco's Constitution it meant a form of recommendation to Member States; in the Charter of ILO it meant a treaty. The Australian Federal Government had discussed the treaties of ILO and had come to the conclusion that they could not be accepted by a government with a limited constitution; at the instigation of the United States, it had been arranged that federal governments should take conventions as recommendations to be passed on to their federal states.
