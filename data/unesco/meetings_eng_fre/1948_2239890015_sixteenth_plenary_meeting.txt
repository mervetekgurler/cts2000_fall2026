Hence the  importance of Unesco; for Unesco, truth and peace  constitute essentially a single enterprise.  A serious and disquieting doubt at present pre-  vails among all nations about the power of our  Organization. It would be a betrayal of the ideas  we uphold to ignore this doubt. But  we must realize that many of the aims which  inspired us in creating Unesco are now in jeopardy.  Far from allowing ourselves to be disheartened, it  is our duty to work with added zeal to restore the  full value of those aims. Unless that  conscience triumphs, it will be difficult for the  new World Order to assert itself durably.  In pondering over the present world crisis, we  realize that the discords of the time constitute the  most eloquent proof of the distress of the peoples  in their efforts to attain at one bound, to the three  aims : Liberty, Equality, Fraternity, proclaimed by  the French Revolution. There are still individuals  and groups who place the idea of liberty above  that of equality. Other groups and individuals set  the ideal of equality above that of liberty.  Both of them suffer, for both of them forget  that, without equality, liberty becomes a vain
168  symbol, to the advantage of the readier and more  powerful and that, without individual liberty,  equality would soon become a sorry, undistinguish-  ed thing. The most distressing feature in the  drama of our civilization is that the two protago-  nists are on the one hand, society, a joint and  inseparable whole which it is, alas, so difficult to  guide in one direction and unite for this single  purpose; and on the other hand, individual Man. In the endeavour to reconcile these two terms,  the world has, after every great struggle, believed  that a saving formula was discovered in a treaty  of international co-operation. But how  can we imagine an effective union of nations  without a previous union of the minds which are  the common denominator of each nation? The State must constitute a bridge for good  understanding between man and humanity. Unesco is different in that it owed its creation to  the belief that “wars begin in the minds of men”.  Unless we accepted this postulate, our institution  would be meaningless. Peace, like war, is above  all, a state of mind. We have no hesitation in  affirming that this was our fundamental idea.  We believed, and still believe, that in whatever  philosophy man seeks the reason for his being, he  is a living centre of self-determination. Neither  the absence of material well-being nor the evolu-  tion of science can urge man irresistibly to war,  if his mind be not first invaded by a hostile  determination.  That being so, the essential purpose of the  United Nations : *‘to save succeeding generations  from the scourge of war”, will remain unattain-  ably remote, unless we strive to destroy war in its  most secret stronghold : the human mind. Tt

Scientific truth,  indeed, consists of a body of objective evidence  which is comprehensible to all and whose validity  all must admit. The problem here is simply to  discover the best means of disseminating scientific  knowledge as widely as possible through educa-  tional curricula and methods, exchange of books  and information and other practical measures.  Moral solidarity, on the other hand, bristles with  difficulties, and we might easily be discouraged  by the number of different existing systems and  the variety of ways of looking at the world and at  life.  We do not, of course, claim that all men,  in every part of the world, should agree on the  philosophical principles of their conduct. In every part of the world, voices  proclaim that we are moving towards a new  humanism. The intellect has exercised all its latent energies  in the field of technology, in the subjection of  nature to man, but has proved incapable of firing  the train of moral grandeur. Born of the war, its mission 1s to  fight against war. And it is precisely the slender  nature of the means available to us in this fight  that brings me to the third difficulty.  Very often, words have little relation to the true  implications of ideas. À disastrous gulf has opened  between speech and action. In the presence of  this danger, Unesco must build up a rampart of  truth against the eloquence of officialdom, all too  ready to make promises but tardy in keeping  them.     , The difficulty in this, as in so many other mat-  ters, is due to the atmosphere of transition in  which we are now living. Undivided sovereignty  of the State is today a legal survival which there  is no eluding. The strong brandish it like a  spear; the weak shelter behind it as behind  a shield. So internationalism, in spite ol  all, is still only a noble hope for the future. lo  expect Unesco, by its mere existence, to change  the innermost -nature of men, is to expect a  miracle which Unesco can never perform. We  are no weavers of spells; our only appeal is to  principles. In order to advance the welfare of  man, a path has been marked out for us which we  cannot ignore. To follow this path we must have  the consent of governments. The National Com-  missions, it is true, make up for certain constitu-  tional restrictions upon our Organization, but have  we made sure that in all countries the National  Commissions are able to operate on the scale which  the problems of our times require?  This is a very delicate task. Unesco has no  right to interfere in the domestic affairs of govern-  ments. We have neither the power nor the wish  to transform the Organization into a world Ministry  of Education. Nor do we want to burden the  impoverished countries still further with unjusti-  fiable bureaucratic machinery. Our work must be  based, above all, on two factors for which no  substitute has so far been found : the good faith  of governments and the force of principles. We  shall continue to demand these essential condi-  tions, for, without them, it would be wiser to post-  pone our hopes to better days. Fortunately, that  is not necessary. We do not view the future with  pessimism. Much has been accomplished by  Unesco since the London Conference. It is only  fair to recognize that all the members of the  Executive Board, as well as the whole staff of the  Secretariat, have contributed in a marked degree  to these successes; and I will ask them to colla-  borate with me in the same spirit in which they  worked with my distinguished predecessor,  Dr. Huxley.  In naming that eminent representative of con-  temporary learning, I realize how much Unesco  has lost by his departure. For us, the memory of  Dr. Huxley's gifts and authority will be imperish-  able. Unesco owes him the highest possible  tribute.  The applause which follows him as he goes  brings home to me the tremendous magnitude of  the tasks that await his successor. In the accom-  plishment of those tasks, I shall need your constant  goodwill and support.  Gentlemen, during the summer of 1947  read in the newspapers of a very tragic happening.  I refer to an accident that occured off the coast of  WE  )

Those responsible for them leave concealed  in the waters, theoretically restored to peaceful  traffic, many deadly mines. The most dangerous  of them all is intolerance.  To perpetuate intolerance would be to allow  our way to be strewn with a mechanism as fear-  ful as that which caused the death of those Italian  children on that bright summer’s day. Dr. Wadie FARAG (Egypt) (Rapporteur of the  Procedure Committtee) (Translation) : Mr. Pre-  sident, gentlemen, as my Report indicates, the  Procedure Committee, of which I had the honour  to be Rapporteur, held nine meetings to consider  suggested Rules of Procedure in draft submitted  to us by the General Committee. We discussed  this draft, rule by rule, and took a separate vote  on each section. You have before you the draft  Rules of Procedure in both working languages;  they comprise 104 rules and constitute a complete  procedural code for the Organization. I am bound  to say that the work of our Committee, compared  with that of similar committees in national par-  liaments, was in the highest degree pondered and  scientific. In addition, we had before us amend-  ments to some articles of the Constitution and a  certain amount of other business.  The report before you is divided into three sec-  tions covering the points I have just outlined. The  first section deals with amendments to the Consti-  tution. In this connexion, it is my duty to bring  to your attention a minor error which has crept  into the report : referring to the amendment to  Article IV E of the Constitution, submitted to you  by the Director-General, it is stated that this draft  was adopted; I must inform you that it was in  fact discussed at length, and a resolution was sub-  mitted by the Australian delegation, but it was not  adopted; consideration of the text was postponed  until later, and it will come up for discussion and  adoption at a plenary meeting. It follows, then,  that the discussion on this part of the Report must  be reopened in plenary meeting when a decision  will be taken. Furthermore, the actual text of  the draft rules has been re-cast, or corrected, or  subjected to minor changes in wording. We have  underlined those rules which have been the sub-  ject of extensive re-casting and in this connexion  I have only a few points to make.  Firstly, with reference to the matter of working  languages, all those who attended the meeting of  the Procedure Committee probably remember the  thorough discussion of this point. I do not wish  to recapitulate the long debate regarding Spanish.  A satisfactory compromise was finally arrived at,  and the portions of the text referring to working  languages have accordingly been re-cast in that  sense.  Secondly, a new fourth paragraph had been  added to the old Rule 22 on Credentials. This rule        is specially concerned with the presentation of  credentials (“pouvoirs”). Some members took  the view that credentials must come from the  Minister of Foreign Affairs, while others again  thought it should be the Head of the State, others,  the Minister for Education or some other Cabinet  Minister and others a Minister Plenipotentiary.  After prolonged discussion, the Committee judged  it best to leave the rule in its original form until  such time as the Secretariat can supply us, for the  next Session, with the data requisite for its refram-  ing; thus the Conference will be able to legislate  on the credentials of delegations in the light of  fuller knowledge.  Lastly, we have added to the draft Rules of Pro-  cedure a number of new paragraphs, in particular  to Rules 32, 33 and 34. We have also added a  Rule 104 on suspension; this you will find at  the end of the paper. These articles were the sub-  ject of exhaustive consideration by the Committee,  which spared neither pains nor time. No good  purpose would be served on this occasioh by dis-  cussing each rule individually or taking a separate  vote on each several section. If you are prepared  to trust your Committee, which devoted much time  and trouble to the matter, then vote on the draft  as a whole; this is in fact the procedure followed  in similar cases in national parliaments. If anyone  wishes to comment on a particular article, I am  ready to answer with the assistance of our dis-  tinguished Chairman, Dr. Nielsen, who is still  present.  Before concluding, it remains for me to thank  the Chairman and members of the Committee for  their valuable help in considering these Rules of  Procedure.
