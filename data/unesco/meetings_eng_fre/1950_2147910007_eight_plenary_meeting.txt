[5] This is the first opportunity which the  Organization has had since the Conference of  Beirut to take stock of the development of Unesco,  because it will be recalled that the Conference in  Paris last September was restricted, as far as  possible, to the transaction of urgent, essential  business, and did not afford so large an opportun-  ity for a complete examination of the stage we  have reached and the directions in which we are  likely to move. From that point of view, the  Australian Government and the Australian dele-  gation consider it very important that we should  examine carefully and with due reflection on this  occasion the developments of the last couple of  vears and the particular problems confronting  Unesco at the present time. Looking back over  this period, the first thought that we have in our  mind is to congratulate our Director-General upon  the way in which he has directed the work of the  Secretariat and inspired the activities of the whole  Organization throughout this period. We are all  impressed by the fact that the annual Report  submitted to the Conference on this occasion is  extremely encouraging because of its solemn  record of achievement in so many different fields.  [6] We think that now, in 1950, it can truly be  said that the Organization is beginning to find its  way, to find the way that it should follow through  the complexities of the post-war world. We find  evidence of that in the four main points which  seem to be before this Conference in Florence.  Those four points are: first, that of concentrating  the programme on a few important practical  activities; second, the problem of reaching a firmer  conclusion regarding the best methods of work        for the Organization. The third is the emphasis  on the role of the Member States, which, I think,  has already been stated more clearly and by more  speakers, and been stated more firmly by our own  Director-General on this occasion, than ever  previously in discussions at Unesco. Finally, we  have the fourth major problem of this Conference,  the relation of our programme and our methods  to the problem of world peace. The Australian  delegation is convinced that in Unesco we are at  last facing up to these four major problems.  These problems have been with us from the very  beginning, but it is only now that they are emerg-  ing as the four great problems of Unesco, the four  great problems that we have to determine and for  which we must find solutions as far as possible  at the present Conference. These major problems,  I say, are now emerging very clearly, but we feel  that it is none too soon that they should emerge  clearly. We have been for too long caught, as it  were, within a vicious circle of programme pre-  paration and discussion, and I think many of us  have shared the feeling that it was not possible  for the Organization to go on with conference after  conference at which we debated at great length  all the details of a complex and extensive pro-  gramme. We did seem to have got ourselves into  a sort of vicious circle in which there seemed to  be no way out from this continual discussion of  the details of a programme of work for our Secre-  tariat.  [7] We have also struggled with the burden of  the documentation to which reference has been  made by some others who have already spoken.  The stage has been reached in this continual elab-  oration and discussion of the programme in which  the documentation produced was far beyond the  capacities of any delegation to absorb, and we  have been running the risk of holding annual con-  ferences at which matter after matter would be  decided by vote, when perhaps not more than  two or three of those who voted had had the time  and the energy to master the complexity of the  subjects which are dealt with in the many docu-  ments put before us. We have been told that there  has been danger of a breakdown in the Secretariat  from the point of view of the pressure of work  and the burden upon the health of its members.  We have ourselves feared a situation in which the  General Conference might become increasingly  ineffectual just because of the physical impossibil-  ity of dealing in a serious way with the mass of  material put before us. One of the difficulties is,  of course, that the delegates who come to the  Conference do not have the rest of the year to  prepare for it. Most of us leave other occupations  which also make great demands on our time and  energy, and then we are thrown, sometimes at  short notice, into a Unesco Conference with piles  of documents which have arrived in the preceding  fortnight and continue to arrive in the early days  of the Conference, and it is extremely difficult to  feel that we really are taking decisions on the basis  of a careful study of all the problems involved.  We have felt for some time that it was necessary  to break out of this vicious circle, if the Organiza-  tion, as an organization, was going to fulfil the  purposes laid down for it when it was founded.  But now, as I said, it seems to me that the prob-  lems on this occasion are being very clearly posed,  very largely owing to the clarity and fervour of  our Director-General, but also owing to a gradual  realization by numerous delegations, that the  Organization must find solutions to the great prob-  lems that are facing it and to which 1 referred a  moment ago.  [8] The Organization is now four years old, and  it was not perhaps surprising that an organization  with so vast a purpose, one covering the whole  field of education, science and culture, and bound  to work for co-operation between the nations in  these fields and to contribute to peace, should in its  arly years find some difficulty in having a clear  conception of the direction in which it should go  and even in deciding which were the main prob-  lems to which it should address itself. But  now, after four years, the friends of this Organiza-  tion in Australia, the Australian Government, the  Australian Co-operating Bodies, are convinced  that a time has come when our great need may  well prove to be the need for stability, stability in  our programme, stability in our Organization and  even stability in the budgetary provisions of the  Organization.  [9] Now, I propose to say just a little about the  four problems that I mentioned and to give you  some of the observations that have been made in  Australia, in Government circles, in our national  Co-operating Bodies and throughout the public,  regarding these problems facing Unesco. First of  all, the problem which we all agree is a very  serious one indeed, that of concentrating our  unduly diffuse programme upon a limited series  of activities, the value of each of which must be  clearly understood and recognized by the mem-  bers of the Organization. In this connexion we  have before this Conference resolutions which  were adopted by the General Assembly of the  United Nations, concerning the programmes of  the Specialized Agencies. These resolutions have  been made the subject of a special report by the  Executive Board and at a later stage in the Con-  ference it will be our duty to examine the views  expressed by the United Nations and addressed by  its General Assembly to all the Specialized  Agencies. The general direction of those resolu-  tions is to impress upon us the need at this time            for each of the Specialized Agencies—and that  means for Unesco as well—to make sure that we  are concentrating our efforts on those activities,  preferably on a restricted number of activities,  which will make the greatest contribution to the  common objectives of the whole United Nations  system at this time. The whole problem, of  course, is one of choice, and if we are going to  concentrate on a few great enterprises, there are  many other enterprises that will just have to be  pushed aside. That is one of the laws of life,  that when emphasis is to be given to important  matters, less important matters must take a second  place. So far as we are concerned in Australia,  we would give priority—and throughout the Con-  ference this will be the policy that we shall follow  —to projects designed to promote co-operation  and mutual assistance in the development of  education and science, particularly in the less  developed countries.     [10] In the early stages of the post-war period,  we welcomed the very great emphasis placed on  the reconstruction of war-devastated areas. We  are convinced that the main emphasis over the  next few years must be upon the assistance of the  stronger countries for the development of the  educational systems and the scienfitic resources  of the less developed countries, and for our part  we especially hope that considerable attention and  assistance will be given to the needs of the great  neighbouring countries in South East Asia, in  which Australia is taking a very great interest. If  we are going to give emphasis to the practical  work of building up and strengthening the less  developed parts of the world, the inevitable con-  sequence will be that some, perhaps many, of our  more abstract studies, and some of our broader  cultural activities, may have to be postponed and  some of those that are at present being undertaken  may have to be reduced in scope and even, for the  time being, held over. That is the first observa-  tion I wish to make from the Australian point of  view, following the discussions that have taken  place in Australia about the work of Unesco. We  would hope that, in considering the concentration  of the programme, we should give special  emphasis to the problem of assisting the devel-  opment in a practical way of the countries which,  for various reasons, are less advanced in their  educational systems and in their scientific organ-
1zation.  [11] The second problem is the problem of  methods of work. And here we welcome the dis-  cussion which it is proposed to hold regarding a  list of methods of work which has been drawn up  by the Executive Board. Our Constitution en-  visaged that one of the main activities of Unesco  would be to recommend to Member States inter-  national conventions and recommendations of a  technical nature, which it was hoped would assist  governments to develop their cultural relations  with other countries and would assist Member  States and the peoples of Member States to make  progress within the fields of education, science  and culture through international co-operation.  We understand that to this Conference will be  submitted draft rules of procedure to cover the  procedure that should be applied in the future to  the consideration of conventions and recommenda-  tions. All this is virtually a new subject, a new  field of work to which no previous session of the  General Conference has given any serious atten-  tion, and while we welcome the preparation which  has been undertaken of a series of draft regulations  to cover the procedures, we feel in Australia that  there 1s a more urgent task still, which must be  undertaken in the course of the Programme Com-  mission's discussions of the methods of work of  the Organization. That task is the following: we  should ask ourselves what are the subjects, what  are the matters which are ripe at the present time  for some degree of international regulation in the  fields of education, science and culture. If the  answer is that there 1s none, well then, of course,  there is no point in drawing up any conventions  or series of recommendations. If the answer is  that there are some, then we must be led on im-  mediately to say which are the important ones.  We have, it is true, considered once or twice pro-  posals made by individual delegations for a con-  vention or some recommendations on a particular  subject, but no attempt has been made by the  General Conference to survey the whole field and  to make up our minds what are the subjects on  which we, as an Organization, wish to attempt  some degree of international regulation through  the machinery of regulations and recommenda-  tions. And that question seems to us one of the  important questions to which this Conference  should address itself. We also welcome the stud-  les that have been made of cultural agreements.  We find there is a document submitted to the Con-  ference which reviews the many cultural bilateral  agreements which are in existence between dif-  ferent Member States. We have noted that some  Member States have made a great deal of use of  this form of international co-operation: others  again have not, and we are inclined to the view  that a careful study of the use that has been made  of cultural agreements may point the way to others  of us, and indicate a new form of co-operation  that may well yield useful results.  [12] I also wish to make an observation, still  under this general heading of the methods of  work, regarding the use of expert committees.  And here my remarks are of a somewhat critical    “H N  nature, based not on any failure to recognize the  need which Unesco has for expert advice in various  fields, but upon the experience which Australian  experts have had in attending certain meetings  called by Unesco. We feel that there is room for  very great improvement in the use of this particu-  lar method of work by Unesco. All too often in  the experience of Australian experts who have  come to Paris, the meeting has been insufficiently  prepared in advance and a great deal of their very  short stay in Paris has been devoted to preliminary  work which should have been done in advance by  the Secretariat. Secondly, we know that the meet-  ings of experts are kept to as short a period as  possible in the interests of financial economy.  But we have felt very often that it is false economy  to bring an expert from the other side of the  world at considerable expense, involving con-  siderable personal fatigue, because travel across  these immense distances, although it may be rapid,  is still a fatigue to the human body and spirit.  We feel that it has often been false economy to  bring experts from distant countries for a meeting  that lasted three or four days, or perhaps only a  little longer, and that much better results would  often be obtained if fewer meetings were held but  if they were of sufficient duration to enable full  advantage to be obtained from the views of the  experts brought to them. 1 think that is a point  in which there will be agreement among delega-  tions from other distant countries, not always  realized by people who are accustomed to go from  one capital to another in the space of a few hours.  It is not always realized just what is involved in  bringing experts from the other side of the world  to work on a particular problem. Furthermore,  we have been seriously concerned by a tendency  in some cases, after the experts have been brought  from great distances and have worked together in  Paris—as they thought, very well—by an apparent  disregard of the views and findings that they have  put down in their reports. In particular, we have  been concerned at the lack of attention paid to the  findings of some of the experts who have been  brought together by the Department of Natural  Sciences. 1 have been asked to refer particularly  to the fact that a meeting held almost a year ago  on scientific abstracts produced a report which has  not yet, so far as we know, been made available,  and that the course of programme activities fol-  lowing that report ran counter to the proposals to  which the experts attached so much importance.  [ am asked also to say, in connexion with studies  of the arid zone, that the proposals brought before  the Conference at this time are entirely different  from the line advocated by the meeting of experts  in Paris, which gave very definite advice as to how  this problem should be approached. [13] In Australia also there has been some criti-  cism of the amount of travel by Unesco’s own  experts. We have had visits to Australia of Unesco  officials which have been a very great help in  stimulating interest in the work of Unesco. These are all points to which attention  must be given, and in our view it is our duty to  draw attention to these weaknesses, because unless  we have a frank exchange of views between our  Member States and those concerned with the day-  to-day work of the Organization, it will be dif-  ficult to remedy the weaknesses to which reference  is made.  [14] The third major point to which I wish to  refer is the problem of finding the proper role in  all our work for Member States and the National  Commissions and national Co-operating Bodies.  The Constitution of Unesco speaks a great deal  about the organization of co-operation among the  nations, which, to our way of thinking, implies  that the task of Unesco is very largely to encourage  the flow of information, the movement of people  as between Member States, and also to encourage  Member States to work together. That is not the  same thing as undertaking in some centralized  secretariat a number of specific projects. We  have felt in Australia that there has sometimes  been a tendency to look upon the National Com-  missions as a mere auxiliary of the Secretariat.  We have tried in our own country to make our  national Co-operating Bodies centres of effective  work in which they themselves co-operate with  similar centres in other countries, and to find for  our national Co-operating Bodies a field of work  which certainly includes obtaining answers to  questionnaires and helping the Secretariat, but  which also includes the conduct of relations with  groups in other countries who desire to co-operate           with us in the cultural field in Australia. I might  just mention in passing the twenty or so Unesco  fellows financed by the Australian Government  who are at present studying in Australia, who  have come from various countries, mostly from  South East Asia, and the fact that we expect to  welcome thirty more fellows, financed again by  the Australian Government under the general  auspices of Unesco, in the coming year.  [15] We feel that this problem of working out  the type of activities which Member States can  organize on their own soil, the type of co-operation  that Member States can undertake with other  Member States, is one of the prime problems of  the Organization at this time. This new role of  Member States, which, we think, is just coming  into the limelight and to which more and more  attention must be given, places new duties upon  our Secretariat and undoubtedly affects the type  of work which the Secretariat will be called upon  to do. We welcome in Australia the remarks of  our Director-General in his Report in relation to  the future work of the Secretariat. The Director-  General has said that this work in future should  be confined in the main to documentation, the  exchange of information, and enquiries, those  activities being undertaken only as preparation for  definite constructive action. And inevitably, as  the role of the Member States in the work of  Unesco develops and expands, the function and the  duties of the Secretariat will be modified in the  direction indicated by the Director-General.  [16] The fourth of the great problems facing  this Conference is the relation of all our work to  the problem of peace. And here I would merely  say very briefly that we in Australia feel that  Unesco will make its greatest contribution if it  concentrates upon the problems that fall clearly  within its own field. We know that education is  a slow process, and ours is primarily an educa-  tional organization. Political issues should go in  the main to the United Nations. We feel that the  greatest contribution this Organization can make  to the maintenance of peace is to work at the prob-  lem of securing the participation of as many  Member States as possible in the gradual formation  of those habits of mind which will assist us to  maintain peace throughout the world. We would  stress the duties of the Member States in this  regard, and the hope that in the programme of  Unesco we shall find them taking the leading part  in certain of the activities to which attention has  been drawn again and again. When we speak  about education for international understanding,  when we speak about teaching about the United  Nations, and about educating the world regarding  the Declaration of Human Rights, nothing very  much can be done in any of those fields unless it
145  is done by the Member States. We would not  believe in Australia, that the best way of spread-  ing throughout the world an understanding of  the principles underlined in the Universal Declar-  ation of Human Rights was to increase greatly  the part of Unesco’s budget to be spent upon dis-  seminating the Declaration. But we would say that  the Declaration will never become a reality unless  the Member States are prepared to devote time,  attention and money to ensuring that teaching  and understanding the Declaration of Human  Rights comes to be part of the educational systems  of their respective countries.  [17] Over the past four years I think we have  found that the problem of organizing collab-  oration among the nations is perhaps more  difficult than it appeared in 1945. We have  come to realize perhaps more clearly than  before the great difficulties in our way aris-  ing from the diversity of our different cultures,  and sometimes I know our Secretariat has been  discouraged, sometimes we have all been  discouraged, by the difficulties arising from the  fact that in different parts of the world the way  of approach to cultural problems and educational  matters and to international dealings is so dif-  ferent. But as we have realized the difficulties, I  think we have come to realize just how much we  gain by recognizing and insisting upon the main-  tenance of our diversity. I might, in this con-  nexion, quote the words of the great modern phi-  losopher, Whitehead, which seem to me to under-  lie all the work of Unesco:  A diversification among human communities is essen-  tial for the provision of the incentive and material  for the Odyssey of the human spirit. Other nations  of different habits are not enemies, they are godsends.  Men require of their neighbours something sufficiently  akin to be understood; something sufficiently diffe-  rent to provoke attention, and something great  enough to command admiration.  This diversity which appears to put obstacles in  the way of organizing co-operation among the na-  tions will remain at the same time one of our  most precious possessions.  [18] But we have recognized also in the past  four years that there are other difficulties in the  way of collaboration between the nations. One  of the greatest obstacles is the existence of great  inequality in the wealth and power of the States  members of this Organization. It is not altogether  easy to organize co-operation between persons or  groups that are of unequal strength and, although  Unesco is required by the Constitution to respect  the diversity of cultures, we must not be surprised  if we are confronted by demands that we should  work together to iron out some of the very great           |  |  w  B  a  La  La ,  i  a  (¥  =  i    inequalities between nations which are an  outstanding feature of the world today. And it  is from this point of view also that we feel that  an increasing emphasis in the work of Unesco  must be given to mutual assistance, in which the  strong will aid the less strong and in which the  wealthier nations will come to the aid, in the  fields of education, science and culture, of the  less wealthy nations, which have such terrible  problems to overcome before they can afford the  very minimum of education, science and culture  for their peoples. Now with this thought in my  mind, just shortly before 1 left Paris, as I was one  day walking in the Ile de la Cité, I noticed the  monument which was erected a considerable time  ago to a Frenchman, Théophraste Renaudot, the  founder of one of the earliest newspapers and also  one of the founders of one of the earliest forms of  social services, namely free medical consultations  for poor people. My attention was drawn to this  particular monument as one of the many monu-  ments in Paris which had suffered during the  occupation by the enemy, and as I looked at it and  read the words that were written upon it, it seemed  to me that here again was one of the many pre-  cursors of some of the main ideas of Unesco. On  one side of this monument you read the words—  | will give you the English version—:  Here in the rue de la Calandre, at the sign of the  Great Cock, was the office of Théophraste Renaudot,  where he founded the Gazette and instituted free  medical consultations for the poor.  Then on two other sides you find two striking  quotations from the words of Théophraste Renau-  dot. The one relates to his interest in the free  flow of information, the other to his interest in  helping the poor. In one we read:  I would only have one prayer to the princes and  foreign governments, not to lose their time uselessly  trying to prevent the passage across their frontiers of  my newspapers, since that is a type of merchandise  which we have never been able to stop moving  around the world and which shares the nature of  those torrents which grow stronger and broader  whenever they are resisted.  It seemed to me that those are observations which  certainly forecast our modern interest in the free  flow of information. Then when you turn round  and look at the other side of the monument you  find Renaudot’s words on the other subject:  It is necessary in every State that the wealthy come  to the aid of the poor, because the harmony of the  State comes to an end if one part is strengthened too  much and the others are inevitably weakened.  The statue which stood on top of the monument
146  to which I have been referring, disappeared dur-  ing the war, and like many others in Paris, has  never been found again, being made of bronze.  But we in Unesco, it seems to me, are not only  still bound to keep before our minds our ideals  in promoting the free flow of ideas which will  sweep across all frontiers, but we must give in-  creasing emphasis in our work to the mutual aid  and assistance by which the wealthier and more  technically advanced countries can come to the  aid of those who are labouring with very difficult  problems. At this moment, already 48 per  cent of our population is literate; and our A-B-C  campaign is proceeding so satisfactorily that we  feel confident that within ten or fifteen years  from now the last vestiges of illiteracy will be  eliminated. By 48 per cent I mean 48 per cent  of the persons over six years of age; and, by  literate 1 mean that they can read, whether in  Latin, Arabic, Javanese, Buginese, Balinese, or  any other characters in use in Indonesia.  [89] So we enter the ranks of Unesco, enthu-  siastic and confident, perhaps over-enthusiastic,  but not over-confident, for we fully realize that  many things remain to be done. It is very clear,  however, that, as a young nation fighting on  behalf of humanity and progress, we may be  expected also to be very critical of this world  after the second great war. Indonesia certainly  will do her utmost to promote all that is worthy  in Unesco, more especially as an instrument of  peace, but we also want to make it clear that we  will try to prevent it from becoming an instru-  ment used only for purposes which are not to the  advantage of peace and good understanding in  the world. But it is especially for technical  reasons that we intend to co-operate closely with  Unesco, in order to have the advice and aid of its  experts. At the same time we will always be  willing to give Unesco all the information and  all the help it wants for the correct and success-  ful implementation of its work. May 1 thank all  of you who are willing to recognize us as your  fellow delegates, especially those from our neigh-  bour States in South East Asia, Australia, Burma,  India, Pakistan and others. 1 do not want to take  up too much of your time, but let me again, on  behalf of my country and my people, express my       sincere gratitude and reiterate the promise of full  and intense co-operation from our side. [2] Dr. Hermann ZEISSL (Austria) — Mr. Pre-  sident, fellow-delegates, ladies and gentlemen, at  the beginning of my speech I must confess that  I cannot wholly suppress a feeling of doubt and  embarrassment. This is the third day of an  extensive debate, and hecatombs of speakers—if  I may use this classic term with all due respect  to my colleagues—have fired all kinds of inspired  and convincing arguments at you. After these  admirable and eloquent speeches, what can pos-  sibly be left for me to say? How, for instance,  could I improve upon the glowing tribute which  has rightly been paid on behalf of all of us to  this country, and to this city and to its over-  whelming hospitality? Nevertheless, our delega-  tion feels from the bottom of its heart that the  intimate neighbourhood and friendship which  links our two peoples needs expression. Perhaps  some of you remember the famous passage in the     Gloria when Mass is sung, the passage which  has inspired many great composers, where thanks  are given to God for all his glory. Let me say  to our Italian friends, on behalf of our delega-  tion, nay, on behalf of the Austrian people:  Thank you, thank you for the glory that is Italy,  thank you for the glory that is Florence. No  people whose life-blood is imbued with the great  Christian universalistic and humanistic tradition  of the West, can and will ever forget the glorious  contribution made to it from this peninsula and  in this city. The fair face of Italy has become  more dear to us because of the scars which have  been inflicted on it, and this feeling goes deeper  with us because we are also in this respect  fellow-sufferers.
