[1] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] We shall resume the discussion of the  Director-General’s report. 1 call upon the repre-  sentative of Yugoslavia.  [2] Mr. Vladislav RIBNIKAR (Yugoslavia) —  | Translation from the French] Mr. President,  delegates, ladies and gentlemen, the Director-  General’s remarkable report is full of far-reaching  and constructive suggestions which may open up  new possibilities for Unesco’s activities and provide  fruitful matter for our discussions. In connexion  with this report, I may perhaps make a few  comments on Unesco’s past activities and future  opportunities.  [3] But I should like first to outline briefly the  reasons why Yugoslavia has waited until now  before becoming a Member of Unesco. In Novem-  ber 1945, at the Conference in London, Yugosla-  via’s representatives, among 41 countries belonging  to the United Nations, signed the Constitution of  Unesco. The peoples of Yugoslavia were bound  to welcome the establishment of an organization  designed, in the words of the Constitution, to  advance  through the educational, scientific and cultural  relations of the peoples of the world, the objectives of  international peace and of the common welfare of  mankind for which the United Nations Organization  was established and which its Charter proclaims.  [4] Although Yugoslavia signed this Constitution,  she did not become a Member of the Organization.  There were good reasons for this, and the Yugoslav  delegation set them forth explicitly at the First  Session of the General Conference in Paris in its  criticism of the first programme of work for  Unesco which was then under discussion. At  that time, the Yugoslav delegation pointed out that  the programme fell far short of the fundamental  aims clearly set forth in Unesco’s Constitution.  It was obvious that the sponsors of the programme  intended to make Unesco a sort of world mis-  sionary organization responsible for propagating a  particular culture and ideology or even a partic-  ular philosophy among the peoples, to bring about  a ‘‘spiritual renaissance of mankind”. The Yugo-  slav delegation protested against this tendency  towards centralization and uniformity in cultural  affairs and opposed the attempt to formulate an  official international philosophy. The delegation  believed that fruitful co-operation among the  nations was dependent upon free cultural develop-  ment and competition, upon the preservation of  a diversity of opinions and points of view regarding  educational, scientific and cultural matters. The  delegation also protested strongly against the view  that Unesco was not required to play an active  part in the struggle for peace because—as was  then suggested—its work in connexion with educa-  tion, science and culture was enough to secure  better understanding between the peoples and  therefore made a contribution, albeit indirect, to  the maintenance of peace. Since ‘wars begin in  the minds of men” and “it is in the minds of  men that the defences of peace must be con-  structed”, it was held to be enough to spread the  idea of peace. As we know, that concept was  embodied in the Preamble to the Constitution of  Inesco. You may remember that a few enthu-  siastic champions of Unesco then asserted in all  seriousness that to work for the propagation of  education, science and culture was enough to  ensure that wars would automatically be brought  to an end. The Yugoslav delegation protested  against these ideas and emphasized the need for  Unesco to take an active part in the endeavour to  secure peace, in the endeavour to prevent any act  likely to give rise to unrest and hatred among the               peoples, to combat the war propaganda which, in  the most diverse forms, finds its way into educa-  tion, science and culture. Unesco’s programme  in fact made no provision for active measures for  the defence of peace.  [5] These were the main reasons for which my  country then decided not to become a Member  of Unesco. Nevertheless, from 1946 onwards, we  have attended sessions of the General Conference  in the capacity of observers and have kept in  close touch with Unesco’s work and progress,  playing our part in many international enterprises,  whether or not sponsored by Unesco, designed  to foster international co-operation and to promote  contact and understanding between the peoples.  As we could not approve Unesco’s tendency to-  wards centralization and its deliberate neglect of  the urgent problems of peace, we endeavoured in  the first place to establish close co-operation, in  cultural and other matters, with the U.S.S.R. and  the popular democracies. We believed that, by  co-operating with socialist and socialistically  inclined countries, we should safeguard our  national and cultural independence. We thought  that co-operation, of whatever sort, between social-  ist countries, could be based only on the principle  of equality and mutual assistance. We regarded  this co-operation as the best guarantee of the free  development of our national culture and the safe-  guarding of our independence. For that reason,  we fostered cultural contacts with the countries of  Fastern Europe. Experience soon showed us,  however, that in the Fast, as elsewhere, there  was a policy hostile to the principles of equality  between large and small nations, a policy which  threatens the independence of peoples and the  untrammelled development of their culture.  Experience taught us that the tendency of large  countries to exploit and subjugate small ones 1s,  in some circumstances, quite unrelated to the  social order established in those countries, and that  differing and contradictory ‘‘ideological reasons”  are only empty words disguising policies which are  essentially the same.  [6] The peoples of Yugoslavia are small but they  are firmly determined to safeguard their indepen-  dence. By their refusal to accept a status of  inequality and their firm insistence that, in the  relations between the peoples, the principle of  equality should be respected, they have come into  conflict with all those who hold that the world  consists of ‘governing nations’ and dependent  nations, and that the latter can take no active  part in the moulding either of their own destiny  or of that of humanity as a whole. This policy  followed by certain great nations with regard to  smaller or weaker countries resulted in the  subjugation of some countries by others, the  formation of strategic blocs, and the bargaining  which goes on between the great nations for the  division of the world into spheres of influence.  This policy is at the root of the present state of  tension in the world.  [7] This situation is not due to ideological  differences, as the propagandists would have us  believe. The ideological differences which are the  outcome of social differences do not, in them-  selves, entail the threat of war, any more than  similarity of ideological views alone 1s a guarantee  of equality in relations between countries. Si-  milarity in ideologies excludes neither antagonism  nor conflicts. We can see that unity of views 1s  non-existent either between socialist countries or  between capitalist countries, and that within those  groups conflicts are not impossible. Yugoslavia's  is a case in point. Wars are not the result of  ideological differences; they are caused by viola-  tion of the principles of national independence  and equality of rights—principles proclaimed in  the United Nations Charter but constantly  neglected and dishonoured.  [8] To fight for the cause of peace 1s to fight  for freedom and equality between the nations, to  combat the attitude conducive to war, which  people try to tell us is inevitable because of  ideological conflicts. To uphold the cause of  peace is to strive for the development and expan-  sion of co-operation between the peoples on the  basis of equality, irrespective of forms of social  organization and of ideological differences. The  United Nations and their Specialized Agencies are  the instruments best calculated to secure the suc-  cess of endeavours to bring about such co-  operation.  [9] In the present state of the world, it is our  duty to neglect no possible opportunity for inter-  national co-operation. Yugoslavia is convinced  that, today, her collaboration with Unesco may  really advance the common cause of peace and  fruitful co-operation among the nations and benefit  the Yugoslav peoples themselves. Faithful as ever  to her policy of peace, Yugoslavia will assist any  of Unesco’s activities which are designed to  strengthen international co-operation in mutual  respect for the sovereignty and independence of all  the nations, based on equal rights for all the  peoples, great and small, and the policy of non-  interference in the affairs of other countries.  Yugoslavia will be found particularly ready to  co-operate in any work to combat agressive ten-  dencies or hegemony, discriminatory measures and  incitement to war, from whatever source and in  whatever form they may appear.  [10] The Yugoslav delegation is convinced that  Unesco’s peace policy must be a constructive and  forward-looking policy based on a true appreciation      of facts. Unesco’s activities must make inter-  national co-operation easier instead of more com-  plicated. The Yugoslav delegation therefore con-  siders that support of the Kuomintang delegation is  not only an injustice but also a very serious  obstacle to Unesco’s own work and to the advance-  ment of peace. We cannot overlook the fact that  the Kuomintang government does not represent the  Chinese people and is in fact opposed by the  whole nation. In our opinion, the participation  of the peoples in the cause of peace and culture  is bound to be incomplete whilst the largest nation  in the world and one of the most ancient civil-  izations remains absent.  [11] One of the most effective means of promot-  ing peace is to combat lies with truth. At the  present time, aggression is being plotted under the  cloak of pacifism, and on every hand falsehood 1s  used to sow hatred and distrust among the  peoples. The only means of frustrating these  plots is truth, and this must be the weapon  wielded by Unesco. My country is well qualified  to speak on this subject. Recently, some Western  countries set on foot a campaign against us inspired  by a single purpose—to conceal and give specious  cover to the aggressive intent of its promoters.  On the other hand, the savage outcry which, for  the past two years, has been raised by the Soviet  Union and the countries of Eastern Europe against  the people of Yugoslavia, is also a grave threat to  peace. The experience and example of the Yugo-  slav people in this way may be most helpful in  Unesco’s endeavour to safeguard and consolidate  peace.  [12] The Yugoslav delegation will try to con-  tribute to the development of that true inter-  national co-operation in cultural affairs which 1t  is Unesco’s fundamental purpose to secure. This  co-operation is now more than ever necessary,  and we are convinced that Unesco can do much  to further it. We therefore hope that the Organ-  ization will make an even greater endeavour,  revising its programme and adapting it to the  new needs and requirements of the present day,  so that it may be fully consonant with Unesco’s  fundamental aims and, once implemented, may  fulfil the hopes the nations placed in Unesco at  the time of its establishment.  [13] In principle, we agree with the desire  shown by many delegations to cut down the  present programme of Unesco, limiting it to  matters of immediate interest and value to all  the peoples of the world and the general cause  of peace. We appreciate the efforts the Executive  Board and the Director-General have made to this  end, but we feel that what has so far been done  can be regarded only as a first step. There are  great gaps in the programme, but it is still too
72  wide; some of Unesco’s activities overstep the  limits laid down for the Organization and  encroach upon the field of other agencies and  organizations of the United Nations. Owing to  the wide scope of the programme, which is beyond  Unesco’s capacity, the results achieved by the  Organization’s work are inadequate and, as the  Director-General recently said, there is a danger  that its work may seem purely symbolical.  Unesco must therefore adapt its programme to its  real resources and concentrate on essentials.  [14] It also seems to us that the programme still  smacks of an outmoded idea of Unesco as a sort  of higher academy designed to deal mainly, with  the help of its scientific institutions, with the study  of the great philosophical, psychological, moral,  sociological, political and other problems of the  present day, considering those problems—and even  sometimes science and culture in general—as  things-in-themselves, apart from the economic and  social forces and relationships which determine  their nature. It is not essential that Unesco  should be a focus of “scientific thought’; it  should chiefly be a meeting place where all the  achievements of science and culture are to be  found, where differing opinions are exchanged,  where scientific and cultural acquisitions are  passed from one to another, where arrangements  are made for mutual assistance in the field of  education, science and culture, and where efforts  are made to bring the peoples together in an  atmosphere of peace and international co-  operation. If Unesco really wishes to fulfil the  hopes the nations have placed in the Organization,  instead of devoting so much effort to scientific  study, which generally has no relation to Unesco’s  fundamental purposes, it must pay more heed to  the settlement of specific questions directly con-  cerned with the development of that true inter-  national co-operation on which peace must be  founded. There must be fewer surveys of ideo-  logical views and conflicts, fewer studies of  “social tensions” (usually approached in the light  of abstract hypotheses), and more endeavours to  lay bare and confound the attempts of those who  wish to use instruments designed for the exten-  sion of education, science and culture to propagate  a belief in the inevitability of war and to foster  hatred among the peoples. Life demonstrates that  there can be no world-wide centralization in cul-  tural affairs, and that no “universal” philosophy  can be imposed on anyone. We believe that the  realization of this fact has helped, to some extent,  to modify the original conception of Unesco's  “world mission’, and that the influence of the  adherents of cultural centralization is no longer  predominant in the Organization. Resolutions  recently formulated by Unesco have often men-         tioned the need to assist the free development of  varied cultures and have contained more and  more allusions to the benefit to be derived from  the inter-action of different civilizations. It 1s  obvious that Unesco’s “world mission’ consists in  providing general assistance for this mutual  enrichment of the various peoples of the world.  [15] Now more than ever, when differences in  views and variations in culture are exploited  to the full by propaganda, with the object of  dividing the world into hostile camps, it must  be Unesco’s duty to combat such propaganda and  to prove, by example, that, however much their  characteristics may differ, the nations are able to  live together in peace and to develop fruitful co-  operation, on an equal footing, in the interests  of the cultural advance of humanity. In the  effort for the defence of peace, Unesco can, in  its own field, do extremely useful work. Its  primary task should be to oppose any violation  of the principles of democracy wherever the risk  of this appears in international relations In con-  nexion with education, science and culture—  wherever a tendency may be displayed to smother  the culture of any people and to impose 1deo-  logical views. Unesco may go on defending these  principles of democracy by the means it has  already used to some extent, but should redouble  its efforts to develop peaceful co-operation between  the nations by facilitating international under-  standing, organizing a more extensive interchange  of cultural achievements and providing as many  opportunities as possible for the comparison of  differing opinions on education, science and  culture. Unesco might also take more active  measures to ensure that everywhere the principles  of democracy are observed in all civilizations, and  to safeguard freedom in scientific and artistic  creation, without which there can be no advance  in culture. The Organization must combat the  ominous tendencies to cultural isolationism no  less than aggressive cultural nationalism. And,  above all, Unesco must resolutely oppose war  propaganda when it finds its way into education,  science and culture. Unesco must make still  greater efforts to find a basis for general cultural  co-operation and to facilitate discussion between  the two opposing schools of thought in an effort  to find immediate practical solutions to secure  peace.  [16] In order to spur on the latent forces of  peace, the Yugoslav delegation, following out the  Executive Board’s suggestions, proposes the follow-  ing resolution as a first practical measure:  ConsipErING that the activities of Unesco set forth in  the Draft Programme of the Organization are capable  of making a valuable contribution to the develop-  ment of the intellectual and moral solidarity of
73  mankind and, in this way, of serving the cause of  peace and general progress:  CONSIDERING that these activities can bear their full  fruits only in the comparatively distant future, as the  Draft Programme contains no provisions for measures  by which the factors upon which peace today depends  may be directly and immediately influenced;  AND CONSIDERING that it is necessary that Unesco  should co-operate actively in the United Nations’ work  for peace in the present as well as in the future, thus  endorsing the observations set forth by the Executive  Board at its Twentieth Session;  THE GENERAL CONFERENCE,  OBSERvING that the most serious obstacle to effective  international co-operation in connexion with educa-  tion, science and culture is the artificial maintenance  of a war psychosis;  AND BEING CONVINCED that, in order to combat this  psychosis and to develop international co-operation, it  would be most useful for representative intellectuals,  of different schools of thought and from every quarter  of the world, to meet to discuss what work Unesco  might do, and by what means, for the immediate pro-  motion of peace;
1. Appears to all organizations and all people  engaged in educational, scientific and cultural work  to take an active part in the consolidation of inter-  national peace, in order to lay a lasting foundation for  intellectual co-operation between the peoples;
2. InsTRUCTs the Director-General, with the aid of  the Secretariat, to make the necessary arrangements  to convene a Congress on Intellectual Co-operation  before the end of 1950, the duty of this Congress being  to consider possibilities of action by Unesco as part of  the Organization’s normal operations, and its purpose  to strengthen international co-operation, to strive to  reduce the danger of a war psychosis and the belief in  the inevitability of armed conflict between countries  with different social systems, and to suggest. in the  form of recommendations, practical measures which it  would be possible to take immediately;
3. Recommenps that, as soon as possible, the  Director-General issue invitations to all national pro-  fessional associations of scientists, teachers, writers,  artists, musicians, scenario-writers and journalists in  all Member States of the United Nations, and to pro-  minent people who have distinguished themselves in  the endeavour to promote peace;
4.  AUTHORIZEs the Director-General to fix the date  and meeting place of the above-mentioned Congress,  taking into account, firstly, its urgency and, secondly,  the technical and financial resources required by the  Secretariat for this purpose;
5. Expresses the desire that this Congress be held  in Europe.  That is the draft resolution that the Yugoslav  delegation has communicated to the Secretariat.  [17] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] I call upon the delegate of Denmark.    [18] Professor Jakob NIELSEN (Denmark)  Mr. President, I do not want in this general dis-  cussion to offer any considerations of general  character. I propose only to make a few points  which deal with what may be a question of  methods, but which, even if they seem small,  have a bearing on Unesco’s activity at the present  turning-point.  [19] The Danish delegation welcomes the setting  up of a basic programme and the arranging for  its implementation by fixing during each General  Conference the individual steps to be taken in the  interval until the next Conference. This change  is the logical consequence of the fact that Unesco  has now passed the experimental stage and that  we know better than we did in 1945 both what  we are trying to do and the methods by which  we want to achieve it. This arrangement of the  Programme carries with it, in our view, the need  also to change the conditions of work of the Secre-  tariat by allowing longer periods of uninterrupted  action. In the initial stage it was quite natural  that there should be General Conferences each  vear, and that we should have to place a heavy  strain on the Secretariat by asking the staff to  carry out many investigations for these con-  ferences, whilst the Member States were trying  to agree on a more permanent course to follow.  Now it should be sufficient to hold General Con-  ferences every second year only. My delegation  had thought of raising this point and we are  pleased to see that the United States delegation  puts it before us in the form of a definite proposal.  It fits in with the fact that we have had to give  increased powers to the Executive Board, and that  we shall surely continue to do. It has also many  conveniences for Member States, but the main  reason for the change is that it provides better  working conditions for the Secretariat.  [20] So far as the question of methods 1s con-  cerned, we want to emphasize the following point.  It has often been stated explicitly that Unesco  will, as far as possible, work through existing  organizations. I am fully aware of the fact that  in many cases this or that organization may have  been slow to perform services for which Unesco  has asked, and that in such cases the Secretariat  may be tempted to do the job itself, which again  means more specialists on the staff. 1 think we  must take into account some inertia on the part  of the organizations concerned and realize the  fact that the Secretariat, however well staffed, can  never replace an organization which commands  the best specialists in the field in question. It is,  in our view, for good reasons that the words  “encourage”, “promote” and “stimulate” are so  often to be found in our resolutions. I think it  is a marvellous result that the United Nations
74  should have created this agency of Unesco in  order continuously to stir up the cultural organ-  izations of the world, should they be in danger  of falling short of their lofty task! Most of the  instruments of educational, scientific, and cultural  life have existed since olden days. What we need  is a nerve-centre linking them all together and  making them serve a common cause. I do not,  of course, deny that there are cases where Unesco  must do the job itself or must call experts to-  gether for information and advice. But, as a  general rule, we must rely on enlisting the col-  laboration of existing bodies of competent people  as the only means of obtaining a decisive impact  with a limited staff and limited resources. We  are glad to see from the Dierctor-General’s Report  that many non-governmental organizations have  done valuable work for Unesco, and we have been  pleased this morning to hear from the Chairman  of the Executive Board that the Board, as I  understand it, shares the view that we must use  the existing organizations. Many organizations of  high scientific and cultural character have, in  themselves, a far-reaching bearing on Unesco 's  aims, even if this is not explicitly evident from  the definition of their objective. In such cases  we must take care not to be too narrow In our  conditions for collaboration.  [21] Finally, let me mention another question  of method, that of the written communications  and publications of Unesco, which have often  called forth criticism. If, for instance, there has  been a gathering of people discussing some special  problem, why then publish verbatim reports?  Let the result of the meeting be published, to-  gether, when suitable, with a short statement of  the main points brought forward in the discus-  sion. Then we should get three pages instead of  thirty. In these five years I have had to read  piles of Unesco papers, and in many cases I have  wasted much of my time finding my way to the  real point. You may object that there 1s a cer-  tain tradition, the tradition, if I may call it so,  of the “whereases” and the “considerings”; 1  mean the tradition of painting in very broad  lines the background of certain questions. That  is a well-established tradition, but we may ques-  tion its wisdom. The purpose of a written com-  munication is not that everybody should be able  to find every detail in it, but that it should actual-  ly be read. And we all know by experience that  a paper finds readers if it is concise and goes  straight to the point. Most of the qualified per-  sons whom we want as readers are busy people  who have no time to waste.  [22] And now for the printed publications.  Some of these must be of a periodical character,  such as those issued by purely technical services,         for instance, the Libraries Bulletin, and we need  also one single periodical of a general character,  like the Courier, which is on the whole well  written. But all those publications which are  intended as propaganda in one form or another  should not be periodicals, but separate pamphlets.  The reason for issuing a new publication must be  that it has a new idea to bring out, not that a  new number is due in a series which cannot be  stopped. And such publications should not be  written by a staff member in special charge of  editing, but by members of the Programme Depart-  ments as a direct result of their work. Let con-  siderations of high quality be the guiding prin-  ciple in our publications policy, and let us then  print the really good publications in numbers suf-  ficient for wide distribution.  [23] But I do not wish to give you the impres-  sion that I am implying still more criticism of  the Secretariat. The Director-General is right in  saying, as he did this morning, with well-justified  emphasis, that the General Conferences have so  far mainly imposed still more duties on the  Secretariat without defining the corresponding  duties of the Member States. He is right in  emphasizing this fact at the moment when a more  permanent programme comes into being, and this  moment is a turning point at which Member  States should warmly confirm their faith in  Unesco. The points I have mentioned are meant  as a contribution to that feeling of responsibility  which all Member States must share. There 1s  the danger of Unesco’s trying to cover too vast an  area, dissipating its energy, and presenting a dif-  fuse picture. Even if we are aware of all the  problems of our time, we must be cautious In  selecting those which are our immediate concern.  If these are well defined and tackled vigorously,  there will soon be no further doubt as to the signifi-  cance of the cultural agency of the United Nations.  [24] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] 1 call upon the delegate of Canada.  [25] Mr. Jean DESY (Canada) — [Translation  from the French] Florence has had an eternal  attraction for pilgrims in search of beauty. For  centuries they have come from every corner of  the world to meditate amid the wonders of this  city. The United Nations Educational, Scientific  and Cultural Organization was bound, in its  turn, to succumb to its age-old magic. When the  founders of the city gave it its name, they per-  haps foresaw that one day it would be adorned,  as with a flowery garland, with the most wonder-  ful works created by human genius. City of  Spring and garden of the mind. Spring lives in  the immortal gardens where Botticelli’s nymphs  go floating by, flowers ornament the hat of  Donatello’s David and the embroidered mantles  of Angelico’s Virgins. And there are those other  flowers of humanism, heroism and asceticism,  whose perfume scents the air of Florence. Yet  at first there was nothing to mark out Florence  for such an extraordinarily rich development  among the rival cities growing to power in her  vicinity. The reason for her pre-eminence 1s  obviously not to be found in any superiority of  her technical resources, in the endowments of her  University or the organization of her schools.  Her secret probably lies in the atmosphere in  which, at one time, her most gifted sons found  full freedom for development and self-expression.  Within two hundred years, at the instance of  Cosimo the Elder and Lorenzo the Magnificent,  the splendid buildings which are the glory of  Florence were raised. Yet while these flowers  they had nurtured came to bloom, the Medicis  took care to impose no constraint or compulsion  upon the men of genius they gathered around  them. Thev were content to create the atmos-  phere in which masterpieces might be produced.  Art needs patronage, not direction.  [26] Unesco today has to choose the more useful  of these two roles. The discussion on which we  are now embarking will, I hope, induce the  Organization to concentrate its efforts on the  strictly limited field in which we believe it may  properly operate. May Unesco initiate a new  Renaissance, like those enlightened patrons who  made Florence one of the fairest blooms of civi-  lization. If it succeeds, it will have more than  fulfilled our brightest hopes. Culture, which  Burckhardt defines as the sum of naturally occur-  ring intellectual developments which make no  claim to compel universal acceptance, 1s an  indigenous growth requiring nothing more than  a suitable climate. It is the body of changes in  the moral, aesthetic and material conditions of  life brought about, in any given environment, by  the natural operation of the intellect. For that  reason, as Mr. Herbert Read says, it can never be  imposed “ready-made”. In our opinion, it is the  duty of external agents to facilitate such an  upward movement without attempting to guide it  into predetermined channels. Unesco may sow  the seeds of culture; it cannot purvey it.  [27] On behalf of the Canadian delegates to the  Fifth Session of the General Conference, I am  glad to welcome the observers from Germany and  Japan, whose advice will no doubt make it possible  to execute the work planned in those two coun-  tries more efficiently.  [28] I hope our meetings may guide Unesco  into paths better suited to the nature of the  Organization and its resources. Its primary duty  is to help the cultures of the different nations to            develop fruitfully, and later to open up channels  of communication by which the contributions each  can make to the progress of all may pass from one  to another. Everything extraneous to this already  extensive programme should be struck out. The  programme the Director-General has submitted  to us indicates a very definite desire to condense  and co-ordinate the Organization’s work. I take  this opportunity of specially congratulating  Mr. Torres Bodet, and of telling him how much  we appreciate and admire his exemplary devotion  to his duties and his excellent direction of Unesco.  He rightly places in the forefront the question of  the exchange of information between countries.  This promotion of contact and understanding 1s,  in fact, still the most important part of Unesco’s  work, and I am glad to see that it is still given  priority in every service. From this point of view,  [ consider that the projects concerned with the  exchange of persons and the development of the  social sciences in countries in which they have  hitherto been neglected, deserve to receive our  approval. Such measures merit our support, for  they weave bonds of friendship between the  peoples. If peace is to be firmly founded, the  first necessity is to lay the groundwork of inter-  national collaboration and intellectual under-  standing.  [29] The execution of other proposals might,  however, in our opinion, profitably be postponed.  We do not think it essential to publish mono-  graphs on free and compulsory education, to  issue popular articles to broadcasting organ-  izations, to produce film strips or pictures for the  use of lecturers, to trace the stages in the cons-  titutional development of the Specialized Agencies,  or to collect photographs and illustrations supplied  by scientific or technical societies. There 1s too  much urgent work awaiting our attention for us  to waste time on such enterprises.  [30] Finally, the Canadian delegation would be  glad if several projects were omitted. 1 wonder  indeed what advantage artists would derive from  having their rights set forth in a declaration  which, when all is said and done, would simply  be a more restricted version of the Universal  Declaration of Human Rights. The establishment  of an International Institute of the Press and  Information has so far won so little support that  the only possible course is, we believe, to abandon  it. The nations  may be likened to vessels anchored in a roadstead,  on which the gale of Unesco’s projects and acti-  vities strikes with such force that they drag their  anchors and are driven out to sea before the  crew has had time to embark. The danger can  be avoided by more efficient organization of the  programme. Thus it would be advisable not to  start on the execution of new projects when there  are similar incompleted projects still in hand;  even the drafting of others should only be under-  taken when the published reports show that real  results have been achieved in earlier undertakings  of the same kind.  [73] Unesco, I repeat, has reached a critical  point. It is easy enough to add new items to  the programme and any one of us could go on  contributing them indefinitely; we must however  call a halt to this process. The first time a child  is allowed into a garden riotous with growing  things and a blaze of varied colours, with leave to  pick all the flowers that he wants, he may well  hesitate where to begin; he has the choice of so  many glorious colours that he cannot make up  his mind. There is nothing abnormal about  such hesitation; it is merely the outward sign of  the child's happy surprise before prospects un-  hoped for and unforeseen. In the case of an  adult, hesitation may spring from quite different  causes: the psychopatic sufferer who goes to a  department store with the intention of buying a  particular article loses all power of willed action  the moment he sets foot inside the doors; he  feels overwhelmed by the variety of articles dis-  played before him and choice becomes impos-  sible. These two examples are both, of course,  extreme cases. Nevertheless, the same possibilities  and the same difficulties come into play to some  extent in the case of every man and every large-  scale organization. Briefly the task is to avoid  being swamped by the abundance of opportu-  nities.  [74] Today this applies with special force to  Unesco itself. Unless the general public comes  to look on Unesco as a living organization, much  of Unesco’s appeal will be lost. The question is  asked all too often what actual results can be set  off against so many ambitious projects. To some  extent the implied criticism is justified. It can  fairly be claimed that Unesco has had to feel its  way forward at the cost of many errors, but the  time has now come when it must increasingly  make a deliberate choice of the road it should  follow. Often the sole result of excellent research work

[76] If the programme could be planned on a  two-year basis, in accordance with the United  States’ proposal, then there would be still greater  need to make it at once more practical and more  consistent. There is a danger that Unesco’s  energies may be dissipated if it attempts to flash  too many opportunities at once before the world. An encouraging example  here is that of Unesco’s sociological investigations  in India, where the Government itself has under-  taken the preliminary research. The course  on which the Organization has embarked, ac-  cording to its published documents, is one which  inspires the Netherlands delegation with com-  plete confidence. Nevertheless, as I have tried to
1S      show, there must be pruning and we must get  results to avoid an excess of projects leading to  the paralysis of all practical work. To this end we must tackle the con-  crete problems facing every cultural centre, all of  which spring ultimately from a lack on the  human side, a lack which finally is of the spirit. [110] In conclusion, I would stress the desir-  ability, nay the necessity, for Unesco to under-  take a serious self-examination, not for the purpose  of doing public penance for errors it has not com-  mitted and making honourable amends, but in  order:
(1) To reconsider its programme and its finan-  cial and other resources, with a view to achieving  more surely the aims prescribed by its Consti-  tution—understanding and peace between all  peoples through the long and arduous process of  education, science and culture—with greater  regard for present-day problems, especially polit-  ical problems, which have changed materially  since Unesco was first established;
(2) To bring out the truth wrung from experi-  ence, namely that the Secretariat cannot, by its  own unaided efforts, perform the tremendous  task it has been set, and that Member States and  their National Commissions too must share the  responsibility for any failures and shortcomings;
(3) To stress the need for resorting to the pro-  cedure of international conventions which would  be binding upon the signatory States;
(4) To institute, alongside but apart from the  regular programme and budget, an immediate and  direct campaign for peace, such a campaign to be  carried out with the aid of a special fund.  [111] These are the few ideas I would ask you  to consider, discuss and adopt, should they meet  with your approval. They are prompted, not by  any spirit of criticism, but by a deep devotion to  the Organization and a desire to help it to develop  and succeed in every respect.
