Carneiro (Brazil), Joxe  (France), and Khoury (Lebanon) seconding.  Adopted unanimously.  Mr. Désy then took the Chair. The  accepted practice was, however, to regard such  documents as provisional credentials. The States  concerned were the following:  Argentina India  Bolivia Monaco  Costa Rica Salvador  Czechoslovakia Sweden  Greece Turkey  Haiti Uruguay  Honduras Yugoslavia. [6] The SECRETARY then reported that three  States (Ecuador, Monaco and Sweden) had pre-  sented credentials signed for the Minister by a  senior official of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs.  At the previous session of the General Confer-  ence the Credentials Committee had accepted  credentials signed by another Minister on behalf  of the Minister of Foreign Affairs as regular, but  had reserved its decision on credentials signed by  a senior official of the Foreign Ministry.  [7] Mr. JOXE (France) asked whether the docu-  ments in question were in fact credentials in the  strict sense or extensions of powers conferred  under existing credentials, only these extensions  being communicated over the signature of a high  official. The SECRETARY explained that they  were credentials proper.     [8] On the motion of Mr. HARDMAN (United  Kingdom), seconded by Sir Sarvepalli RADHA-  KRISHNAN (India), Professor CARNEIRO (Brazil)  and Mr. JOXE (France), it was decided to accept  these credentials provisionally and to inform the  States concerned that, as their credentials did not  meet the requirements of Rule 22 of the Rules of  Procedure, they should be regularized as soon as  possible.  [9] The SECRETARY said that five Member  States—Afghanistan, Saudi Arabia, Peru, Poland  and Syria—had sent no credentials.  [10] The CHAIRMAN having moved that the  delegates concerned be asked to lodge their cre-  dentials and meanwhile be permitted to sit pro-  visionally, Professor CARNEIRO (Brazil) enquired  whether the delegates in question would have the  right of audience and be entitled to vote until  their credentials were lodged in due form.  The CHAIRMAN replied that they would, sub-  ject to their having lodged provisional credentials.  [11] Mr. SABA (Legal Adviser) said that, in  point of fact, these were all States represented by  a permanent delegate, i.e., by a representative al-  ready accredited to Unesco, who had indicated  that he had been accredited to represent his Gov-  ernment at the General Conference. It was  customary in the United Nations—as it had been  in the League of Nations—to accord provisional  rights to delegates whose official credentials were  not yet to hand, but who, being permanent dele-  gates or Ambassadors to a particular country,  were well known, and to ask such delegates to  regularize the position as early as possible.  [12] The CHAIRMAN explained that what was  meant in that context was the lodging, less of  credentials in due form, than of some form of  written authority; this could be signed by the  head of the permanent delegation, as in the case  of Peru.  [13] Mr. KHOURY (Lebanon) having asked  whether votes of such delegates would be valid  while they lacked credentials, the CHAIRMAN  pointed out that it was perfectly easy for perma-  nent delegates (who, being accredited to Unesco,  were well known) to lodge documents which  could be accepted as provisional credentials.     [14] Dr. WALKER (Australia) said that, in  order to take part in the session, every delegate  must produce some document to the Credentials  Committee, on which the latter could rule as  constituting full or provisional credentials. If a  country’s permanent delegate or its Ambassador  lodged a note stating that he had been instructed  to represent his Government at the General Con-
688  ference, such document, being signed by a known  authority, would be accepted as constituting cre-  dentials of a sort, if only provisional. Hence it  was essential for delegates at present lacking cre-  dentials to secure from their country’s permanent  delegate to Unesco or from its Embassy in Italy  or an adjacent country, a document enabling the  Credentials Committee to permit them to take  their seats, speak and vote.  It was so decided.  [15] The SECRETARY indicated that certain  non-Member States had been invited to send  observers, and credentials in due form had been  received from the following States:  Bulgaria Nicaragua  Korea San Marino  Indonesia Vatican  Nepal  The Supreme Command of the Allied Powers  in Japan (SCAP) had also accredited observers.  The CHAIRMAN proposed that the report on ob-  servers be approved. Adopted.  [16] The CHAIRMAN suggested that the Com-  mittee examine the draft resolution submitted by  the Czech delegation to the first Plenary Meeting  regarding the credentials of the delegation of  China.  [17] Dr. WALKER (Australia) explained that  the Credentials Committee was required to make  recommendation on that item to the General Con-  ference.  It would be recalled that the communication  from the Central People’s Government of the  People’s Republic of China, to which the Czech  delegate had referred, had been forwarded to the  Director-General, the Executive Board and, by  decision of the latter, to himself as Acting Presi-  dent of the Fifth Session of the General Confer-  ence. The contents of this communication had  been made public at the time it was received,  but the text was available for any delegation or  Committee desiring to consult it. It was the  speaker’s intention to suggest to the President of  the General Conference, when elected, that this  communication, along with any further com-  munications received, should be considered in the  first instance by the General Committee of the  Conference, which had the responsibility of co-  ordinating the work of the Conference and divid-  ing it among the different organs of the Confer-  ence.  [18] As regards the specific question raised by  the Czechoslovak delegate, the situation at the  moment was as follows: as two delegations ob-  jected to the admission of the Chinese delegation,                              under Rule 24 of the Rules of Procedure the dele-  gation should “be seated provisionally with the  same rights as other representatives until the  Credentials Committee has reported and the Gen-  eral Conference has given its decision.” The  Committee could examine the matter at more  leisure and report later; but the speaker felt it  to be possible and desirable to determine the  matter as soon as possible, preferably that same  afternoon in plenary meeting, to avoid a pro-  tracted discussion dragging on indefinitely and  delaving the Conference from proceeding to some  of the tasks before it. He suggested, therefore,  that they should have an exchange of views where-  by they might evolve a recommendation making  it possible to arrive that very day at a solution in  the interests of the Organization as a whole.  [19] Professor BOGDANOVIC (Yugoslavia) said  that the case of China had to be elucidated by the  Credentials Committee. Independently of the  motion already mentioned, the Yugoslav delega-  tion was of opinion that the credentials of the  Kuomintang delegate could not be “verified”,  since the Kuomintang Government was a gov-  ernment with no legal status and hence no  government at all: the Yugoslav delegation  would accordingly vote against any proposal that  the Kuomintang delegate be permitted to take his  seat provisionally with full powers.  [20] Sir Sarvepalli RADHAKRISHNAN (India)  recalled that the Czechoslovak motion had been  moved and seconded in plenary meeting and the  President had stated that it would be communi-  cated to the Credentials Committee for considera-  tion: in the circumstances it would be improper  for this Committee's report merely to mention  that this vital question had not yet been examined.  [21] Mr. SARGEANT (United States of America)  asked whether the credentials of the Nationalist  Chinese delegation conformed to the requirements  of Rule 22 of the Rules of Procedure and were  technically in order.  [22] The SECRETARY explained that the cre-  dentials bore the usual seal and were similar to  those received in previous years. To be able to  give an assurance that they were technically in  order, a knowledge of the Chinese language would  be necessary. However, the translator certified  and Professor Chen Yuan confirmed that the cre-  dentials were duly signed by the President of the  tepublic, the Prime Minister, the Minister of  Foreign Affairs and the Minister of Education, so  that the requirements of Rule 22 of the Rules of  Procedure were more than met.
123] Mr. SARGEANT (United States of Amer-  G89  ica) pointed out that, of the governements re-  presented at that Session, some accorded recog-  nition to the Nationalist Government and some {0  the Central People’s Government. It would be  unfortunate if the General Conference of Unesco  attempted to deal with problems of the recognition  of governments, which were the hardest tasks fall-  ing to any committee, conference or government.  Moreover, Article Il of the Constitution stipulated  that “membership of the United Nations Organiza-  tion shall carry with it the right to membership  of the United Nations Educational Scientific and  Cultural Organization”. In point of fact, the  United Nations at present accorded recognition to  a delegation of Nationalist China, of the same  government which had appointed the delegation  in Florence. It was indisputable that in fact  Unesco took its guidance from the United Nations  as regarded the Member States recognized by the  latter; accordingly such Member States of the  United Nations were entitled to be represented at  the Fifth Session of the General Conference of  Unesco. Mr. Sargeant therefore proposed, on be-  half of his Government, that the Committee should  report to the General Conference that the creden-  tials of the delegation of Nationalist China con-  formed to the requirements of Rule 22 of the  Rules of Procedure and that that delegation should  accordingly be permitted to take part in the pre-  sent Session with all the rights of a Member  State.  [24] Sir Sarvepalli RADHAKRISHNAN (India)  said that he too had his instructions. First of all  it was not a question of technical correctness or  the legality of the position: the Constitution itself  provided that each Member State should make  such arrangements as suited its particular condi-  tion for the purpose of associating its principal  bodies in educational, scientific and cultural  matters with the work of the Organization. They  had to decide whether they should accept the  tyranny of a theory or recognize the facts as they  were. Had the Government of Nationalist China  the authority to work in the spirit of the Constitu-  tion? Could it carry out the obligations laid on  it by Article VII and set up a national co-operating  body to implement the resolutions of the Confer-  ence and make the Conference well known to the  people of China? They had to go not on theories  but on the facts, to find out the best practical  means of fulfilling Unesco’s function in a vast  area with 450,000,000 inhabitants. The funda-  mental question was therefore whether the Nation-  alist Government had the power, the authority.  the practical means and the techniques to work  for Unesco.  [25] Secondly, they had to bear in mind the  E E   N R A  ==   future possibilities of the Organization. Already  there were several States which were not mem-  bers of Unesco. Any decision the present Gen-  eral Conference might take might have far-  reaching consequences and could affect Unesco’s  future activities and make of it an organization  representing only part of the world. They could  add fuel to the existing ideological conflict, but  each one should ask himself whether he preferred  to secure Unesco’s future or to cripple its activities  by an ill-judged decision. It was not a question  of deciding whether they approved or disapproved  of the administrative methods of the Chinese Gov-  ernment; all that concerned them was to find the  best means of promoting among the masses of  the world’s population the ideals for which Unesco  stood.  [26] Lastly, as regarded representation of Nation-  alist China, Professor Chen Yuang who would  continue to be a member of the Executive Board  until the end of the present Conference would  have the fullest opportunity of expressing his  point of view.  [27] Thus, having regard to the fact that  Nationalist China was not in a position to imple-  ment the resolutions of the General Conference,  that recognition of its delegation would be bound  to cripple the future activities of Unesco and  seriously jeopardize its future and, lastly, that  there was someone present at the General Confer-  ence capable of representing the point of view of  Nationalist China pending a decision by the United  Nations, the Organization would be in a much  better position than if it decided on a point of  law, theory or doctrine.  [28] Professor CARNEIRO (Brazil) thought that  in a problem so complex they had to eliminate  all extraneous elements. While it was well to take  into account the element of effective co-operation  requisite for acceptance as a Member State of  Unesco they had to beware of creating a precedent  with serious consequences for the future by having  the Credentials Committee take cognizance of this  factor and subordinating recognition of a State to  the consideration of how far it was able to meet  its commitments to the Organization.  Whatever the position regarding the Chinese  question might be, all were agreed in thinking  that what the Chinese themselves regarded as  “China” should be represented at the General  Conference. However, to change the present state  of affairs, it seemed essential to await a decision  by the United Nations. By accepting the creden-  tials submitted by the Chinese Nationalist Gov-  ernment, the Committee would in no sense be  setting itself up in judgment on the domestic
690  affairs of China: pending the decision at political  level, which was the prerogative of the United  Nations, the Committee was merely recognizing  the representative of China, who had co-operated  with Unesco from the Organization's birth.  Mr. KHOURY (Lebanon) supported Professor  Carneiro’s proposal.  [29] Professor BOGDANOVIC (Yugoslavia) re-  peated that, quite apart from the motion already  submitted on the subject, the question of the cre-  dentials of Nationalist China could only be  examined by the Credentials Committee. Sir  Sarvepalli and Professor Carneiro had displayed  great knowledge of the question and submitted  brilliant suggestions. The delegates of Nationalist  China could no longer take their seats in Unesco  because they no longer represented anyone; on  the other hand, it was impossible to shut the door  of Unesco to hundreds of millions of Chinese.  Hence the Yugoslav delegation would oppose any  provisional solution however emphatically its pro-  visional nature were stated.  [30] Mr. HARDMAN (United Kingdom), speak-  ing on behalf of his government, said he could not  support the proposition of the delegate of the  United States of America. In saying that, he  recognized the force of the arguments adduced for  and against. What struck him most was that  here was a great territory described by one writer  as perhaps the largest area of civilization over  thousands of years that we had in the world:  there was no doubt in his mind that not China  only, but another great country which was not a  member of Unesco had a part to play in the devel-  opment of the Organization. From that point of  view the arguments in favour of non-recognition  of a delegation from Nationalist China to the pre-  sent Session of the Conference were very strong.  China should be in Unesco but the delegation of  the United Kingdom was not able to support the  American proposal and, if a vote were taken on  the subject, his delegation would abstain.  [31] The CHAIRMAN thought it impossible to  present that same afternoon a satisfactory report  on a discussion whose importance was plain to  all.  The question was of great complexity, being  simultaneously political and legal. Were the  General Conference of Unesco and its Credentials  Committee competent to deal with political ques-  tions? (in the present instance the credentials  question before them had mainly political implica-  tions)” Ought that Committee to exceed its terms  of reference? Should it state that the credentials  submitted to it were not in order (in which case  it had to give its reasons)? If those credentials  were in order, could it recommend the exclusion                           t m   of the Chinese delegation on political grounds?  Could Unesco tell the United Nations that a partic-  ular delegation did not belong to a Member State  of the United Nations?  [32] As the question warranted fuller examina-  tion, while the Committee had to present a report  at the beginning of the afternoon to avoid delaying  the constitution of the Plenary Conference, the  speaker suggested a statement that the Committee  approved the report of the Secretariat as regarded  all the other items examined and that question of  the Chinese delegation’s credentials would be the  subject of a later report. That was a practical pro-  posal, committing them neither way on the point  in question. Nevertheless, pending a decision  Rule 24 of the Rules of Procedure should be  applied.  [33] Mr. JOXE (France) was prepared to support  the Chairman’s proposal as regarded the method  of working: the matter was too serious and too  heavy with consequences to be settled in a few  moments. It was clearly essential that China,  with its civilization, its traditions and all that it  could bring, be present at the Fifth Session of the  General Conference in whole or in part.  [34] However, the problem facing them was one  of those recently raised in the Co-ordination  Committee of the United Nations and Specialized  Agencies: the present political situation had made  the normal operation of the United Nations and  Specialized Agencies impossible and of that the  present discussion was glaring evidence.  On the one hand, there were the arguments  based on law adduced by the delegates of the  United States of America and of Brazil; on the  other, there were arguments based on fact pre-  sented by the delegate of India. Their Committee  might see fit to take a decision on the point of  law (which was within its competence) or a deci-  SECOND  sion on political grounds (which would go further  than the decision on law and for which the Com-  mittee would appear to have no competence).  [35] It would therefore be well to pursue the  discussion so that all might be able to express  their views. In any case it seemed essential that  Unesco should approach the United Nations, the  political body, and enquire how this delicate  question, which was preventing the General Con-  ference from starting its work, might most quick-  ly be settled.
136] Mr. KHOURY (Lebanon) said that, of the  proposals put forward, three (those of the United  States of America, of Brazil and of France) were  closely akin in spirit: all three sought to keep  Unesco clear of the political problem. The  speaker himself felt that the General Conference  of Unesco had no competence to deal with the  problem’s political aspect, but if they confined  themselves to the law in the matter, there was  nothing to prevent recognition of the credentials  of the Chinese Nationalist Government: it would  seem, therefore, that until the question was  settled by the United Nations, who alone had  authority to do this, there were no valid grounds  for objecting to the admission of the delégates of  the Government of Nationalist China.  [37] On the motion of the CHAIRMAN it was  decided to report at the beginning of the after-  noon on the credentials examined by the Com-  mittee, including those of the Yugoslav delegate  and of the permanent delegate of Peru (just  handed to the Chairman), to reserve the question  of the Chinese delegation’s credentials for later  consideration and meanwhile to apply the pro-  visions of Rule 24 of the Rules of Procedure in  that delegation’s case.
