Those systems are everywhere  tainted by academicalism and dilettantism, by intel-  lectual formalism, and by racial and religious pre-  judice, so that men thus prepared for life lack the  technical ability which is nowadays an essential  requirement, and also the understanding, sympathy,  sense of brotherhood and fellowship, which can unite  individuals and peoples and bring into being true  world understanding. In the educational and cultural  spheres, it seems necessary, all over the world, to  improve the technical training provided for students  and manual and intellectual workers of all categories,  and to strive without ceasing for the development of  a humanism which, while allowing each people to  benefit by the experience of past generations as set  forth in their own classics, would have a firm bias  towards à new conception of the universal human  community. .  On this last point, I want to say that there are not  and cannot be any irreducible differences between men,  At the meeting held in New Delhi under Unesco's  auspices, in December 1951, to study the fundamental  concepts of education and culture in the Orient and  the Occident, Prime Minister Nehru delivered a  speech which we are;bound to recall today because of  the remarkable breadth of the ideas and suggestions  it contained. The Head of the Indian Government  said that he had always rejected the idea of a division  of the world that placed the Orient and the Occident  in opposition to each other, and added that the true  difference between the peoples was the result of the  inequality in their economic development rather than  of their psychological tendencies. Whether we like  it or not, we must start from the idea of the funda-  mental unity of the human species and make education  and culture into efficient means for bringing about  reconciliation and understanding between the peoples.
1 wish also to stress that the concept of humanism  is based on the essential principle of the freedom of  peoples and individuals, and that this principle, con-  stantly menaced, must be an object of vital and per-  manent concern to all educational and cultural in-  stitutions thrbughout the world,   PLENARY  On the other hand, I would say that Unesco must  continue its work - which is already worthy of high  praise - and carry out a wider programme to foster  the development of education and culture in all  countries. .  In this connexion, I should like to mention a number  of large-scale projects dealing, for instance, with  vocational education, scientific research or the en-  couragement of the arts, But I shall ask leave merely  to submit a few considerations on one problem, that  of primary education, which deserves, I think, to  have priority, ’  Only a few nations have succeeded in finding the  much needed solution to this problem, In most coun-  tries, a very large number of people are still com-  pletely without any kind of education, or receive an  unsatisfactory primary education, extending over too  short a period, which cannot develop the skills and  the modes of feeling appropriate to present-day man,  This subject is so complex that I cannot deal with it  at this time, when we are properly concerned with the  exchange of greetings and compliments. I feel it is  my duty, however, to ask Unesco to make primary  education its first and main preoccupation,  We are bound to recognize that the problem under-  lying all these matters is financial. Educational and  cultural activities call for enormous expenditure,  For that reason, 1 venture to repeat the suggestion,  , made two years ago at Montevideo by my eminent  colleague, Mr. de Berredo Carneiro, for the creation  MEETINGS

I personally  am convinced that primary education must play a  decisive part in contemporary efforts to raise the  level of civilization throughout the world and I would  venture to suggest that half of the International Fund  should be permanently set aside to finance primary  education projects.
