Dr. GHOSH (India) expressed India’s hope that Unesco’s effort in the field of technical assistance to under-developed countries would make a great contribution to civilization. At the recent Confer- ence at Lake Success on the Utilization of Natural Resources, due attention had been given to Technic- al Assistance, and it had been stated that the popula- tion of the world had increased by one thousand million in the last four generations and was likely to increase by a further one thousand million by the end of the century. In more advanced coun- tries, where resources were exploited, the rate of production of essential goods was greater than the rate of increase of the population: thus there was no hunger, and people had the expectation of a decent life as a reward for normal labour. In less fortunate countries, where modern science and technology had not played their part, the increas- ing population was not only suffering the same standard of living as before, but often the latter was going down. A drastic remedy was therefore needed in countries like India, with its mediaeval living conditions, famine, disease, and untimely death. Delegates to the Lake Success Conference had been horrified at the situation in countries like India, and had come to the conclusion that there was a great gap between what could be done to help such countries and what was being done. The gap could only be bridged through the good will of all men. The question wasraised as to what part Unesco could play in bridging the gap: it could provide teachers, raise money for fellowships and, above all, make knowledge available which hitherto had been restricted to small sections of the world’s population. A delegate at Lake Suc- cess had said: The store of scientific and technical knowledge existing today is the world’s best resource, and one which grows with use and is enlarged by sharing. It was Unesco’s task to find means of making it
3 29 ~ 1 shared and enlarged for the benefit of humanity as a whole. In order to improve living conditions in India, there must be sufficient man-power to develop industry, and this could only be done by training people in the country itself: there was a lack of expert teachers in the technical training centres, and Dr. Ghosh suggested that experts in war- devastated areas who could find no work and technical experts displaced from Eastern Germany might be sent to the under-developed country to train the people there. Teachers might also be found in the United States where the attitude among scientists was that it was their moral duty to fight evils, disease, ignorance and poverty all over the world. In the poorer countries, there was also a lack of cheap textbooks and other teaching materials, and Unesco would render a great service if it could provide them. Unesco could also help to provide facilities for foreign students to enter the technical training schools of more advanced countries. If talented fellows from under-developed countries were unable to follow up their education by train- ing on the practical side, they could not help to build up their country’s industry when they re- turned. Dr. Ghosh realized that the funds available for technical assistance were not large; but, as the United Kingdom delegate had said, “More money does not mean more efficient work”, and an effec- tive start could be made with the funds available. It should not be forgotten, moreover, that the under-developed countries themselves would have to co-operate in working out their own salvation, and Unesco would have to aim at changing the outlook of the people concerned, making them self-reliant and giving them a hope for the future. The increase of production and productivity alone might be to the disadvantage of men, and it was impossible to            make full use of the world without making full use of men.
