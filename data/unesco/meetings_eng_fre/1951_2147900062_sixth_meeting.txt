Distribution of Members of the Secretariat by Nationality. Item 9.5.5 of the Agenda (docu- ment 6C/ADM/13). [26] Mr. GIELGUD (Head, Bureau of Personnel and Management) introduced the Secretariat docu- ment, which, he said, had been drawn up in the same form as in previous years. There was a total of
383 “geographical” posts in the Secretariat, the aim being to obtain the best possible distribution between nationalities compatible with the selection of the best candidate for each post. [27] That difficulty had been experienced in combining those two criteria was shown by the fact that a total of 41 geographical posts were at the moment vacant, in spite of the Secretariat’s policy of informing National Commissions of all vacancies. The Secretariat regretted the slow improvement in that matter. The existing situation was obviously unequal, but improvement could only be gradual, and it was for National Commissions to assist in finding highly qualified candidates. It was not in the interests of the Organization to reduce the level of staff competence in order to procure a better geogra- phical distribution. [28] Mr. KIRPAL (India) was pleased to note that some progress had been made towards implementing the resolution of the Fifth Session calling for a better     ||||  VII-6 geographical distribution of posts, but his delegation, whilst recognizing the Director-General's difficulties in that respect, would urge the Secretariat to make even greater efforts. [29] Mr. Kirpal knew that under-developed coun- tries could often not afford to release their best personnel, but felt it was important that Unesco's Secretariat should be representative of all nation- alities and cultures. [30] He proposed the following draft resolution: While taking note of the fact that during the past year more progress has been made towards a better distribu- tion of members of the Secretariat by nationality, The Administrative Commission is of the opinion that many important geographical regions are still not adequately represented on the Secretariat, and Recommends that the Director-General take further steps to make this distribution more adequate and representative of different regions and cultures. [31] Mr. KRAMET (Pakistan) supported the reso- lution. He felt that the efficiency of under-developed countries would be improved by greater represen- tation on international secretariats. He urged that, where there was only a small difference in the qua- lifications of two candidates, priority should be given to the one from an under-developed country, even if he were the less qualified of the two. Countries which were not represented on the Secretariat were at a disadvantage when attending the General Con- ference and could not take part in its debates as fully as would be possible it they had a national represen- tative to stimulate their interest and provide back- ground information. He appreciated the Director- General's difficulties in that respect, and therefore made no formal proposal, but merely wished the situation to be noted and studied. [32] Mr. de SPOT (Belgium) observed that the complex problem involved had already been dis- cussed at Florence. In his opinion, one of the main reasons why certain States were not adequately represented was that they did not nominate candi- dates. He thought that vacancies in Unesco’s Secre- tariat should be given publicity in all countries, and suggested that the Secretariat, in informing National Commissions of such vacancies, should ask them to publish notices thereof in their countries’ Official Journals. [33] Mr. MASON (New Zealand) requested infor- mation about the prevailing methods of recruitment for “geographical” posts. [53] Sir Ben THOMAS (United Kingdom) said that he had some general comments to make on the report. The organizational changes made by the Director-General fell into two categories, those made in the programme Departments, and those made in the administrative Bureaux, the aim being to secure greater economy and a better distribution of func- tions. The United Kingdom delegation could not agree that that had been achieved in either sphere. Among the programme Departments the example of Cultural Activities could be taken. There the total number of posts had been reduced from 74 in 1951 to 68 in 1952, but the new structure did not appear to be much better than the old. In particular, the duties of the “Division for the Development of Inter- national Co-operation” seemed, to a large extent, unnecessary. The other Divisions would naturally maintain relationships with non-governmental organi- zations in their own fields, and the only function of the Division mentioned would seem to be the co-ordination of such relationships. It was felt that that could better be achieved by the provision of one or two specialists in the Office of the Director,         - VII-7 AD MINS TRATE which already contained a high-graded Deputy Director. [64] The United Kingdom delegation was also highly critical of the new structure of the adminis- trative Bureaux. As it had pointed out at previous sessions of the General Conference, a thorough overhaul of the Organization’s methods of accounting and control was required. It still felt strongly that the existing close financial control of programme Departments was unnecessary and wasteful. [65] Sir Ben Thomas noted that, despite the disso- lution of the Bureau of Administrative Management and Budget, four Bureaux remained in existence, at a total cost of over one million dollars, or more than ten per cent of the total budget. Thus the reorgani- zation carried out by the Director-General appeared to result in a negligible economy, and the United Kingdom delegation regretted the statement that the limit of compression and economy in the adminis- trative services had been reached. [56] In particular, it was critical of the new organi- zation and distribution of functions in the Office of "the Director-General. That Office contained 28 posts, including an unclassified Deputy Director-General, and a Director at grade 19, as well as a separate Programme and Budget Division. The necessity for a Director at the same level as the Directors of pro- gramme Departments was not understood. From the outline of the functions of the Office, it appeared that the programme and budget planning of the SEVENTH E COMMISSTON 468 different Departments would be duplicated in the Office of the Director-General, under the title of “synthesis and co-ordination”. That seemed a most wasteful system and might even lead to strained relationships within the Secretariat. With great respect, the United Kingdom delegation would recommend that the Director-General himself, with the assistance of his Deputy, could take the task of co-ordination and synthesis into his own hands. That could be achieved very simply by regular and full meetings between the Director-General and his Deputy and the heads of the programme Departments and administrative Bureaux concerned, with joint meetings when necessary. At a lower level, small committees could be established under the Deputy Director-General to deal with problems affecting one or more Departments, such as publications policy and control of Unesco’s literary output, general per- sonnel policy, etc. [67] Sir Ben Thomas made the foregoing general comments as a prelude to the debate on the matter at the Commission’s next meeting.
