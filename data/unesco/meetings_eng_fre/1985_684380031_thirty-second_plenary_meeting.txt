57.1 Mr LUCKHOO (Guyana) :  Thank you, Mr President. I must confess that a certain amount of dismay  infected me this morning when I heard the proposals for amendments put forward by  my very good friend Mr Gordon, the distinguished representative of the United  Kingdom. I could well understand stylistic amendments as they related to part I  of 23 C/PLEN/DR.13; they were in the nature of minor amendments. But when it came  to part II of the draft resolution, the first thought which came to my mind was:  in what way is the United Kingdom affected by part II so as to be impelled to  make the particular amendments concerned? The second thought which came to my  mind was: is the United Kingdom speaking for the United States of America? I had  that uneasy feeling because I could find nothing in part II to which the United  Kingdom could take any legitimate objection.
57.2 We have heard a lot this morning about goodwill, negotiation and appeal-  ing to a sense of reconsideration of our attitudes, a good deal about not indulg-  ing in a spirit of vendetta or vengeance of any kind. Let us look at part II,  Mr President, and see what it contains. In my humble view, operative paragraph 1  of part II has inherently built into it that spirit of goodwill, that spirit of  negotiation, and how does it read? It 'authorizes the Executive Board at its
123rd session to appoint from among its members a group whose task will be to  open negotiations with the authorities of the State concerned over its financial  contribution for the 1985 financial year'. If to open negotiations is not in a  spirit of goodwill, I do not know what is. My own draft resolution, Mr Presi-  dent, 23 C/PLEN/DR.9 which I withdrew last Saturday in an effort to try and reach  consensus was a harsher approach; it was trying to lay the foundation for  recourse to an arbitral tribunal the decision of which would be final. That was a  harsher approach. This paragraph 1 offers an easier modus of trying to reach  agreement by negotiation. So I do not see how complaint could be made about that  particular paragraph.
57.3 Now we come to operative paragraph 2. Why should the United Kingdom wish  to disturb the rights of the Executive Board to have recourse to the Inter-  national Court of Justice? Is it not within the rights and the competence of the  Executive Board, if it considers necessary, as paragraph 2 says, if it consideys  desirable, as paragraph 2 says, to go for an advisory opinion? And would it then  not be the right of the Board to decide what should happen after having received  that opinion? That is normal. This recourse to the International Court.of Justice  is in relation to a matter that has passed. It is an opinion in relation to a  something that has passed. It is an opinion in relation to something which has  already taken place, and if the Executive Board felt it necessary, the Executive  Board is entitled to know its legal position in relation to what has taken place.  If the United Kingdom wishes to propose that rules should be introduced for the  future in order to be able to establish beyond a mere peradventure and in a  solid, firm, clear unambigious way what should be the legal position in relation  to a Member State which leaves the Organization during a budgetary period, then  by all means it would be at liberty to take such steps or have other Member  States take such steps to secure that particular amendment to the Constitution.  That is a prerogative which is open to the United Kingdom and to other Member  States which might wish to see that entrenched position introduced in the Con-  stitution. But certainly, Mr President, that is a far cry from disturbing para-  graphs 1 and 2 as they stand, and I do not find any justification - logical or  otherwise - for the introduction of any amendment which would completely obliter-  ate paragraphs 1 and 2 in part II.
