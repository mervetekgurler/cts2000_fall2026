1. Le PRESIDENT, -  La dix-huitième séance plénière est ouverte, Je donne la parole à S, Exe, M. Mongo  Soo, chef de la délégation du Cameroun.
2,1 M. MONGO SOO (Cameroun), -  Monsieur le Président, vous me permettrez de me joindre aux éminents orateurs qui  m'ont précédé pour vous adresser, en mon nom personnel et au nom de ma délégation, de  vives félicitations pour votre élection unanime à la présidence de la dix-septième session  de la Conférence générale, Je saisis cette occasion pour vous assurer du concours total de  la délégation du Cameroun,
2,2 Mesdames, Messieurs, le Cameroun essaie, depuis son indépendance, de procéder à  la recréation culturelle et à l'intensification de ses moyens d'information, à l'effet de mo-  biliser les forces des différentes cultures dont il émerge, en vue de l'action pour le déve-  loppement du pays dont le Président de la République unie du Cameroun a dit qu'elle dépend
18  avant tout du savoir des hommes, en un mot de la science, Dans ce contexte, mon pays a  décidé de faire de la science l'axe principal de l'effort de rénovation de son système d'édu-  cation et de sa culture, et c'est dans cette perspective que nous examinerons les interven-  tions de l'Unesco à nos côtés, dans notre lutte quotidienne pour le bien-être,
2.3 Le Cameroun souhaite plus que jamais que l'Unesca soit le facteur essentiel d'accélé-  ration du dé,eloppement global des Ftats membres et rende plus effective cette coopération  intellectuelle internationale dont nous savons aujourd'hui qu'elle vaut tous les transferts de  capitaux, car, encore une fois, "le facteur essentiel du développement demeure le savoir  des hommes”.
2.4 En cette étape de l'évolution de nos pays et des relations internationales, la rénova-  tion de l'éducation est l'une des plates-formes privilégiées qui appellent intensément une  telle coopération, et le contenu des rapports rédigés par les membres de la commission  présidée pa. M, Edgar Faure montre combien chaque groupe social national, réfléchis-  sant aux multiples aspects de son éducation, gagnerait à connaître les résultats auxquels  sont arrivés les groupes de réflexion d'autres pays,
2.5 Les multipies relations progressivement établies, au cours de l'histoire, entre des  sociétés de structures différentes ont favorisé l'épanouissement, ici et là, d'éléments cul-  turels hybrides qui font en sorte que, malgré la diversité du phénomène humain, on peut ra-  mener les aspirations des peuples à un certain nombre de principes sur lesquels devraient  se fonder les entreprises d'éducation, Cette reconnaissance d'aspirations communes et uni-  verselles au sein de l'humanité fonde la nécessité d'une solidarité qui doit faciliterl'har-  monisation des réflexions nationales dans des domaines tels que la rénovation de l'éduca-  tion, En choisissant la science comme pivot de notre action dans cette entreprise, nous  avons pensé que c'est autour d'elle que l'humanité contemporaine se recrée dans l'unité et  la plénitude. Et, afin que les connaissances scientifiques constituent ce centre moteur,  nous pensons qu'elles ne doivent pas devenir un corp“ figé ou qui se cristallise autour des  hommes ou autour des ensembles sociaux qui les ont rendus possibles, Pour être le fonde-  ment de la r‘énovation de l'éducation, la science doit nécessairement être installée dans  l'esprit des hommes comme une disposition permettant de voir la nature avec détachement,  mais avec optimisme, Telle est la base de la réflexion de mon pays dans le processus de  la constitution d'un système renouvelé d'éducation qui s'enracine dans les réalités objec-  tives de notre sol, de nos forêts, de nos cours d'eau, de nos climats, réalités qui ont favo-  visé l'éclosion, dans notre population, de ses multiples langues et de ses diverses mani-  festations spirituelles, éléments fondamentaux de sa culture, c'est-à-dire de son esprit.
2.6 Nous croyons que la recherche scientifique doit constituer, par-delà toute autre acti-  vité, le principal souci de l'Unesco au cours des prochaines années, car c'est elle qui per-  mettra une meilleure tonnaissance des données de tous ordres - géologiques, juridiques,  sociologiques et économiques - leur meilleure utilisation et leur meilleure mise en valeur  en vue de l'amélioration des conditions de vie, qui est bien le but recherché par tout gou-  vernement et par toute institution scientifique oeuvrant pour la paix,
2.7 Si nous insistons sur la nécessité d'insérer la science dans nos systèmes scolaires et  de la vulgariser au niveau des masses, ce n'est pas seulement parce que nous percevons  qu'elle commande nos existences, mais surtout parce que nous avons pris conscience de ce  que ses méthodes et ses exemples peuvent être généralisés et étendus aux disciplines so-  ciales et humaines. To
2.8 Pour vulgariser la science dans la vie de notre peuple, il nous est apparu fondamental  de réaliser d'abord la réforme de l'enseignement, afin de façonner l'homme camerounais  nouveau, susceptible de vivre plus pleinement, plus naturellement, la vie du monde de la  science moderne, Dans une telle tâche, des tâtonnements, voire des hésitations, sont iné-  vitables. À mon avis, ces tâtonnements se sont manifestés non seulement en ce qui concerne  l'action nationale mais également dans les interventions de l'Unesco à nos côtés,
2.9 En effet, au lendemain de notre indépendance, le gouvernement camerounais s'est trou-  vé devant des problèmes multiples et urgents ; des solutions partielles, adoptées à la hâte,  ont paré au plus pressant, mais il manquait le recul nécessaire à une coordination véri-  table ; tant et si bien qu'à la demande de notre pays, l'Unesco, au cours de cette première  période, a apporté son assistance dans des secteurs opérationnels divers, parfois contigus  mais rarement coordonnés : l'alphabétisation, l'envoi de professeurs, la collect. de sta-  tistiques, la formation des personnels de l'éducation,
2.10 Cette période devrait pouvoir être considérée comme révolue, car il serait regret-  table de continuer à utiliser l'assistance internationale pour des efforts sectoriels isolés,  voire incohérents, Mon pays est arrivé à un stade où il dnit définir sa politique d'ensemble  en matière d'éducation, penser l'innovation et planifier is opérations qui, plus tard, la réa-  liseront sans heurts, Le moment semble venu pour lui d'utiliser l'aide des Nations Unies à  des fins plus efficaces,
2.11 Cette aide étant assez limitée, surtout si on la compare aux besoins des pays en voie  de développement, l'Unesco devrait du moins pouvoir faire appel à des experts internatio-  ‘naux de grande classe, voire à des experts nationaux de haut niveau, capables d'aider les  gouvernements à définir leur politique d'éducation, afin que soit obtenu d'emblée le meilleur  coefficient multiplicateur des services rendus par l'Organisation,
2.12 11 est vrai que toute politique d'éducation est une affaire nationale, C'est au gouver-  nement d'en fixer les modalités, d'en décider en toute souveraineté, c'est à lui de définir  les finalités de l'éducation qui convient au peuple intéressé, en fonction d'aspects a la fois  culturels, économiques et sociaux qui entraînent parfois des transformations de toutle sys-  tème éducatif, notamment de ses structures, de ses programmes et de ses méthodes, voire  de la technique pédagogique,
2.13 Cependant, si la stratégie de l'innovation est une affaire nationale, il n'est pas con-  tradictoire que, pour y parvenir, on fasse appel à des spécialistes internationaux lorsque  cela se justifie, L'essentiel est de s'assurer de leur compétence et de leur dévouement au  pays d'accueil, qui est l'objet même de la mission qui leur est confiée, Par ailieurs, il con-  vient que la nomination de ces spécialistes corresponde aux besoins réels du pays solliciteur,
2,14 Les gouvernements devraient du reste, comme par le passé, pouvoir bénéficier, sui-  vant les formes qui leur conviennent, de l'assistance de l'Unesco pour la réalisation de leur  politique, grâce à la niise en place de projets opérationnels permettant que soit assurée la  continuité entre la conception et l'exécution,
2.15 L'assistance que l'Unesco apporte actuellement à la République unie du Cameroun mé-  rite d'être évoquée, car elle répond à cette double finalité.
2.16 Le système éducatif est au Cameroun ce qu'il est dans bien des pays africains : les  effectifs scolarisés, à tous les niveaux, sont en pleine expansion, le budget global de l'édu-  cation n'a cessé de croître, en valeur absolue comme en pourcentage du revenu national,  et un gros effort est partout sensible en faveur d'une démocratisation de l'enseignement.
2,17 Par contre, face à cette expansion explosive - et peut-être à cause d'elle - il appa-  raît clairement que l'éducation constitue une dépense de consommation toujours plus pe-  sante ; au Cameroun comme dans bien d'autres pays, le coût d'un élève formé à l'école  primaire atteint, d'après les économistes, sept fois le coût normal dans un système sans  déperditions, Et le plus grave, c'est que cet élève "formé' n'est préparé ni dans la tradi-  tion culturelle, ni en vue d'obtenir un emploi, ni dans la perspective d'améliorer "la qua-  lité de la vie", Car l'éducation répond toujours aux objectifs que l'on poursuivait, à juste  titre peut-être, du temps de l'époque coloniale : elle vise à préparer l'enfant aux études se-  condaires, et plus tard éventuellement à un emploi de commis, alors qu'aujourd'hui le dé-  veloppement du Cameroun exige une éducation des masses, et non plus sélective ; une édu-  cation tournée vers la vie, visant à la transformation du milieu, et non plus seulement un
18  enseignement académique ; une éducation permanente enfin, ouverte sur l'évolution, etnon  plus un savoir fermé sur des programmes exclusivement encyclopédiques,
2.18 Ce que je viens de dire du faible rendement et de la désadaptation de l'enseignement  primaire est également vrai des autres degrés d'enseignement. En un mot, l'éducation dé-  racine la jeunesse de son milieu d'origine, sans lui donner à coup sûr le moyen de mieux  vivre demain,
2.19 C'est pour remédier à cette situation que le gouvernement camerounais a décidé de  procéder à une réforme des finalités, des structures, des programmes et des méthodes du  système éducatif, afin que l'éducation à tous les niveaux, s'adapte aux besoins réels - cul-  turels, économiques et sociaux - de la nation, Et comme notre pays est essentiellement ru-  ral, cette réforme a été appelée ‘'ruralisation''. Le président Ahidjo l'a définie de la façon  suivante :  “L'école d'aujourd'hui, et singulièrement l'école africaine, ne doit pas se préoceu-  per seulement de préparer des bureaucrates et des technocrates, pour lesquels les  débouchés se rétrécissent chaque jour davantage ; elle doit éveiller l'attention des  jeunes aux multiples possibilités d'emploi qui s'offrent dans les secteurs primaires  et secondaires, " '
2.20 Pour passer de cette option politique à la mise au point technique des aspects con-  crets de la réforme, le gouvernement camerounais s'est adressé au PNUD et a 1'Unesco,  et c'est avec leur concours que sont nées ou vont naître trois institutions,
2.21 Tout d'abord, l'Institut de pédagogie appliquée à vocation rurale (l'IPAR) de Yaoundé,  Créé en 1967, il est chargé de concevoir la "’ruralisation" de l'enseignement primaire dans  la zone francophone, c'est-à-dire d'adapter cet enseignement aux besoins économiques, so-  ciaux et culturels du pays, par la création d'un type d'école nouvelle, la même d'ailleurs à  la ville qu'à la campagne. La réforme des structures, des programmes et des méthodes  une fois adoptée parles responsables, l'IPAR est chargé dela mettre en application, après  avoir produit les documents et manuels qui la concrétisent pour les élèves et pour les  maîtres, et après avoir procédé au recyclage de l'ensemble des enseignants en poste,  Toutes les écoles normales forment désormais les nouveaux maîtres à Jeur double rôle  d'éducateurs et d'animateurs pour une école de promotion collective, selon l'optique de la  réforme. Et celle-ci sera appliquée dans toutes les écoles du Cameroun, en commençant  par les classes de première année, dès la rentrée de 1975,
2,22 l.e même travail sera entrepris, pour la zone anglophone, par la section de l'IPAR  qui va s'ouvrir incessamment à Buéa. Poursuivant les mêmes objectifs, il aboutira à une  réforme similaire, mais concernant l'enseignement en anglais, Ainsi sera constituée, au  niveau de l'école primaire et pour la République unie du Cameroun, une plate-forme com-  mune d'éducation, progressivement orientée vers le bilinguisme officiel,
2,33 Une troisième institution doit être créée cette année à Yaoundé avec l'aide conjointe  de la France, du 1’°NUD et de l'Unesco : c'est l'Institut national d'éducation, dont le rôle  sera d'aider le gouvernement à définir sa politique en matière de réforme des enseigne-  ments à tous les niveaux, ainsi que dans le domaine de l'éducation parascolaire et perma-  nente, Intégrant les efforts des divers organismes de recherche, il proposera au gouver-  nement des solutions concernant le prolongement de la "’ruralisation'" entreprise au niveau  du primaire, Ainsi sera réalisée la réforme de l'ensemble du système, réforme qui sera  d'ailleurs périodiquement remise à jour en fonction des résultats obtenus et de l'évolution  du pays.
2,24 Ce tableau rapide montre à quel point l'Unesco est étroitement associée, au Came-  roun, à la conception et à la réalisation de la r'énovation en matière d'éducation. Jene vou-  drais pas manquer d'évoquer les autres assistances qui sont venues soutenir les efforts du  gouvernement camerounais : assistances multilatérales comme celles de la BIRD, de l'Uni-  cel, du PANI ; assistances bilatérales comme celles dela France, du Canada, de la Répu-
18  blique fédérale d'Allemagne. Toutes ces aides complémentaires contribuent évidemment  au succès de notre entreprise, dont dépend l'avenir du Cameroun,
2,25 Cependant, il serait plus juste de dire que l'avenir en dépend en partie. En effet, si  l'éducation nouvelle est un facteur nécessaire du développement, il ne faudrait pas s'ima-  giner qu'elle en est le facteur suffisant, Son impact ne sera effectif que si, parallèlement,  les institutions économiques et sociales procèdent aussi à une réforme, de façon que les  jeunes formés à l'école trouvent une société préparée à les accueillir, La réformeagraire,  l'exploitation forestière, la création d'une infrastructure pour la circulation et la vente des  produits, le développement des coopératives, des banques, des institutions de crédits, sont  autant de conditions dont dépend la réussite d'une réforme de l'éducation. Toute innovation  en ce domaine doit, sous peine d'échouer, s'inscrire dans un plan concerté de développement.
2.26 Pourtant, au Cameroun, nous n'avons pas attendu que ces conditions soient toutes  remplies pour entamer la réforme du système éducatif, car nous savons qu'un temps assez  long doit s'écouler avant que les premiers fruits d'une telle réforme n'apparaissent.
2.27 En considération de ce qui précède, la délégation camerounaise constate avec satis-  faction que l'assistance apportée par l'Unesco au Cameroun répond aux principes définis par  le Directeur général de l'Unesco dans les documents 17 C/4 et 17 C/5 en ce qui concerne  l'aide aux pays en voie de développement, D'autre part, la politique que poursuit le Came-  roun est conforme également aux conclusions du rapport de’la Commission internationale  sur le développement de l'éducation, C'est pour nous, Camerounais, un grand encourage-  ment dans la voie que nous nous sommes tracée : nous puiserons dans ces documents des  idées et des forces nouvelles, Il m'est agréable de préciser que la délégation camerou-  naise, que j'ai l'honneur de présider, apporte son plein accord à cette sorte de charte de  l'éducation qui répond entièrement aux préoccupations de notre gouvernement, Celui-ci  croit à la planification et considère que les modèles d'autres pays, tout en étant valables,  peuvent ne pas produire leur plein effet chez nous ; il attache par conséquent du prix à ce  que l'aide extérieure s'adapte à nos besoins réels et à ce qu'aucun modèle ne soit a a priori  considéré comme parfait. ‘
2.28 C'est ainsi qu'à l'instar de ce qui est accompli dans l'enseignement primaire, la ré-  forme des étndes médicales, qui a abouti à la création du Centre universitaire des sciences  de la santé de Yaoundé, s'est inspirée davantage de la nature de la mission du personnel de  santé dans un pays en voie de développement et .de la topographie du milieu dans lequel ce  personnel est appelé à exercer, que des modèles existant dans le monde,
2.29 Si cette position nous semble valable pour les études médicales, mon gouvernement  estime qu'il doit en être de même pour la plupart des disciplines scientifiques enseignées  à l'Université de Yaoundé,
2.30 C'est dans ce contexte que le Cameroun attache une importance capitale à l'existence  sur son territoire d'un Centre de collecte des traditions orales et à la création d'un Centre  régional de recherche pour la promotion et le développement du livre, Il estime en effet que  ces deux outils ont une réelle complémentarité et sont ensemble nécessaires pour faire  naître un Camerounais nouveau, capable d'être la symbiose vivante du passé et du présent,  c'est-à-dire prenant appui sur la connaissance de ses ancêtres pour mieux assimiler le sa-  voir du monde contemporain, D'ores et déjà, pour matérialiser cette idée, mon gouverne-  ment a créé à l'Université de Yaoundé un Centre de recherches africanistes, qui accueillera  toutes les activités dans ce domaine.
2.31 Les activités amorcées dans le cadre de la rénovation de l'éducation nous semblent  de nature à intéresser notre Organisation, en raison même de ses interventions passées,  et à l'inciter à mieux soutenir l'effort entrepris, En cela réside notre quête auprès de vous  tous, individuellement et collectivement, afin que se justifie plus que jamais notre foi en  l'Organisation des Nations Unies pour l'éducation, la science et la culture,

(4.1) Mr. ALI ABU NOWAR (Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan) (Translation from the Arabic):  Mr. President, ladies and gentlemen, heads and members of delegations, I should have  liked, as preceding speakers have done, to express my country's opinion on the future plans of  action of our Organization, especially after listening attentively to the excellent comprehensive  introduction with which the Director-General opened the general policy debate. But matters  more important to my country at this stage compel me to concentrate in my brief address on  some of the questions which are directly related to the basic ideals and principles of this Organi-  zation. What indeed are its future programmes and plans but a way to the ultimate realization  of these principles and virtues?
(4.2) Part of my country, Jordan, has been under the military occupation of a member of this  Organization since the aggression of 1967, Occupation, as you know, is bound to be loathsome  to the soul of a people and adverse to all the basic principles and values of civilized societies.  Inherent in this occupation are all the evils of slavery imposed by force of arms; and under its  odious spectre human rights cease to exist,
(4.3) Jordan, in spite of its limited revenue and resources, was achieving rapid progress to-  wards making education available to all without exception. Then, with the 1967 aggression,  thousands of students poured over from the occupied Western part of the country to the East  Bank. The already crowded schools of the East Bank could not cope with these numbers, Faced  with this harsh situation, the Government was compelled to adopt a two-shift system in its main  schools in the cities, and even in some a three-shift system. In other words, ithadto compress  the students of three congested schools into one in spite of the limited educational facilities avail-  able. One of the effects of the evils of occupation was therefore a severe blow at the efforts of  the Ministry of Education to improve the quality of education. In these difficult circumstances  it has become impossible to fulfil the programmes for renewal of education initiated by Unesco.
(4.4) By force of arms the occupation authorities have taken to defining and restricting what  should be taught to the Arab students in the occupied areas. They have falsified facts and events  related to the history of Arab civilization, including its religion. Anything linking the studentto  his true cultural heritage, his cultural environment and his legitimate national aspirations has  been omitted from the textbooks. These are basic components in textbooks and curricula all  over the world. In fact they are essential to the development of the true patriotic feeling of the  student, within the framework of his national culture.
(4.5) Human rights are one of the bases of a large number of Unesco's educational and cultural  programmes and of its guiding principles, such as literacy, adult education, education for all,  life-long education, non-discrimination in education, the universality of science and the diver-  sity of cultures, and the absolute right of every people freely to maintain and to enjoy its national  culture, \Vhen the Universal Declaration of Human Rights was issued, it was not restricted to  one particular people but intended to encompass man at all times and in every corner of our  globe. But for over a quarter of a century Israel has denied the people of Palestine the right to  return to their homes, in accordance with the United Nations resolutions, with all the gross re-  pudiation of human rights inherent in this denial.
(4.6) What can Unesco do in face of this painful reality, this persistent denial of the human rights  of the Palestinian Arab? What can be done by this Organization, which has been entrusted with  the support and preservation of human rights, for over a million Palestinian refugees, excluded  for over a quarter of a century from the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and needing most  urgently to demand these political rights?
(4.7) I shall now turn to another aspect of the evils of occupation: the effacement of the unique  cultural features of Jerusalem, which is an open challenge to the international community and a  rejection of its resolutions. A mere four days after the end of hostilities, Israeli bulldozers  went to work and flattened an entire district of Old Jerusalem in less than a week. This was the  beginning of [srael's extremist expansionist plan to disfigure Jerusalem's cultural features. It  then started excavating all along the western and southern walls of the Haram esh-Sharif, des-  troying some of the historic buildings near the walls and causing grave damage to others, Israel
18  has also confiscated thousands of acres of Arab land, privately-owned and in charitable trust,  has destroyed the buildings on them, and has built huge modern residential blocks.
(4.8) What Israel has already done and continues to do in Jerusalem to change its features is no  longer a secret, because of the sheer size of the conversion projects which have either been  completed or are under way. They aim at encircling the Holy City with towering residential  buildings to hide its original features from sight.
(4.9) In an attempt to stop these frantic plans, Unesco's General Conference adopted at its fif-  teenth session a resolution calling for an end to the excavations and to the defacement of the  cultural features of Jerusalem. As the occupation authorities did not comply with this resolution  as the Executive Board had hoped, it adopted at its 82nd, 83rd, 88th and 89th sessions decisions  calling on them to cease those activities and confirming its previous decisions. Regrettably,  however, these all met the same fate as the volume of resolutions adopted over the past quarter  - of a century by the United Nations and the Security Council.
(4.10) Dozens of petitions and complaints have been submitted to the occupation authorities by  the people of Jerusalem, by national bodies and by prominent local persons. These have all ap-  pealed for a cessation of the measures aimed at changing the cultural features of Jerusalem and  destroying historic buildings and sites. But the occupation authorities, which have imposed  silence on the defenceless Arab population of Palestine by force of arms, have not heeded their  feelings or their rights to home and property. On the contrary, they have taken further measures  towards making occupied Jerusalem Jewish, towards making its own people, its own Arab inhabi-  tants, strangers in the Holy City and making it strange to all those who knew it before its  occupation.
(4.11) I did not wish to embark on a discussion of this subject, but I am compelled to deal with  the question which the Israeli delegate raised yesterday as "dialogue and negotiation’. He re-  ferred to the address of the Soviet delegate, who had said that there had been a détente, an eas-  ing of tension and conflict in the world and a world-wide trend to replace conflict by negotiation,  and that it would be courageous for the parties to the Middle East conflict to move towards nego-  tiation. 1 am bound to agree with what the Soviet delegate said; but at the same time I can only  point out the vast difference between the nature and causes of the Middle Eastern conflict and  conflicts elsewhere. Conflicts in all other regions are caused by political or economic factors,  or by competing colonial influences or regional interests; the Middle Eastern problem is that  of a people uprooted bodily from its homeland and forced to live in exile all over the world. It  is also the problem of a racist, expansionist, colonialist entity with a policy based on aggres-  sion, as experience has proved. Israel has always resorted to falsification of facts and to the  diversion of matters from their true course in international organizations. It has always turned  world attention away from the heart of the problem to its fringes, away from the real core to  formalities. We therefore have little hope that this aggressor State will return to the right path  in order to convince the world that it truly desires peace, and that it wants negotiation to lead to  peace. Had Israel truly desired peace, it would have responded to the world's repeated appeals  at the Security Council at the United Nations or at Unesco. But five resolutions in succession  from this Organization alone have not met with one assent from this State which claims to seek  peace through negotiation.
(2.12) The whole world cannot impose on this aggressor State the proper attitude to right and  to the way to peace, or even to the details of the problems. One of these is obvious to us, a  typical instance of the whole problem: the aggression perpetrated even against the Arab culture  in Jerusalem, following the aggression against an entire people. After all that, this braggart  stands here to say that courage is needed for negotiation and peace. Negotiation with whom?  Negotiation with those who will not listen, not only to their foe, but not even to their closest  friends?” With those who deny the world its right to intervene to preserve human rights, to  succour a people uprooted from their land, and to end the conflict and remove its causes” In  spite of all this, he still comes here to bluster about the need for courage to achieve peace. 1  do not deny that we lack sufficient courage to negotiate in the face of his extremist, aggressive] ~  oppressive attitude, which neither knows nor allows any way to negotiate for understanding on any  of the points at issue, either general or particular. He who seeks to negotiate must admit that  there are rights he has violated, and that he has committed aggression against an innocent people,  before we can accept negotiation with him. ho can call on us to negotiate while the other party  denies and persists in his aggression, while he denies his violation of human rights and calls for  forgiveness, remission and an easing of tension? Have the United Nations, its various agencies,  and the future of the Arab nation all been allocated as a testing-ground for the aggressionist  experiments of this intrusive colonialist element? Does this State, born out of a United Nations
18  resolution, believe that this empowers it to continue to ignore all the resolutions of the Organi-  zation which created it and erected it on the corpses of a peaceful, innocent people? Ifthe world  will accept its moral responsibility for this terrible injustice which it helped to bring upon the  people of Palestine, I do believe that the world's conscience will awaken to this crime and to its  persistence and aggravation - a crime perpetrated againsta people which is in no way to blame  for the sufferings of the Jews at the hands of oppressors such as Hitler or any others in’ Europe  and elsewhere. I believe this to be a sufficient answer to this brcggart aggressor,
(4.13) We in Jordan are proud to belong to this Organization. We are truly happy to take part  in its activities. It is true that Jordan is a small country, with limited material and human re-  sources; yet it appreciates as always the results of the various efforts and activities of this  noble Organization, which have contributed largely to the development of our educational system,  We have tried, and we still try, to collaborate with this Organization in many of its regional and  international activities within the limits of our financial and human resources. We do this be-  cause we have faith in the validity of the Organization! s objectives and in the importance of its  projects, programmes and activities aimed at laying the foundations of understanding among na-  tions and at propagating peace and justice. I have not the slightest doubt that the success of this  Organization up to this day has been achieved through its sound administration and the co-operation  of its Member States,
(4.14) The final point in my address concerns the United Nations Relief and Works Agency (UNRWA),  which was established to express the minimal responsibility felt by the international community  for the Palestine question, This agency now suffers from a budgetary deficit, especially on its  educational services. In the name of Jordan, the country which has borne the burden of the in-  ternational community's mistakes over tne Palestine question, I appeal to you to give increased  financial support to this agency, which is fulfilling an important duty in the absence of the appli-  cation of the United Nations resolutions.

6.1 Mr. MAGNUS TORFI OLAFSSON (Iceland):  Mr, President, distinguished delegates, may I, first of all, congratulate you, Mr,  President, on your election to this high office, I wish you continuous success in your du-  ties leading this importance Conference.
6.2 On behalf of the Icelandic delegation, I would like to speak in this general debate es-  pecially about some areas of the programme of Unesco which are of intrinsic interest to  the Government of Iceland, as well as about some general policy matters which we view  as important.
6. 3 In the field of education we have noted in particular the increased emphasis placed on  the right to life-long integrated education and on the expansion of adult education, as well  as the development of modern educational methods, materials, and techniques, and the  promotion of educational planning, both of which will receive considerably increased UNDP  support. These priorities appear well chosen, On the one hand, it remains a top priority  for our Organization to promote human rights and dignity through education offered to all,  In this respect, particular stress should be put on studying the socio-economic and cultural  barriers which still prevent large groups of the world's population froin developing through  education, thus barring them from adequate participation in the economic and political pro-  cesses of their respective societies, The afcrementioned priorities in Unesco's draft ed-  ucation programme stress, on the other hand, the need for bridging the gap between theories  of learning and educational practice, through well-planned research, interdisciplinary ef-  fort and enlightened educational policy-making,
6. 4 With respect to international educational co-operation my Government considers it  very important that the work of Unesco should be adequately co-ordinated with that of re-  gional organizations like the Council of Europe and the Organization for Economic Co-  operation and Development, which are also developinginternational co-operation in several
617   fields of education, especially educational innovation. For the governments of small Mem-  ber States it is obviously very important that the programmes and projects of these organi-  zations are so co-ordinated that duplication of effort is avoided,
6,5 Turning to the natural sciences and their application to development, I would like to  dwell on Unesco's work in the field of oceanography. The work of the International Oceano-  graphic Commission (IOC) is becoming increasingly important in view of the rapid deterio-  ration of the marine environment, The marine environment is affected in many ways, and  the ecological balance is threatened by a variety of factors, Although we still lack adequate  knowledge of fundamental aspects of the physical, chemical and biological working of the  ocean, we know that these relationships are delicately balanced, Marine life is inter-  connected in the system of interrelated food chains which ultimately depend on the chemical  situation in the marine environment, Thus for instance the greatest long-term danger from  marine pollution lies in its potential for upsetting the ecological balance of the oceans, in  such a way that destruction of marine life may become the rule rather than the exception,  As an example it may be mentioned that oysters have been found to amplify small concen-  trations of DDT 70, 000 times in a month, The terrifying implications of such dangers are  only too evident, Dumping of noxious materials in great quantities into the ocean is bound  to lead to disastrous consequences, It is thus fervently to be hoped that the prospective  anti-dumping conference to be held in London will be successful,
6.6 We in Iceland, because of our overwhelming dependence upon the living resources of  the sea, tend to look upon hydrographic and marine biological research from a very prac-  tical point of view, Our oceanographic research has increased rapidly in recent years,  but it is heavily geared to the solution of immediate practical problems, There is no doubt  that the hydrographers can help the fishermen in many ways, and to our mind the impor-  tance of hydrographical research to practical fisheries is well demonstrated in the 70- year-  old history of the International Council for the Exploration of the Sea, In my country hy-  drographers are working in close connexion with marine biologists to investigate the de-  pendence of marine organisms on the physical and chemical properties of the sea, Fluc-  tuations in the marine environment do have a profound influence on the abundance of life  in the sea and the knowledge of these changes is of fundamental importance for the evaluation  of the fluctuations in the great fisheries. Admittedly, the degree of depletion of the most  important fish stocks in the North- Atlantic is man-made and can only be solved by drastic  reduction in fishing effort, but we must make use of marine science to help us disentangle  the various factors in this complex matter,
6.7 To our mind IOC can make a valuable contribution to the fisheries in the developing  areas by promoting oceanographic research in order to use this knowledge to improve the  catches of the fishermen in these waters and make their fisheries more profitable,
6.8 The work of IOC in the area of marine pollution is of great interest to us and we note  with satisfaction that IOC activities covered by the Long-Term and Expanded Programme  on Oceanic Research (LEPOR) have been considerably increased. And the Action Plan of  the Stockholm Conference recommends additional tasks for IOC. In connexion with the  Global Investigation of Pollution in the Marine Environment, one of the major LEPOR pro-  grammes, an international co-ordination group has been established, and concerning marine  pollution monitoring, IOC has recognized that a programme could be developed as a func-  tion of the Integrated Global Ocean Station System, Preparations for the Ocean Data Acqui-  sition System seem to be well under way, and there is additional progress in many other  fields, All this is of great importance and my Government fully supports these endeavours,  G.9 It is also worth mentioning here that my Government is considering plans to develop  further our research on sea-ice in Arctic waters, We are grateful to Unesco for having,  during the present biennium, supported an international conference on sea-ice convened by  us in Reykjavik, We are interested in seeing international co-operation develop further in  this field under the auspices of IOC, and we have reason to believe that several other coastal  States of the northern Atlantic may also be interested in such co-operative research efforts,
6.10 Some attention has been given to amending the IOC structure to enable it to handle  its growing responsibilities, and a working group is studying this problem, Asfarasmy  Government is concerned, we support the idea that the work of IOC should be strengthened  and co-ordinated with the work of other organs and agencies in order to achieve the greatest  possible collaborative results, Such use of existing bodies should form the basis for assess-  ing any plans or proposals for the establishment of new agencies, For instance it might be  possible to establish closer co-operation with the prospective United Nations Department  of Human Environment,
6.11 The Chairman of IOC said at the last meeting of the preparatory committee for the  law of the sea conference, that the pursuit of more profound knowledge of the oceans re-  quires that all ocean-oriented countries .pool their expertise and experience in the fight  for a safer, cleaner, healthier and more productive marine environment, My Govern-  ment hopes that this good advice will be accepted as a guiding light by all concerned,
6. 12 We have noted with satisfaction that the draft social sciences, humanities and culture  programme of Unesco has been rather strongly expanded, In this sector of the programme,  ‘as well as the communication sector, Unesco has an extremely important part to play. As  a matter of fact the universality of Unesco's membership is of particular importance for  these spheres of the Organization's activities.
6.13 In this sector of the programme there is one item which is especially appreciated by  us, that is the translation and publication of representative works of little-known literatures  and of works by contemporary authors written in languages not widely spoken (paragraph
3219 of the Draft Programme and Budget), First and foremost, it should be emphasized  that this item of the programmeis particularly important for the developing Member States,  whose literary heritage and modern literature.are unfortunately quite insufficiently known  in other countries, For us Icelanders this programme has-already been of welcome help  in the publication of representative works of our ancient literature in a world language, We  remain interested in further co-operation with the translations programme of Unesco which,  we note with satisfaction, has obtained a significantly increased budgetary allocation in the  draft budget,
6.14 In conclusion, Mr. President, let me express the hope that we may see Unesco develop  further and steadily as an agent for the strengthening of peace and equality. The vocation  of Unesco as a promoter of culture and international co-operation, and the universality of  its membership, give this Organization of ours unique possibilities for increasing mütual  knowledge and deepening mutual respect for each other, thus enhancing human rights and  dignity. Unesco is even endowed with unique possibilities for becoming in a way our con-  science, a constant spur to effort and new achievement, Thank you,

(8.1) Dr. AHMED ABDUL SATTAR AL-JAWARY (Iraq) (Translation from the Arabic):  Mr. Presideni, Mr. Chairman of the Executive Board, heads and members of delegations,  ladies and gentlemen, it gives me great pleasure to congratulate the President on the unanimity  of his election to this important office. We have great confidence in his ability to discharge the  vital duties with which he has been entrusted, and in the success of the General Conference.
(8.2) It also gives me great pleasure to welcome the States which have become members of the  Organization since the last session of the General Conference. The absence of the People's Re-  public of China for over two decades has been a source of amazement and of allegations that this  Organization did not fully represent the peoples of the world. Now that this great State has joined  our Ovganization, it will certainly play an important part in our activities, and its attitude to-  wards the national liberation causes will certainly influence the policy of the Organization, I  should also like to welcome the four Arab States Bahrain, Oman, Qatar and the United Arab  Emirates, whose admission marks a definite step forward.
(8.3) Last year we celebrated the 25th anniversary of the foundation of Unesco, which through  its achievements and its untiring efforts has now emerged from its infancy. Thisthrows aheavy  burden and weighty responsibilities on it at a time when numerous problems call for urgent so-  lution and the ability of international organizations to solve them is questioned.
(8.4) I must commend the Director-General and his collaborators for the ability they have  demonstrated in the preparation of the documents for this Conference, in particular the two main  documents 17 C/4 (the Draft Medium- Term Outline Plan) and 17 C/5 (the Draft Programme and  Budget), which deal with the Organization's programmes and trends. I! must also praise the  Executive Board for its study of these documents and its valuable comments on them. This is  the second time that we find this two-stage planning of the activities of the Organization, and I  believe that the experiment has, on the whole, proved successful. It gives a clear view of the  program:nes' objectives and trends over a convenient period of time, and facilitates the partici-  pation of the Member States, within and outside the framework of the General Conference, in de-  fining the general policy of the Organization and in ensuring that this shall be reflected in its  programmes,
(8.5) 1 should like to discuss a number of the principal issues in the general programmes policy,  the first of which is the growth of the Organization's programmes and activities. Inthis connexion  we must recall a number of international problems, especially as we are at the outset of the Sec-  ond Development Decade. It is no secret that the gap between the rich and the poor nations con-  tinues to widen, a phenomenon which is not in the interest of anyone. The participation of the  rich States in the development programmes is dictated by their own interests. In this connexion  we must remember a number of basic facts. One of these is that the rich States' contributions  to development has not yet reached 1% of their national income; and in fact the share of their  public sectors in particular is on the decline. Most of this contribution is made within the frame-  work of bilateral aid and the financial and political conditions and restrictions so often connected  with it. Bilateral aid amounts to approximately seventimes the aid received through international  organizations. The education and science sectors are still not receiving their proper share of  international aid. These are facts to which attention has been drawn by international reports,  such as the Pearson Report, and the Report of the International Commission on the Development  of Education now before the Conference.
(8.6) These facts induce us to take a positive view of the growth rate of the programmes. We  believe that the proposal of the Director-General, endorsed by the Executive Board, to plan  within an 8% rate in the biennium 1975-1976 and 7. 5% in that of 1977-1978 for the operating budget  represents the minimum which must be maintained in the light of the preceding basic considera-  tions. '
(8.7) Approval of the budget ceiling proposed for the coming biennium by the Director-General  is © logical outcome of this view, We are pleased that this has in fact been accomplished, des-  pite the opposition of several rich States which can in fact contribute to the activities of this Or-  ganization without overburdening themselves. Our confidence in this view of the growth of the  programme in general and the Regular Budget in particular is enhanced by the current increase  in extra-budgetary resources, especially for education. This requires increased efficiency in  the intellectual and administrative ranks of the Secretariat, and an expansion of the scope ofits  services to the Member States. It would be unwise of the Secretariat to shrink from fulfilling  those tasks which could be accomplished with the extra-budgetary resources. Such a renuncia-  tion would be likely to induce a deep feeling of regret and disappointment in the developing States.
(8.8) In this respect we support the direction of the programmes towards operational activities;  for this accords with the needs of the developing States and involves a selection of projects be-  fitting their various conditions and situations. What could be better than that this trend should  grow during the Second Development Decade? Moreover, closer attention by many developing  States to an integrated attack on their problems and to the introduction of radical changes in  their situations calls for closer attention to the quality of aid and its adaptation to the conditions  and needs of the developing States, so that they may be enabled to make proper use ofitinaccord-  ance with their needs. Here national programming and the provision of high-level consultants  at the request of Member States are both appropriate measures. Many developing States no  longer need piecemeal aid, but rather comprehensive integrated project: which stimulate national  efforts and depend on them, It is essential to study methods of fulfilling these aims accurately and  in detail, relaxing the restrictions and conditions attached to approval of the projects by the fi-  nancing agencies, reducing interest rates on loans and credits, improving the efficiency of in-  ternational staff, and ensuring the soundness of their attitude towards tie developing States.  {8.9) The education sector will continue to take priority inabsolute figures, since its programmes  are essential and meet pressing needs of the developing States. Attempts to increase the growth  rate of the sectors of natural science and of the social and cultural sciences should be accom-  panied by efforts to enable the developing States to participate effectively in their programmes.  This applies particularly to international programmes in the natural sciences sector, whichare  based on sound intellectual principles and are likely to be of great benefit both to the advance-  ment of knowledge and to the promotion of international scientific co-operation among govern-  ments, international non-governmental organizations and individual scientists. But the partici-  pation of the developing States in these programmes must be assured, and the means to achieve this  must be studied, We believe that success of these international programmes would confirm the Uni-  ted Nations Specialized Agencies as the bodies to be entrusted with th? overall problems facing the  international community, subject to sound intellectual preparation by well-established organiza-  tions dedicated to them. We observe a widening scope in the MAB and UNISIST programmes for  participation and contribution by the social sciences. This healthy trend is also observed in  other intersectoral programmes, such as those on youth and population.
(8.10) There is a tangible improvement in the preparation of the programmes of the education  sector: in defining their objectives, restructuring their machinery, and selecting their scope  and contents. \Ve join the Director-General in paying tribute to the Assistant Director-General  for this sector and his collaborators. We are especially concerned with renewing educational  systems and adapting them to the employment and development needs of the various communities.  We welcome the adaptation of agricultural education to the conditions of rural environments; and  we support the establishment of a regional agricultural education project for the Arab States  which would develop this type of education and harmonize it with development requirements. We  also welcome the greater attention given to improvement of science teaching, technical educa-  tion and teacher training. These trends meet the needs of many devaloping States and would  help to redress the imbalance in some educational systems such as those of the Arab States. In  this respect we reaffirm the importance of the Regional Centre for Educational Planning and  Administration in the Arab States, believing that it has important and badly-needed functions  which should not be ignored or abandoned.
(8.11) I must praise the valuable report prepared by the International Commission on the Devel-  opment of Education, headed by Mr. Edgar Faure, and express our appreciation of the great ef-  forts exerted by the Commission, which has fulfilled its task most satisfactorily, It has madea  comprehensive survey encompassing education's historical dimensions, its rôle in shaping the  future, the dependence of its content and methods on the great progress of knowledge and its in-  teraction with society. The Commission has made a very thorough and searching analysis of  present trends in education, It has underlined the strivings for progress, exposed the elements  of weakness and confusion, and called for a radical renewal of educational systems based on re-  search and study and on the application of modern techniques. This renewal, it said, should go  beyond the traditional methods with a view to bridging the gap between education and the needs  of society and the individual for overall development. It has called for the creation of an en-  lightened community whose members would have access to continuous self-education to com-  plete their personality and enable them to follow the great changes, and participate in the pro-  gress of knowledge and society. It has reaffirmed the need for offshore international co-  operation and solidarity in education, so closely bound to the future and well-being of mankind.
(8.12) This report will certainly remain an important document which will be studied carefully  by experts and consulted by governments. It constitutes an important fund of experience and  wisdom and is a source of inspiration for the planning and implementation of educational policies.  I should perhaps conclude these comments by supporting its closing recommendation, in which  it calls for the establishment of an international programme for the renewal of education, to be  associated with Unesco but to surpass it in material and human resources. This proposal de-  serves study and development, and may well become one of the Organization's prominent pro-  grammes in the near future.
(8.13) lhe attention now devoted to Unesco publications and their increase, wider distribution  and greater accessibility coincides with International Book Year. There has been fruitful and  satisfactory co-operation to bring the Year to a successful conclusion. In this connexion I may  recall the resolutions adopted at previous sessions of this Conference to make Arabic a work-  ing language of the Organization, Besides the tribute this decision pays to the cultures of the  Third World, its implementation will bring Unesco's message to tens of millions of readers of  Arabic in many African and Asian countries, I should dearly like to see an expansion of the  project to include more translation of Unesco publications into Arabic. They contain a wealth  of experience and of information on the progress of knowledge which would make them most  valuable in countries which still need books and studies readily available to their reading public  inside and outside conventional institutions.
(8.14) It is natural that countries with deep-rooted civilizations, like the Arab countries,  should care for the preservation of their cultural heritage and the protection of their monuments  and cultural property. In this connexion we are concerned that the Regional Centre for training  specialists in the preservation of monuments in the Arab States, established by a resolution  adopted at the sixteenth session of the General Conference, should receive continued support and  overcome the financial difficulties with which it is faced.
(8.15) In conclusion, I must discuss certain current cultural problems and the Organization's  relation to them. We are confronted with racial discrimination, colonialist and settlement  campaigns, disregard of human rights and an aggravation of oppression and aggression in Africa  and in Asia, including Palestine, the heart of the Arab World, This Organization has attempted  to take charge of the education of the children of Palestine refugees in conformity with their na-  tional, social and linguistic background, and to protect cultural property in the occupied terri-
625   tories, to uphold the principles of The Hague Convention, and to preserve human rights. The  Isreali occupation authorities, however, continue to disregard these principles and resolutions.  Despite the civilized world's condemnation of the criminal plot to burn the Agsa Mosque, the  occupation authorities are continuing to excavate its surroundings as well as other parts of  Jerusalem and other historic sites in Palestine. They are robbing its monuments, obliterating  its features and distorting and defacing the Holy City.
(8.18) Several days ago the Secretary-General of the United Nations distributed the report ofthe  International Commission on the situation of human rights in the territories occupied by Israel,  The report reveals the violation inflicted by the Israeli occupation authorities on the most basic  human rights: the forced mass migration of populations, the confiscation of their land and homes  and the settlement in these of intruding strangers, and acts of terrorism threatening their peace  and security. All these acts have been reported in the world press. They remind us of the Nazi  and Fascist extermination campaigns, and reveal Zionism as an expansionist racialist move-  ment based on aggression, whose danger is not limited solely to Palestine and the Arab World  but threatens the entire world. In this the voracity of colonialism gives it constant support, es-  pecially ‘merican imperialism, which supplies it with arms and funds and prompts its aggression.
(8.17) If this Organization seeks to solve the problems of contemporary civilization, it has in  Zionism supported by imperialism, a typical example of racial discrimination, expansionism,  tyrannical force and oppression, and disregard for human rights. Zionism, in its excess of  arrogance, defies the civilized world and the international organizations, whose resolutions it  disregards. Itis amovement which imperils world peace. lt mustbe unmasked and its aggressive-  ness revealed, No efforts of the United Nations or its Specialized Agencies will help until the  source of aggression is uprooted and peace and justice are established among the nations on  justice, right and fraternity among men.

10, 1 Mr, NURUL HASAN (India):  Mr, President and distinguished delegates, this General Conference is of particular  significance to Asia, After a lapse of sixteen years, it has elected a distinguished Asian  and the representative of a great culture as its President, 1 join the other delegations in  rejoicing at your election, Mr. President,
10. 2 It is also significant for us that the People's Republic of Bangladesh has been admitted  to membership of this great Organization, The talented and highly cultured people of that  country will, I have no doubt, make a valuable contribution to the functioning of our Organi-  zation, After the devastation which they unfortunately suffered last year, they need the co-  operation of this great world body in the reconstruction of their institutions of education,  science and culture, I extend to them the warm welcome of my delegation,
10. 3 We are also glad that the People's Republic of China, for whose representation we  have been pressing for so long, has secured its rightful place in this Organization, We  have great pleasure too in having the distinguished representatives from Bahrain, Oman,  Qatar and the United Arab Emirates among our colleagues at this Conference.
10. + Mr. President, you have rightly honoured the memory of the late King Mahendra who  was the great leader of our close neighbour, Nepal, with whom we have the friendliest of  ties. Ile was also a distinguislred poet and a patron of learning and cuiture, The world is  poorer for his passing, =
10.5 Your illustrious predecessor, Mr. President, His Excellency Dr, Atilio Dell'Oro  Maini, has made his own valuable contribution to the work of this Organization, I should  like also to express our continuing appreciation of the purposeful leadership provided by  the Director-General, Mr. René Maheu, to Unesco, We greatly esteem his dynamism,  His sense of commitment has been a major factor in making this Organization an effective  instrument for world co-operation,
10.6 During the last two years, a compatriot of ours, Dr, Prem Kirpal, has served as  the Chairman of the Executive Board. We are grateful to the members of the Board for the  trust and confidence they have reposed in him, This is fitting recognition of the long and  dedicated service which Dr. Kirpal has rendered to the ideals and objectives of Unesco.
10. 7 In keeping with the objectives of the United Nations my country is sparing no effort to  work for the cause of peace. Fortunately, the wind of change is already blowing over South  Asia and a sincere effort is being made to resolve differences by peaceful means through  negotiations.
10.8 Mr. President, the need for securing co-operation among the peoples of the worldis  a continuing one. It is also among Unesco's foremost obligations and has received due rec-  ognition at successive sessions of the General Conference. At the last session, you will  recall, a comprehensive resolution was passed calling upon all Member States actively to  oppose colonialism, neo-colonialism, racialism, fascism and all forms of oppression and  tyranny. I should like to invite attention too to situations where the industrial and scien-  tific effort required for developmental activities tend to make them dependent on the sup-  ‘port which is provided by the advanced countries. It is necessary that studies in depth be  undertaken of the emerging forms of neo-colonialism, particularly their manifestation in  the spheres of education, science, technology and culture, We ought to examine also the  way religious and racial fanaticism is used to sow the seeds of dissension and hatred among  people, These dangerous tendencies have to be effectively combated. It is obvious that  unless the necessary intellectual and physical effort is made to build peace and fight col-  onialism, neo- colonialism, bigotry and racialism, our task will not be fulfilled.
10.9 Unesco should aim at encouraging and developing the potential of the developing coun-  tries to help themselves. It should do so by helping them to build their scientific and tech-  nical infrastructure and motivating the scientists and technologists to find solutions to their  local problems and become innovators. Unless this is done the transfer of expertise and  technology may become self-defeating, the gap may increase, and the dependence of devel-  oping countries on advanced countries may increase instead of being reduced,
10.10 It is now well recognized, Mr. President, that the function of education, science  and culture is to enrich the quality of life of the broad masses of the people. Unfortunately,  in most of the developing countries, the common people are subjected to one form of depri-  vation or another leading, on the one hand, to poverty, disease and exploitation and, on the  other, to ignorance, superstition and obscurantism. The sixteenth session, Mr. President,  adopted a resolution relating to the funding of the First and Second Development Decades.  The resolution was addressed, in particular, to the economically and technically advanced  countries. It recommended a contribution of at least 1% of the Gross National Product to-  wards assistance to the developing countries. This vital resolution was adopted both by the  General Assembly of the United Nations and the General Conference of Unesco. Unfortu-  nately, the level of assistance continues to fall far short of this. Enlightened global co-  operation in this all important matter, I need hardly stress, should be of benefit as much  to the developing as the developed countries.
10.11 We feel that there is need for the development of effective co-operation among the  developing countries themselves. We believe Unesco can do much to bring this about. We  can see much scope for such collaboration in the Asian region. There are many fields in-  deed in which we can visualize substantial benefits. The countries inthe region have attained  a high level of culture and also developed their own scientific and technological infrastrue-  ture. It should be possible to pool and share these experiences and facilities. On behalfof  my country, may I say in all humility, that we shall be happy to place such facilities and  expertise as we have at the disposal of our neighbours in this region? Promotion of mutual  co-operation between the developing countries, we feel, should receive high priority in the  programmes of Unesco.
10.12 In considering the different programme sectors of Unesco, emphasis should be laid,
18  I believe, on education, science and culture as means for self-generating, national devel-  ment. While a Copernican revolution in education is certainly needed, the philosophy of  the new strategies would need to be reoriented to face the challenges of the present times.  We hope to see the creation of a link between education, productivity and research. Also,  the development of scientific’ and technological education has to be made more broad-based  by incorporating work-experience as an integral part of the process. Vocationalization  again should be an important element in education. Other reforms, such as revision of  curricula, new teaching methods, the use of modern educational technology based on mass  media of communication, and much needed improvements in the system of evaluation, are  also urgently called for. It has to be realized, at the same time, that the study of the hu-  manities and social sciences cannot afford to remain, as it has tended to do all too long,  remote from science and technology. This has already been seen to create a dichotomy  between ''two culture ‘". There has to be a new pattern, a fresh blend of continuing life-  long education, with proper integration of the several disciplines, leading to a single uni-  fied stream of culture and values. This integrated effort should indeed inform education  at all levels, from the primary right up to the university stage. It should enrich the life  of the entire community. While both institutional and non-institutional arrangements, alike  for formal and informal education, should be available to everyone according to his talent,  need and aptitude, the facilities provided should certainly be such as to secure improve-  ment in the quality of education and life, It is from this point of view that my delegation  welcomes the publication of the recommendations of the International Commission on the  Development of Education, which merit careful consideration by Member States and the  Unesco Secretariat, In this fast changing world, dynamism and innovationare fundamental  to progress: we should be prepared to think far ahead to ensure that the educational sys-  tem continuously responds to the changing needs of society, so that it can serve the objec-  tives of socio-economic equality, humanism and universal enlightenment.
10.13 It is from the same point of view, again, Mr. President, that we welcome the pro-  posal to establish an Asian Centre for Educational Innovation for Development (ACEID).  We note that Unesco's regional institutions in Asia established in the education sector,  since the 1962 session of the General Conference, will close down by the end of this year:  their activities are to be taken up by national institutions established by Member States.  We would expect Unesco to assist these successor centres in specific programmes, using  UNDP funds. A satisfactory relationship, we hope, will be established between the pro-  posed ACEID and the national centres, We also hope that this Asian Centre will work  through these national centres, using their accumulated experience and expertise and giv-  ing them full assistance in continuing their international programmes. We welcome, too,  the proposal to establish an International University, structured on the federal principle,  functionally and geographically decentralized, and devoted to the promotion of higher learn-  ing and research on problems reflecting the world's urgent needs and our main tasks. Ve  expect that the new University, when brought into being, will make full use of the existing  national institutions of excellence in all regions, and particularly in the developing countries.
10.14 Mr. President, we feel that definitive action in regard to youth programmes is  needed, Young people, the world over, have to be enabled to find their identity and meet  the challenges of the time. We are all aware of the importance of participation by both  student and non-student youth in programmes of national development and international  understanding and co-operation. There is need for inculcation of robust personal and so-  cial values in them, through proper vocational, scientific, cultural and social education  and by affording them opportunity to render meaningful service to society. My delegation,  therefore, welcomes the programmes outlined by the Director-General; these should be ’  substantially strengthened.
10.15 In the sphere of natural sciences, Mr. President, my delegation warmly welcomes  the proposal to hold the Conference on the Application of Science and Technology to Africa.  At the same time, I must express our sense of disappointment at the results achieved  following the Conference on the Application of Science and Technology to Asia which was  held in New Delhi in 1968. Several steps were suggested on that occasion to strengthen
628   the development of science and technology in Asia. I am not aware of any significant work  having been done to implement these decisions. Among other things, wé lay great emphasis  on the need for multidisciplinary research centres in developing countries for optimum uti-  lization of scarce resources. We would urge the Director-General to undertake a phased  programme for establishing such centres in the Asian region,
10.16 We welcome, Mr. President, the programmes for the International Hydrological  Decade, the Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission and Man and the Biosphere.  We attach great importance to the continuation of these programmes and to our participa-  tion in them, but it would be desirable to think of ways and means to avoid duplication and  overlap and to ensure the implementation of these programmes in an integrated and co-  ordinated manner. My delegation, as a party to the declaration adopted at the recent  Stockholm Conference on Environment, would, at the same time, wish to see greater em-  phasis placed on the study of man and his environment generally - not indeed so much in  the sense of theoretical ecological inquiry alone, as to bring out guidelines for concrete  action to restore better environmental balance throughout the world. I cannot express  , myself better than to quote what the Prime Minister of India, Shrimati Indira Gandhi  pointed out at the Stockholm Conference: ''On the one hand the rich look askance at our  continuing poverty - on the other they warn us against their own methods. We do not wish  to impoverish the environment any further and yet we cannot for a moment forget the grim  poverty of large numbers of people. Are not poverty and need the greatest polluters? For  instance, unless we are in a position to provide employment and purchasing power for the  daily necessities of the tribal people and those who live in or around our jungles, we cannot  prevent them from combing the forest for food and livelihood, from poaching and from des-  poiling the vegetation. When they themselves feel deprived, how can we urge the preserva-  tion of animals? How can we speak to those who live in villages and in slums about keep-  ing the oceans, the rivers and the air clean when their own lives are contaminated at the  source? The environment cannot be improved in conditions of poverty. Nor can poverty  be eradicated without the use of science and technology. Must there be conflict between  technology and a truly better world or between enlightenment of the spirit and a higher  standard of living?" ’
10.17 Socio-economic underdevelopment itself spells a threat to man's environment. At  the same time, ostentatious consumption in affluent societies leads to misuse of natural  resources and prevents their proper recycling, so vital to man's future, Unesco has a  clear rôle here. The reasons for the current state of affairs have to be probed. Apart  from other factors, the value system which underlies the contemporary civilization of  science and technology has to be studied, analysed and evaluated. False standards and  perspectives have to be set right. Unesco must consciously spark off research and action  on the quality of life, with a view to formulating correctives to the aberrations of our age.  The quest for a design for living is at once a challenge and an opportunity.
10.18 Mr. President, I should like to refer at this stage to the field of culture which con-  stitutes one of Unesco's major missions. The Organization is already pledged to the ideals  of preservation of national cultures and the protection of cultural property. My country  has been closely associated with measures of international co-operation for the conserva-  tion of cultural property. We have had the honour of participating, inter alia, in the pro-  grammes for the protection of the Nubian and Philae monuments, and in the restoration  work at Bamian. We are gratified to note that a beginning has been made for restoration  of the monuments of Borobudor and Syria; we look forward to contributing to these pro-  grammes as well,
10.19 Development of culture in the past, Mr. President, has transcended the present  boundaries of nation States. A study of cultures, therefore, postulates participation by  scholars from all countries which were in contact with each other in the course of the  evolution of their national cultures. It is good that Unesco has taken up a study of the cul-  tures and civilizations of Central Asia and is taking up further studies of the Malaysian and  other cultures. Having been closely connected with the cultures of Central, Western and
18  South East Asian countries, as well as East African countries, we would like similar cul-  tural studies to be undertaken in each of these regions as well, with full participation of  the countries in this entire area,
10.20 The preservation of cultural property, Mr. President, is a question of great intrin-  sic importance, Cultural property constitutes one of the basic elements of each national  culture and indeed of civilization as a whole. We believe that every Member State has be-  come conscious of its moral obligation to respect the cultural heritage of others as wellas  its own, Addressing itself to the problem, the last session of the General Conference  already adopted a Convention on the ineans of prohibiting and preventing illicit import, ex-  port and transfer of ownership of cultural property. We, in India, are actively seized of  the issue and are examining our legal and administrative procedures with a view to ratify-  ing the Convention shortly. But we realize that the Convention will have no value unless it  is ratified by majority of the Member States, particularly the developed countries, I would  go a step further. Theft and smuggling of cultural property is a real threat to cultures to-"  day, especially in the developing countries. May I suggest that the Director-General con-  sider the feasibility of setting up an appropriate committee or commission to ensure imple-  mentation of the Convention? .
10, 21 No less important are certain other measures needed to maintain the integrity of  cultural traditions. These traditions, in developing countries in particular, are in danger  of being swamped by practices and value systems of the affluent world. Recent extraord-  inary developments in mass media of communication and enhanced mobility often tend, in  subtle ways, to weaken the vitality of such cultures, This can create many dangers and  tensions, At a time when traditional cultures are struggling to come to terms with the  new scientific temper of the age, it is an important duty to butiress those elements of the  cultural tradition which can intermingle with and humanize science and technology. What  is healthy and valuable in national cultures, including oral traditions, must be preserved.  Unesco has to recognize the threat, locate it and assist in the struggle for maintenance of  cultural integrity.
10,22 It is also our experience, Mr. President, that the present flow of information be-  tween countries is massively in one direction - from the developed to the developing world.  Major international news disseminating organizations are owned by the developed countries.  The installed facilities for communication also act in favour of the developed areas. Even  television communication reaches many developing countries via the developed countries -  understandably at considerable expense to the former. Measures should be initiated to  overcome these difficulties. This would form part appropriately of Unesco's efforts in the  field of transmission of information on a global basis and could be undertaken in consulta-  tion with the International Telecommunications Union.
10.23 Another recommendation I should like to make relates to the field of mass communi-  cation. We are all aware that mass media, especially in their operation beyond national  boundaries, have tended sometimes to intensify rather than reduce tension; they have also  tended to erode traditional cultures in the name of entertainment, news reporting, and propa-  ganda. My delegation would like to see the adoption of the proposed declaration in this con-  nexion which could assure prior consent of the countries at the receiving end of satellite  and other broadcasts, and the formulation of a code of ethics for operators and manage-  ments handling mass media internationally.
10.24 Our delegation, Mr. President, had occasion to express its views earlier on the  budget and some of the administrative and financial procedures of Unesco. I should like to  offer some comments at this stage on certain other aspects of the two documents 17 C/4  and 17 C/5. My Government and the Indian National Commission for co-operation with  Unesco have studied the Draft Medium-Term Outline Plan with interest and have reflected  over the detailed recommendations made by the Executive Board. We find that the docu-  ment in many places is merely a linear projection of the Short-Term Programme and  Budget. Its approach is also frequently vague and over-generalized. My delegation would
18  propose that, as from the next biennium, the two documents should be amalgamated and  presented as a single document setting out the medium-term strategy and the detailed con-  ‘sequential biennial programme of action. oo :

These would necessarily remain an ongoing activity. But it would be better to  avoid proliferations of programmes otherwise. :

We wish to see Unesco fulfil the ideals set out so eloquently in the preamble to its Consti-  tution. It is our belief that the world can go forward only on the basis of the closest co-  operation between nations and peoples. We consider that Unesco has an abiding rôle to  perform. Its contribution over the coming years, I am sure, will be of progressively  greater value and importance to the cause of peace and humanity, Thank you, Mr. President,

12.3 Mr. President, I bear to this Conference the greetings of the President of the Supreme  Revolutionary Council of the Somali Democratic Republic, who, fully aware of the vital role  of Unesco in development, peace and international goodwill, sends this Conference his best  wishes for success, on behalf of the Government and the people.
12.4 Mr. President, my delegation firmly believes that the overriding issue of our time,  on which all other issues such as peace, democracy, stability, hinge is the issue of how to  bring about a balanced development within the community of nations, of how to close the  so-called gap between the developing nations and the developed nations. Ih the final analysis,  Unesco is called upon to address itself to the resolution of this issue through programmes  in education, science and culture, and its success will be measured largely by the extent  to which its activities and programmes contribute to the resolution of this issue.
12.5 I must pay tribute to the Director-General for his excellent report last week in which  he evinced a deep concern about the very serious and urgent developmental problems facing  the developing nations and a conviction that Unesco must dedicate itself to the solution of  these problems. My delegation shares with the Director-General this sense of urgency. I
18  think I could not be accused of being a pessimist if I were to say that we are racing against  time and we appear to be losing, The task before Unesco in this regard is enormous and  most urgent and the resources available are limited. What, then must be done? Of course  it is essential that constant eftorts be made to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of  the Organization so that maximum mileage can be achieved with resources already avail-  able, In this regard, we believe that it is essential that the Organization be programme-  oriented in its expenditure patterns and that over-head administrative expenses be subord-  inated to expenditure on programmes - without of course sacrificing the Organization's ad-  ministrative capability to carry out these programmes.
12,6 It is also essential that the Organization concentrate its activities on the major and  more immediate development problems, Above all, however, Mr. President, the Member  States should be prepared to accept the notion that Unesco must necessarily grow if it is  not to stagnate and eventually become defunct. No nation, regardless of how developed it  may be considered today, can ever realize its full potential unless all nations first attaina  decent level of development. \Ve maintain that the yearnings of mankind for peace and sta-  bility will remain just that, as long as such a huge proportion of mankind is prey to abject  poverty, debilitating ignorance and ravaging diseases. In the final analysis, the develop-  mental problems of developing nations are not theirs alone; they are also the problems of  the developed nations which must, therefore, show more concern about these problems  than has been the case so far, This notwithstanding, Mr. President, my Government and  my people are firm believers in the principle of self-reliance and self-help. \Ve believe  that no amount of external aid can help a nation solve its problems unless it mobilizes its  resources, both human and material, to help itself; unless it first takes its own destiny  in its own hands; unless it takes the initiative and daringly formulates its own solutions to  its own problems in accordance with an ideology conducive to rapid development. This is  one of the basic principles of our socialist revolution and we apply it in every sphere of our  construction programme,
12.7 The development problems which Unesco is called upon to do something effective  about, are not only urgent but often complex, they often defy traditional methods of solu-  tion and call for radically new methods which must therefore be invented. The application  of science to the developmental problems in developing countries should be viewed some-  what differently from the application of science to the problems of developed countries for  the problems are different and the path for the latter, unlike that for the former, has been  largely blazed. It seems to me that science has yet to come to grips with the unique prob-  lems in developing nations, It is not often that solutions and technologies devised for the  developed nations are immediately applicable to the solution of problems in developing na-  tions. In the area of science as well as in other areas, the Organization must at all times  remain creative and innovative in its programmes and activities, Any Organization, par-  ticularly an international organization, becomes, with the passage of time, susceptible to  the encroachments of bureaucratic traditions which are not necessarily sound, to apathy  and to the tendency to fit solutions to problems into comfortable preconceived patterns.  Unesco must constantly be on its guard against these encroachments on its vitality and  creativity.
12.8 Mr. President, the great socialist revolution which took place in my country on
21 October 1969 under the leadership of Jaaile Mohamed Siad Barre has already brought  about many far-reaching transformations in the Somali society. Dedicated to political,  cconomic and cultural independence, guided by the principles of scientific socialism and  self-reliance, the Somali people, under the leadership of the Revolutionary Government,  have mobilized all their energies and resources for rapid development in all aspects of  national life, I'he achievements of the Revolutionary Socialist Government of the Somalia  Democratic Republic in the field of education must necessarily be viewed against a capita-  list colonial past which was characterized by an educational system which was quantitatively  meagre in the extreme and qualitatively wholly irrelevant in content and direction to the  needs of an independent socialist nation. These achievements must further be viewed  against the situation which prevailed inthe country before the socialist revolution of October
1969 - a situation which was characterized by corrupt indifferent régimes often under the  direction of neo-colonialist interests.
12.9 It was a particular misfortune of my country before the revolution that, while it is  blessed with a homogeneous population which speaks only one language from one end of the  country to the other, this language had never been given an official script. All attempts by  nationalists to do so during the colonial era were frustrated and blocked by the colonial  rulers while subsequent régimes, corrupt and indifferent as they were, paid no more than  a noisy lip-service to this vital matter. The result has been that a multiplicity of foreign  languages came to be used as media of instruction at different stages of the educational  system. These foreign languages, have, as can be well imagined, played havoc with the  effectiveness of not only the regular system of education but also of literacy programmes.  Furthermore, the absence of the written form of the Somali language, placed serious con-  straints on all attempts to revitalize the national culture after the suppression it had been  subjected to during the colonial era,
12.10 The Revolutionary Government saw from the very beginning the need for the radical  transformation. of the educational system - both quantitatively and qualitatively - in full  realization of the fact that relevant education is a vital and indispensable instrument for  development. Of the many things accomplished since the revolution I should like to men-  tion the following:
1, The masses were fully mobilized and through self-help schemes the school system  was rapidly expanded, As an example: the enrolment in Grade I of primary schools  increased by 100% last year over what it was in the previous year.
2. Curricula have been extensively revised and in many cases new curricula devised  to make the content relevant to the Somali environment, culture and political system.
3. New textbooks, also environmentally, culturally and politically relevent, have been  written by Somali authors, .
4. Broader teacher-training facilities have been provided.
5. A system of national service for secondary school teachers has been instituted  whereby, after a period of national orientation and training in teaching methods,  they go out to the villages to teach in schools and work on literacy campaign pro-  grammes for one year.
6. All regular teachers have been put through orientation seminars and courses where  they were instructed on the socialist philosophy of education to be implemented in  the country.
7. Technical and pre-technical education, ignored by colonial administrations and  neglected by régimes before the revolution, is being given great emphasis as much  for attitudinal training as for the acquisition of skills,
8, Literacy and adult education have been expanded through voluntary campaigns by  civil servants, armed forces personnel, students and the literate public. These  campaigns have been led by the President of the Supreme Revolutionary Conacil  himself.
9. Most important, Mr. President, the Revolutionary Socialist Government of the  Somali Democratic Republic, on 21 October 1972, on the occasion of the third  anniversary of the revolution, announced an official script for the Somali language.  Thus the step that stymied previous régimes has been taken.
12.11 Mr, President, the decision just taken by my Government regarding the official  script for the Somali language is indeed revolutionary and of historic proportions for my  people, It is my Government's policy to introduce the Somali language into schools as a  subject and as a medium of instruction immediately. This action will no doubt make edu-  cation more relevant, more meaningful for Somali children. It will make the learning pro-  cess easier, thereby greatly enhancing the internal efficiency of the system. My Govern-  ment further intends to use the written Somali language as the basis for a massive literacy  campaign aiming at nothing short of wiping out illiteracy in a short time, It will also be  immediately introduced as the language of administration. From the beginning it has been  my Government's intention and policy that there should be no time lag between formal  announcement of the script and its implementation. In order to make this possible, all  necessary basic reading material for regular school programmes and literacy programmes  had already been prepared by a commission of Somali scholars before the announcement  of the decis_on on the script.
12.12 My Government's policy regarding the use of the written Somali language gces much  further than has already been mentioned. The national culture greatly suffered under col-  onialism and was much neglected before the revolution of 21 October. We believe that for  a nation to attain its aspirations it must have a dynamic culture based on the masses of the  people; it must first culturally and mentally liberate itself from the after-effects ofthe col-  onial experience. My Government's policy, in the final analysis, is to use the written  Somali language as an instrument for a cultural transformation touching upon all the masses  and aimed at total cultural liberation and the unleashing of the creative energies of the  masses. [tis in this light that Unesco's endeavours in the development of African languages  are of particular relevance to us and we request that great importance and high priority be  attached to them. Thank you Mr. President.

14.1 Mr. KORTNER (Norway):  Mr. President, fellow delegates, the Norwegian delegation will concentrate its inter-  vention on some reflections on the future development of our Organization. In his Draft  Medium- Term Outline Plan the Director-General has advanced a number of interesting  ideas which have also been commented upon by the Executive Roard.
14.2 The first of these ideas is constituted by the Director-General's proposal for a future  shift of the main emphasis of Unesco's functions towards advisory services to all Member  States in regard to national policies in the field of aducation, science, culture and commu-  nication. This would be an interesting, but also an extremely ambitious undertaking. It  would make it necessary for Unesco's intellectual capacity, effectiveness and speed of re-  action to novel problems to exceed that of the national services in the individual Member  States in these fields, To realize these ambitions might take considerable time.
14.3 But there is one area in which Unesco could and should act very quickly and where  the Organization is so placed that it could render a very substantial service to its Member  States in the development of their national policies. What I have in mind is science policy.  Let me emphasize at once, Mr. President, that I am not using the term in the somewhat  narrow technical sense in which it is being used in the Draft Programme before us. I am  thinking of a science policy where the main aim is to contribute to the identification and  solution of the great problems with which our civilization is faced, with the aid of research  in the natural and social sciences and the humanities. This must be borne in mind in the  development of the individual scientific disciplines.
14,4 This idea has to some extent found expression in the Draft Programme and Medium-  Term Outline Plan prepared by the Director-General. One is nevertheless left with the  impression that the projects which are taken up are, to a very large extent, concentrated
18  on the development of specific scientific activities, and that the priority given to the vari-  ous tasks is somewhat arbitrary. But a science policy in the sense in which I am using  the term must also include an assessment of the relative impact on society of the individ-  ual scientific disciplines.
14.5 All the Member States of Unesco are faced with the problem of developing science as  a tool in social planning. Co-operation across traditional sectors and divisions is essen-  tial to such an undertaking. This applies particularly to.co-operation between the social  and the natural sciences. Such a comprehensive science policy has been in the forefront  in the public debate for some time. If Unesco is to serve as an adviser to its Member  States in the development of their national policies in this field, it must not only echo, but  it must spearhead this trend.
14.6 Individual international programmes planned or launched by Unesco should mature  from such a comprehensive policy and make up parts of a total united plan or relate to such  a plan as experiments, Let me mention as an illustration that the programme on Man and  , the Biosphere and the programme on Man and His Environment would have formed inter-  related parts of one united programme, had such a comprehensive science policy existed  in Unesco, I have noted with interest that a movement towards greater co-ordination. of  these programmes is taking place. -
14,7 It is quite possible that the development of a comprehensive science policy by. Unesco  which reasonably corresponds to the problems and needs of individual Member States, may  require changes to be made in the organizational structure in order to bring all science.dis-  ciplines under one joint leadership. The division between natural sciences on the one hand  and social sciences, the humanities and culture, on the other, is unfortunate from the point  of view of a comprehensive science policy.
14.8 Mr. President, it is my hope that the Director-General will give a considerable part  of his time towards the further examination of this problem. The same applies to the new  Executive Board which we are going to elect tomorrow, Member States, international non-  governmental organizations and individual experts should be called upon to participate in  this process.
14,9 My delegation considers the problem of which I have now spoken as a major problem  of our civilization, It is also convinced that a solution to the problem of a comprehensive  science policy in Unesco and the development of a suitable organizational structure is a  necessary prerequisite for the realization of the Director-General's high aspirations in  this field, My Government has no ready-made solutions to offer. But we are ready and  willing to co-operate in joint deliberations at any time, if our co-operation is sought.
14.10 Mr. President, I shall turn now to another major problem of our time. I am think-  ing of the world informational imbalance. A process has been going on throughout this  century whereby the technological and economic basis of information has been concentrated  in a very limited number of countries. This concentration of the means of information is  on the verge of developing into a factual monopoly enabling these countries to dominate the  world community. The situation poses considerable problems for the cultural development  of the majority of Member States of Unesco which are unable to meet this challenge. Some  of the States may be too small numerically, or they may belong to a language area whichis  small in the global connexion, or they may lack the technological or economic prerequisites  because of their stage of economic development; these countries have perhaps paid greater  attention to their cultural development. We are now at the point when a virtual monopoly of  the means of information may enable a very limited number of countries to impose their  ideas and beliefs oa the public opinion of the world. The problem becomes acute with the  advent of direct satellite broadcasting. But it goes much further. It includes also films,  book production, video records and newspaper reporting, to mention a few other aspects.
14.11 Concurrently with this development, a concentration is also taking place within the
18  media-controiling countries themselves whereby the means of communication are being  planned, directed and used by huge companies or associations of companies to further their  own business interests without any form of democratic control, It might even be feared  that freedom of expression which, at least in the Western World, started out as a private  right, is in the process of being transformed into a public wrong. Freedom of expression  is indispensable to a democratic country and must also be upheld internationally. Butitis  abundantly clear that the possibilities of exercising this freedom must also be made avail-  able. It is indeed a major task to create international relations which will permit a balanced  access to information media among all the countries of the world. At the nationallevel the  problem consists in affording the same balanced access to the various opinion groups.  These problems are basic to our civilization and my delegation is of the opinion that it falls  clearly within tk» competence of Unesco to contribute towards their solution. We would  therefore like to see the Organization undertake a long-term'and substantial effort in th. s  field.
14.12 Mr. President, I also have a few words to say about the gap between industrialized  and developing countries in Unesco's fields of competence, of which the Director-General  spoke at some length during his introduction to the general debate. My delegation agrees  with the Director-General's statement that Unesco has a great rôle to play in assisting the  developing countries in the fields of education, science, culture and communication.  Furthermore, it is only by bridging the gap in these fields that the foundation will have been  laid for the levelling off of the global economic imbalance. It is therefore of the utmost im-  portance that the intellectual capacity, the organizational structure and the scope and con-  tents of the programmes enable Unesco to execute this task satisfactorily also in the future.
14.13 My delegation shares the Director-General's opinion that a reasonable rate of real  growth should continue in the Regular Programme. And, the suggested rate of 7 per cent  for each of the three biennia in the Medium-Term Outline Plan is acceptable to us. Having  said this, I must also add that the basis from which the growth is computed should likewise  be real. Mr. Hela of the Finnish delegation had occasion to speak to this point on behalf of  all the five Nordic countries during the budget debate, and I confine myself here to refer-  ring to his remarks in this respect.
14.14 Mr. President, I have limited my intervention to a few central points. My delega-  tion holds the view that it is essential for Unesco to cope with the problems I have men-  tioned. A successful solution to them is of the utmost importance to our Organization and  to all its individual Member States.

(16.1) Mr. MESHKOV (Byelorussian Soviet Socialist Republic) (Translation from the Russian):  Mr. Chairman, distinguished delegates, we live in times of great political, social and eco-  nomic change and of unprecedented scientific and technical achievements. New possibilities,  and prospects for further blossoming of science and culture, are being opened to mankind. On
641   the threshold of the fiftieth anniversary of the foundstionof the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics,  the workers of the Byelorussian SSR and of the whole Soviet land look with great pride at the  path of struggle and creation which has been trodden and with optimism towards the future.
(16.2) Through their long experience of living, fighting and working together, all the Soviet  nations have proved for themselves that the pooling of their material and spiritual wealth, their  will and energy, their experience and knowledge in this indissoluble brotherhood - the Union of  Soviet Socialist Republics - brings truly rich rewards and creates infinite possibilities for un-  flagging progress in all spheres of the life of this developed, multinational socialist society.  Everything that our nations have achieved displays and expresses the joint strength and the  organic unity of the part and the whole, Having pooled all their resources, and basing their  actions on selfless mutual help and close co-operation, the peoples of the Soviet countries have  in a moment of historical time overcome the economic and cultural backwardness left behind by  the old order and have established from within themselves the single, mighty Soviet economy:  a harmonious organism, international in substance, incorporating within itseif the economies of  all the Union Republics and developing according to a single State plan in the interests of the  whole country and every constituent republic of the USSR.
(16.3) Such is the dialectic of the social and economic development of the multinational Soviet  Socialist State, where the explosive progress of each republic is shaped by the dynamic, planned  development of the whole country. ‘
(16.4) This can be clearly seen from the example of the Byelorussian SSR. Byelorussia in the  past was a wilderness; or, as one writer expressed it, a country forgotten by God and man; yet  within less than one lifetime its appearance has changed out of all recognition and it has become  a republic with a highly-developed industry and an intensive agriculture, a republic of progressive  socialist culture.
(16.5) In the Brockhaus and Efron Encyclopaedia, published in Russia before the great October  Socialist Revolution of 1917, Byelorussia was dismissed in a few lines. The authors were un-  able to write more, so unusually low was its economic and cultural potential. Now we publish  the twelve-volume Byelorussian Soviet Encyclopaedia, which can scarcely hold all the informa-  tion about the revolutionary, inventive and actively-creative life of the Byelorussian SSR.
(16.6) The matching of the interests of the USSR as a whole with the interests of the various  republics, the relations of genuinely fraternal, mutually-advantageous co-operation between the  free and equal peoples of our country, have an exceptionally beneficial effect on the development  of each Soviet republic, including the Byelorussian SSR.
(16.7) The overall volume of industrial production in Byelorussia doubles every six years. The  socialist agriculture of the republic is developing unflaggingly. In the last five years alone, the  gross production of the collective farms in the Byelorussian SSR rose by 45% over that of the  preceding five-year period,
(16.8) Socialism, the life-giving source of friendship between nations, has also given the Bye-  loruseian people great spiritual values. In Byelorussia, where before the October Revolution,
80% of the population was illiterate, where there was not a single institute of higher education  and where three books were published annually for every hundred people, there are now the  Byelorussian Academy of Sciences, 170 research institutes, 26 higher and 129 special secondary  educational institutes; and the changeover to general secondary education is proceeding. Bye-  lorussia today surpasses many developed countries of Western Europe in the number of its uni-  versity students per 10,000 of the population. A total of 2, 600 book titles, in 26 million copies,  were published in 1971 in the republic - almost three books for each inhabitant. There are now  fourteen active professional theatres in the Byelorussian SSR.
(16.9) We belong to a great family, the name of which is the Soviet people. All our achieve-  ments in economics, education, science and culture result from the victory of socialism in the  USSR and from the practical application of Lenin's national policy by the Communist Party, and  are an eloquent testimony to the life-giving and invincible strength of the new kind of State, the  USSR.
(16.10) Our republic continues to be visited by various delegations from many countries. Ve  are always willing to share our experience of socialist transformation with our friends, and  shall be unfailingly prepared to share it with them in the future.
(16.11) Guided by clear and lofty aims in the struggle for a better future for mankind, the Soviet  State spoke out even in the dawn of its existence for the establishment of completely new rela-  tions between countries and peoples. Peace and freedom for nations, friendship, equality of  rights and the brotherhood of all races and nationalities, peaceful co-existence of States with  various social structures, co-operation and mutual assistance - such are the basic principles  which are proclaimed by the land of the Soviets and which the Soviet Union and the nations of all  its fifteen republics, including the Byelorussian Republic, continue to put tirelessly into practice,
(16.12) These noble principles are now being even more widely acknowledged. They are a de-  cisive factor in modern international relations; they are the foundation of the joint policy of  socialist concord and have become the banner of all progressive forces,
(16.13) The present session of the General Conference of Inesco is taking place in an intes;na-  tional situation in which new trends in relations between States, fulfilling the aspirations of peo-  ples for a guaranteed and lasting peace on earth, are continuing to develop and grow stronger.  Encouraging changes are taking place on the international scene and leading to a lessening of  tension and to a more healthy political climate.
(16. 14) We must note with satisfaction the very wide international acknowledgement of the in-  disputable fact that a great contribution is being made to this cause by the Soviet Union and other  socialist countries and by all progressive forces in the world. .
(16. 15) At the same time we cannot forget that the United States of America is now waging an  aggressive imperialist war against the Viet-Namese people, that Israel continues its provcations  against the Arab States, and that racist and colonial régimes have not yet vanished from the face  of the earth.
(16.16) The Byelorussian people, like the other peoples of our country which make up the USSR  and unite their efforts in creative labour for the construction of a new society, have a profound  and organically inherent interest in peace and progress, just as predatory wars and the oppression  of other peoples are organically foreign to them. For this reason we stand firmly on the side of  the just struggle of the Viet-Namese people and the other peoples of Indo-China, and of the peo-  ples of the Middle Eastern countries, against imperialist aggression. The invaders must leave  Viet-Nam and the Arab territories and cease killing people and destroying valuable cultural and  material assets created by the labour of many generations.
(16.17) Unesco, as an authoritative international organization, must not withhold assistance  from the solution of the very important political problems of our times. The nations expect  Unesco to make a genuine contribution to the strengthening of peace and international security  and to the struggle against colonialism and racism. We regret to say, however, that the def-  inite decisions adopted on these matters by Unesco's highest body, its General Conference, at  previons sessions are not always put into practice with due consistency and purpose.
(16.18) In our attempt to improve Unesco's programme for the forthcoming biennium, the  delegations of the Byelorussian SSR and other States have submitted draft resolutions on very  important matters for examination by the General Conference at its present session. The aim  of these resolutions is that Unesco should fulfil more actively and purposefully the noble tasks  enshrined in its Constitution.
(16.19) I should like, above all to draw attention to the proposal by the USSR and other States  entitled "Implementation of the resolution of the sixteenth session of the General Conference  concerning Unesco's contribution to peace and its tasks with respect to the elimination of col-  onialism'". This draft resolution calls upon Unesco and its Member States to heighten their  efforts to strengthen the peace and security of nations, and in particular to assist in the con-  vening of the Pan-European Conference on Security and Co-operation.
(16.20) As co-sponsors of the draft resolution on the principles governing the use of mass in-  formation media, we feel that one of Unesco's urgent tasks is to prepare a declaration on the  main principles governing the use of mass media to strengthen peace and international under-  standing and to combat war propaganda, racism and apartheid.
(16.21) The Byelorussian people, like all the nations of the Soviet Union which have eradicated  all national oppression, is implacable towards all manifestations of racism, apartheid, colon-  ialism and neo-colonialism. The draft resolution submitted by the USSR, the Byelorussian  SSR and other States to strengthen Unesco's rôle in the fight against racism and apartheid de-
643   clares that racism and apartheid are incompatible with the dignity of the human personality and  constitute a crime flagrantly violating the basic human rights and freedoms. It calls upon Unesco  to strengthen its fight against colonialism, neo-colonialism, racism, apartheid and other forms  of racial and colonial oppression,
(16. 22) I should like to draw attention also to the draft resolution on measures for the defence,  preservation and development of national cultures submitted by the delegations of the USSR, the  Byelorussian SSR and other countries. This document stresses the importance of preserving  and developing national cultures, with their distinctive original features, as the objective foun-  dation for the cultural progress of mankind and the development of international cultural links.  The resolution proposes that 1975 be proclaimed International Cultural Development Year. It  calls on all States Members of Unesco to take an active partinthis enterprise, whichis designed  to increase mankind's spiritual potential and to strengthen friendship and understanding between  all the nations of the world. It accords with the decisions of the intergovernmental Conference  on Cultural Policies in Europe, held in Helsinki in June this year.
(16,23) We also feel that the proposal by the Soviet Union and a group of socialist countries,  including the Byelorussian SSR on improving the programme ''Man and the Biosphere' is ex-  ceptionally important,
(16.24) The Byelorussian SSR welcomes the concentration of Unesco's natural science activity  on large and genuinely international programmes and projects, It gives us pleasure to note that  the International Symposium on the Hydrology of Marsh-ridden Areas took place successfully  in the summer of this year in Minsk, the capital cf Byelorussia, as part of one of these pro-  grammes, the Hydrological Programme,
(16.25) We feel that it would be very important for the improvement of all Unesco's activities  if serious consideration were given to the proposals of the USSR for increasing the effectiveness  of Unesco's programme and practical activity, curtailing the Organization's administrative ex-  penses and the cost of the Secretariat's upkeep, and ensuring equitable geographical distribution  of posts in the Secretariat.
(16.26) We should like to state here very definitely that the.two-year programme (document
17 C/5) is the only legal basis for Unesco's activity. Document C/4 can only be regarded as a  guide and a long-term draft, not binding on Member States either financially or otherwise.
(16,27) The delegation of the Byelorussian SSR stresses the need to bring about a genuine uni-  versality in Unesco. By a decision of the present session of the General Conference, the People's  Republic of Bangladesh has been admitted to the Organization. The question of the admission of  the German Democratic Republic to membership of Unesco should be settled without delay. In-  deed, Unesco itself is the loser from the continuing absence from us of the representatives of  the German Democratic Republic, a State which has many great achievements to its credit in  economics, education, science and culture,
(16.28) The sooner this historical injustice towards the German Democratic Republic and like-  wise towards the Democratic Republic of Viet-Nam and the People's Democratic Republic of  Korea is put right, the greater will be the gain to Unesco and to the cause of all-round interna-  tional co-operation.
(16.29) The delegation of the Byelorussian SSR expresses the hope that the seventeenth session  of Unesco's General Conference will contribute to the strengthening of peace and international  security, and to the further development of international co-operation in education, science and  culture in the interests of mankind,
(16.30) In conclusion, I am bound to say that the delegation of the Byelorussian Soviet Socialist  Republic cannot refrain from expressing its indignation at the malicious and unpardonable speech  made yesterday to this session by the representative of Israel, who permitted himself to shower  from the lofty podium of the General Conference of Unesco all kinds of fabrications, slander and  insinuations on the Soviet Union and its national policy.
(16.31) I wonder what provoked this speech. In any event it was not concern for the life of Jews  in the Soviet Union. His ramblings about human rights and justice are worse than blasphemy,  coming from the representative of a State pursuing aggression, conducting a policy of racial and  national exclusiveness, committing bloody crimes in the captured Arab territories, killing and  maiming peaceful citizens, torturing and humiliating them, and destroying their valuable mate-
18  rial and spiritual possessions created by the work of many generations. We regard the Israeli  delegate's attempt to denigrate the national policy of the land of the Soviets as an unsavoury  attempt by Zionist circles to lead public opinion astray and divert its attention from the aggressive,  chauvinist and racist desires of Israel in the Middle East, as an attempt to use Unesco's General  Conference for Zionist aims, and as a vain attempt to undermine the staunch friendship existing  between the people of the Soviet Union and other countries of the world and to obstruct the grow-  ing trend towards the lessening of international tension.
(16.32) The folly and futility of the slander voiced by the Israeli delegate against the Soviet  Union in connexion with the recently-introduced new arrangements concerning the emigrationof  Soviet citizens to take up permanent residence abroad are quite obvious and immediately evident  to anyone,
(1.33) The Praesidium of the Supreme Soviet of the USSR has passed a resolution that Soviet  citizens wishing to leave the Soviet Union and go abroad shall repay to the State the cost to the  community of their higher education and of the higher standard of training received by skilled  specialists and scientists. This resolution applies to all Soviet citizens irrespective of their  nationality and is an internal affair of our country. That is the first point. The second pointis  that the logic and validity of this decision are completely comprehensible. The Soviet State,  while it offers citizens the opportunity of free education, spends on the development of higher  education and the training of graduate specialists greater sums than any other single State inthe  world. For example, in the academic year 1970-1971 about five million students of all the na-  tionalities of our country studied in institutes of higher education in the USSR. Three-quarters  of the overall number of students received State scholarships, which were significantly increased  from 1 September of the present year. In this academic year the USSR is spending 3. 5 thousand  million roubles on the development of higher and secondary education.
(16.34) It is perfectly understandable that the Soviet Ctate, as it spends such colossal sums on the  training of graduate specialists, has a right to expect that graduates should use the knowledge  they have acquired for the good of their nation. The expenditure on the training of graduate  specialists is a kind of advance made to students, post-graduates and the like. The community  has a right to require that this shall be returned by its citizens in the form of socially-useful  work.
(16.35) It is appropriate to mention that the principle of repayment of the cost of higher educa-  tion has long been applied in many countries of the world. This is understandable. Every coun-  try is concerned that the specizlists it trains shall apply their knowledge in the interests of their  nation. No economically-developed nation can look with equanimity on the loss of its scientific  workers, whose joint creative work is taken into account in the planned development and the  achievement of its economic, scientific and technical programme. Neither the capitalist nor  the developing countries are unconcerned about how and where their graduate specialists are  employed. Many of them have taken effective steps to prevent these from being lost abroad.
(16.36) This trend is clearly underlined by the special resolution of the sixteenth session of  Unesco's General Conference held in 1970. This states quite clearly that the scope and quali-  tative improvement of higher education in various States are incompatible with the outflow of  scientific personnel who have decided to leave their country or not to return to it,
(16.37) The General Conference of Unesco has invited the States Members of this Organization  to take steps to prevent the emigration of specialists.
(16.38) The Soviet Union has done the same by passing an appropriate law. This law applies to  all Soviel citizens irrespective of their nationality: it applies to citizens of Ukrainian, Russian,  Byelorussian, Uzbek or Tadzhik nationality or any other of the many Soviet nationalities, and  also to citizens of Jewish nationality living in the territory of the USSR.
(16.39) The representative of Israel tried here to depict the measures taken in the Soviet Union  as ‘discrimination’ against Soviet citizens of Jewish nationality, including discrimination in  higher education. This is malicious falsehood and slander. Tew people in the world today do  not know that the great October Socialist Revolution once and for all completely did away in our  country with every kind of national inequality. This October victory is enshrined in Article 123  of the Constitution of the USSR.
(16.40) After the October Revolution all the necessary conditions were established for the un-  precedented upsurge of ihe culture of all the nations of Russia, including the Jews, It is suffi-
645   cient to say that at the present time almost a quarter of the Jewish population of the USSR have  received higher and secondary special education,
(16. 41) In the Soviet institutes of higher education there is one Russian student for every 47  members of the Russian population, one Ukrainian for every 65.5 Ukrainians, one Byelorussian  student for every 69 Byelorussians, and 1 Jewish student for every 21 members of the Jewish  population. These figures convincingly refute the Zionist fantasies concerning discrimination  against Jews in the USSR - not to mention that the peoples of the Soviet Union, with their blood  and their lives, saved millions of Jews from physical extermination at the hands of Hitler's  Germany.
(16.42) Whatever the Zionists say, however much malice they spread, and however much they  try to arouse anti-Soviet feeling, they will not succeed in discrediting the national policy of the  land of the Soviets, which was the first in the history of the world to put into practice the prin-  ciples of equality between all nations and peoples.
(16.43) One final point, I point out to the General Conference that the speech made by the re-  presentative of Israel was out of order. We are sorry that this was not pointed out to him.
