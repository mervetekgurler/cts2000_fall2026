SUB-COMMISSION ON NATURAL SCIENCES “  FIRST MEETING  Tuesday, 23 November 1948, at 11 a.m.  Chairman: Dr. H. KR. KRUYT (Netherlands)  Election of the Chairman, Vice-Chairman and  Rapporteur.  In accordance with the suggestions of the  Nominations Committee, the Sub-Commission  elected Dr. H. R. Kruyt (Netherlands) Chairman,  Dr. Carlos Monge (Peru) Vice-Chairman, and  Dr. Joseph Needham (United Kingdom) Rap-  porteur.  Adoption of the Agenda.  At the request of the CHAIRMAN, a new item  was added to the provisional Agenda : grants-  in-aid to the International Council of Scientific  Unions.  With that addition, the provisional Agenda  (document 3C/PRG/5.3) was adopted.  Field Science Co-operation Offices (document
3C/PRG/5.1).  Dr. MANSOUR (Egypt) stressed the importance  of the Field Science Co-operation Offices, and  thought that at least one member of the staff in  each Office should be a scientist, with a knowledge  of the region and of the language generally spoken  there.  Dr. MONGE (Peru) dwelt on the work of the  Field Science Co-operation Office in Latin America  (the international Institute of the Hylean Amazon,  discussion of problems of high altitude biology  and life in desert regions). Since the Montevideo  Conference on the development of science in  Latin America had decided that the first problem  to be taken up should be that of high altitudes,  Dr. Monge offered the services of the Peruvian  laboratories at Lima and at high altitudes (3,000     and 7,000 metres), and invited Unesco to hold a  symposium on the subject in 1949 ‘at Lima.  M. NAJJAR (Lebanon) paid tribute to the work  of the Field Science Co-operation Offices. In the  Middle East a more extensive problem arose, and  M. Najjar suggested that, in that area, scientific  research centres should be set up, similar to that  which had been established in Latin America. In  Lebanon, for instance, there were research workers  who were scattered and lacked sufficient resources.  Mr. NOYES (United States of America) consi-  dered that the problem was much larger than the  Lebanese delegate thought. The problem of the  popularization of science might be divided into  three parts : (1) the education of the masses, and  therefore the arousing of their interest in science;
(2) primary education in the schools, providing  experimental and not entirely theoretical notions  of science; (3) finally, universities and research  centres.  The centres suggested by the Lebanese delegate  should extend their activities in scientific co-  operation to include the study, in association with  Unesco’s Department of Education, of methods of  teaching science in all countries. Such co-  ordination was implicit, for, in many countries,  the educational system was not suited to the re-  quirements of the modern world; the standard of  teaching had to be raised, not merely for research,  but also from a practical point of view.  Mr. PARTON (New Zealand) expressed certain  doubts about the effects of increasing the tasks of  the Unesco staff, as set out on page 5 of document
3C/PRG/5.1. The staff, both in the field and at  headquarters, were doing everything they could.  In view of the reduced strength and budget, what  was necessary was concentration, not extension of  the work.  Mr. Parton agreed with the delegate of the

It was proposed, on page 5 of the working paper  to increase efforts to secure exchange facilities.  Mr. Parton underlined the importance of that sug-  gestion.  Sir Harold SPENCER JONES (United Kingdom)  supported the New Zealand point of view. The  task of the staff of the Field Science Co-operation  Offices was to receive requests and facilitate  exchange of information, material and scientists.  But the requests addressed to them had far ex-  ceeded what they could achieve by themselves.  Until those needs could be met, and until such  time as adequate staff and a correspondingly  increased budget for those Offices were provided,  the United Kingdom delegation could not support  the extension of activities proposed on page 5.  Dr. ARGUELLES (Philippines) subscribed to  the Secretariat's general programme. The scien-  tific work would, to a large extent, depend on what  countries could do themselves. The National  Commissions should be Unesco’s instrument, de-  signed to stimulate governments. Some coun-  tries, however, were more advanced than others,  which had suffered from the war or other cir-  cumstances: the latter ought to be able to count  on stimulation and help from Unesco.  Unesco’s assistance to the Far-Eastern and  Middle-Eastern countries should bear not only on  pure, but also on applied, science. Unesco should  be the inspirer, the scientific and technological  clearing house.  Dr. WALKOM (Australia) did not favour an  extension of functions, for work in excess of that  normally done by a regional clearing house would  require considerable financing, which could not  be justified in existing circumstances.  M. AUGER (Head of the Department of Natural  Sciences) thanked the members of the Sub-Com-  mission for their comments.  With reference to the Egyptian delegates sug-  gestion, he pointed out how difficult it was to  send to the different areas people who could  really talk to scientists in their own language in  the countries concerned. In accordance with doc-  ument NS/28, of June 1947, the Secretariat sent  to Field Science Co-operation Offices scientists  who were not nationals of the countries concerned;  that had the advantage of promoting international  co-operation. It would be difficult to find scien-
328  tists with the required character and qualifi-  cations combined with a knowledge of the local  language. Efforts to that end had in fact been  made : two scientists, who both knew Spanish,  had been sent to Latin America, and efforts to  solve the same problem had been made in China,  India and the Near East.  With regard to the symposium on scientific re-  search at high altitudes proposed by the Scientific  Conference at Montevideo, to which Dr. Monge  had referred, the Secretariat was very grateful for  the Peruvian offer of co-operation. It was de-  sirable that delegates from the Joint Commission  of the International Council of Scientific Unions  should also attend such a Conference on high  altitude research; the Interlaken Conference had  decided to set up the Joint Commission, composed  of members ot the Unions of Astronomical  Sciences, Physical Sciences and Biological  Sciences.  The Lebanese delegate had spoken of research  institutes. Was he thinking of institutes similar  to that of the Hylean Amazon? That Institute,  however, was designed for certain definite pur-  poses, and was moreover independent. Similar  arrangements might, however, be made in other  areas.  The teaching of science had been the subject of  very valuable remarks from the delegates of the  United States of America, New Zealand, the United  Kingdom, the Philippines and Australia. The  (question was to what degree the work of the  Science Co-operation Offic es could be extended  without reducing their effectiveness. Their staff  and budget were small and did not even yet cor-  respond to what was envisaged in document  NS/28 of June 1947; they were only a first step.  The Secretariat would, however, bear in mind the  remarks made by the various delegations with  regard to their apprehensions on the subject of an  extension of the duties of the Offices.  With regard to the position in the teaching of  science, M. Auger stated that that aspect of  education had not been dealt with by Unesco  itself. The Mexico City Conference had decided  that the question of the teaching of science  throughout the world should be referred to the  International Bureau of Education at Geneva for  enquiry and report; the question had apparently  not yet been dealt with, which might postpone  the study of it by Unesco to a fairly distant date.  The proposal that the Field Science Co-operation  Offices should work in association with the  Department of Education raised difficulties, be-  cause it involved an extension of activities not  covered by the budget; the delegate of the United  States of America had there raised a question  which might perhaps be specially discussed, apart               from that of the Field Science  Offices. Mr. NOYES (United States of America) em-  phasized the difficulty of increasing the work of  the Field Science Co-operation Offices and the  impossibility of doing so forthwith, but said that  he would like his suggestion and its budgetary  implications to be discussed. Dr. NEEDHAM (United Kingdom), supported  by M. FLORKIN (Belgium), expressed the  contrary view.  The resolution proposed by the delegate of the  United Kingdom was adopted.  Protection of nature and conservation of  resources (document 8C/PRG/5.2).  Mr. NOYES (United States of America) proposed  that there should be added to resolution 6.92 (doc-  ument 3C/5, page 87) the words :  … the Conference to include the consideration of the  functions of Unesco and the International Union for  the Protection of Nature in a world-wide programme  for the conservation of food resources.  The problem of the protection of natural  resources should embrace that of the safeguarding  of food resources. It was also necessary to define  the part to be played by Unesco and the help it  could give in that connexion to the other Spe-  cialized Agencies of the United Nations. The  working paper contained, in any case, a resolution  to that effect.  The amendment was adopted without discus-  sion.  Sir Harold SPENCER JONES (United Kingdom)  emphasized that the two Conferences contemplated  in resolution 6.92 should not necessarily be con-  vened at the same place and time. If the Technical  Conference were to be held at Lake Success, some  States might be unable to take part in it, owing  to their lack of dollars. Sir Harold suggested  that the meeting should be held in Europe.  Mr. NOYES (United States of America) did not  agree, for, in the light of the amendment which  had just been adopted, it would be impossible for  the two Conferences to complete their task if  they worked separately. The CHAIRMAN submitted a resolution covering  all the above decisions :  The Sub-Commission on Natural Sciences, having  regard to the special aims of the two Conferences  (UNSCCUR and Unesco/IUPN) as set forth in doc-  ument 3C/PRG/5.2, and to the arrangements which  have been made for the necessary co-ordination, ap-  proves programme resolutions 6.91, 6.92 (as amended  by document 3C/5, Corrigendum), and 6.93, and the  budget and work plan related thereto (appropriation  line III, 6. The resolution was unanimously adopted.  Mr. NOYES (United States of America) pro-  posed the. following resolution :  The General Conference,  Noting that the Economic and Social Council  attaches great importance to the conservation of  natural resources and has called a special international  conference to consider this problem,  Agreeing with the Economic and Social Council as  to the importance of this problem,  Recognizing that the problems of soil conservation,  food production and distribution and the better  utilization of the available food supply to raise  nutritional standards, prevent the attainment of  Unesco’s purposes in many areas of the world,  Recognizing that a number of Specialized Agencies  have a direct interest in this world-wide problem,  Instructs the Director-General to communicate to  the Secretary-General of the United Nations these  views of the General Conference and requests him to  place on the agenda of the Eighth Session of the  Economic and Social Council the problem of soil  conservation as it affects food production, nutrition,  and Unesco’s aim of achieving the peace and well-  being of mankind.  The General Conference further urges the Economic  and Social Council to initiate immediately joint plan-  ning by the Specialized Agencies, in order that each  agency may assume specific responsibilities for a  world-wide attack on this problem.  The Director-General is further instructed to offer  the services and facilities of Unesco in the effort  indicated in the fields of education, mass communi-  cation, and the basic sciences.  As the Chairman had not been informed of  that resolution, Dr. NEEDHAM (United Kingdom)  (Rapporteur) raised a point of order; he thought  the resolution should be submitted direct to the  General Conference rather than to the Programme  and Budget Commission.  Mr. NOYES (United States of America) agreed  with that suggestion.  The resolution, seconded by Dr. ARGUELLES  (Philippines), was unanimously adopted, for  transmission to the General Conference by the  Programme and Budget Commission.  The meeting rose at 1.10 p.m.
