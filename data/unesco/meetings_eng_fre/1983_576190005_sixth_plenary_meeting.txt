Our Organization has spared no efforts in promoting acti-  vities for the universalization and democratization of education. But although  this vast expension in education bears witness to the high priority that Member  States have given to education, it hasnot been without its attendant problems,  such as educational wastage and insufficient relevance to the world of work and  national development goals. Educational wastage can be ill-afforded especially  by developing countries because it not only deprives other areas of development  of funds but also because it leads to failure in the production of the expected  educated manpower.
22.4 Whilst the expansion of primary education is essential to stem the ever-  increasing tide of illiteracy, we should also be concerned with the emerging  phenomenon of the 'educated illiterates' in our backyard. By educated illiterates,  I am referring to those children who have been through some form of formal school-  ing but have failed to acquire the basic skills of reading, writing and counting.  Their ranks are growing as a result of certain inadequacies in our rapidly ex-  panding educational system. This matter has been of concern to us in Malayasia  and we have undertaken a thorough study of it. On the basis of our findings, a  new primary school curriculum was introduced which lays more emphasis on the acqui-  sition of the basic skills of reading, writing and arithmetic. Preliminary surveys  have shown very encouraging results. Schoolchildren are better motivated to study,  have developed a greater sense of confidence and demonstrate greater eagerness to  learn. In short, the new curriculum seems to have inculcated positive learning  habits.
22.5 Higher education is one field that is undergoing rapid development in many  developing nations. Demands for such education have often proved to be beyond the  capacity of these States to meet adequately. They have invariably to depend on  facilities elsewhere, especially in develo2ed States. The one interesting trend  that has been developing in recent years has been the co-operation and linkages  between institutions of higher learning in the developed and developing countries,  in which there has been a sharing of facilities and expertise as well as costs in  training manpower to meet the needs of the developing nations. Under this system  students may now prepare themselves in their respective fields of study in their  home countries and, on completion of their courses! would be conferred with their  proper qualifications by the institutions in the developed countries. Malaysia  has beer, closely associated with several leading institutions of higher learning  in developed countries under this system. Such an arrangement has also helped  to nurture cosmopolitan ideals, goodwill and international co-operation and  understanding.
22.6 Mr President, while on the subject of international co-operation in the  field of education, I am happy to observe that Unesco has been collaborating with  many regional organizations that are engaged in activities and programmes in simi-  lar areas. The South-East Asian Ministers of Education Organization (SEAMEO) in  my region is one such institution with strong links with Unesco. This link has  proved not only to be beneficial to both agencies but has helped to avoid unneces-  sary, expensive and wasteful duplication of efforts and more importantly, has  proved to be of formidable importance for development. While Unesco has the in-  ternationally accumulated experience and expertise on the one hand, SEAMEO pos-  sesses a vital and indeed intimate knowledge of the needs of its regions whose  various researchers have been found extremely useful to Unesco and other organiza-  tions as well as to other developing countries elsewhere. Such co-operation has  also helped to further the applicability of endogenous efforts to the development  process. Considering the tangible benefits we derive from such co-operative links,  I would urge Unesco to forge greater co-operation and to give more generous assis-  tance to SEAMEO and other regional organizations for the benefit of mankind.
22.1 Mr President, science and technology have an important role to play in  national development, especially in helping developing countries attain their  socio-economic goals. While it is acknowledged that there is a need to narrow  the gap between the rich and the poor nations, the developing nations are to a
481    great extent impeded by the ever-widening gap in their science and technology  capabilities vis-à-vis the developed countries. In an effort to reduce this im-  balance it is imperative that Unesco should place greater emphasis on measures  that will contribute towards the strengthening of science and technology capabili-  ties in developing nations on a much wider scale and intensity than has ever been  attempted before. The Vienna Programme of Action, as you may recall, reiterated  the need for greater international co-operation between countries in the £ield of  science and technology.
22.8 Unesco's programme on science and technology and their application to  development is especially significant and meaningful to developing countries which  are presently undergoing industrialization. Malaysia reaffirms the need to support  national efforts to improve and strengthen infrastructures and research and train-  ing programmes in the natural sciences, the social and human sciences, technology  and the engineering sciences.
22.9 Malaysia fully supports the programme on information systems and the pro-  posed improvement of access to information using modern technologies, standardiza-  tion and interconnection of information systems. Unesco should give emphasis  to the importance of the development of national systems and regional and inter-  regional networks leading to the establishment of a global information network.
22.10 Major Programme X, 'The human environment and terrestrial and marine re-  sources', is one particular area of activity I would like to dwell upon now. The  oceans have become the focus of much interest in recent years as they have been  perceived to be a new area for the exploitation of natural resources.
22.11 The recent intensification of the exploitation of mineral resources in  the continental shelves has caused grave concern as to the possible long-term  effects on the existing equilibrium of the ecosystem. Any disequilibrium,  needless to say, would have adverse consequences on the world's economy, espe-  cially that of the Third World. Marine resources have been a major source of  protein for the peoples of the Third World, and any disruption of the ecosystem,  not to mention the pollution of the oceans, would pose a threat to this tradi-  tional source of protein supply. It is therefore imperative that the existing  ecosystem of the oceans be preserved.
22.12 In this regard, Programmes X.4 and X.5 concerning the study of the oceans  and its resources, the management of coastal and island regions with a view to  achieving a better understanding of the oceans and the development of better  management techniques in the exploitation and utilization of the marine environ-  ment, are welcome.
22.13 Mr President, our Organization has played a significant role in the field  of culture in the restoration and preservation of monuments and historical sites  all over the world. Sukhothai, Borobudur and Moenjodaro are some of the restora-  tion works that immediately come to mind, and these serve as reminders of the  rich cultural heritage of mankind. These monuments help to reconstruct the rich  cultural heritage of these countries. Such reconstruction, however, would only  be complete with the return of cultural documents, artifacts and historical papers  currently in the possession of other countries, especially countries that were  former colonial masters. In this regard, difficulties are being faced by the de-  veloping countries because of the extreme reluctance and sometimes intransigent  attitude of those concerned to return this essential cultural property.
22.14 Many developing nations are beginning to re-establish a national cultural  identity of their own after independence, as colonialism has brought to these  countries alien cultures that saw the disruption of the development of their  indigenous cultures. In the reconstruction of the cultural heritage of these  countries, Member States should adopt a more sympathetic andpositive attitudewith  regard to the return of cultural property.
22.15 Mr President, we in Malaysia are proud to be among the few independent  developing nations which-have successfully developed our own national language.  The Malay language, which we have adopted as the national language, has been  the lingua-franca of the peoples of South-East Asia, spoken by some 150 million  people. It has seen tremendous developments in the last two decades, culminating  in its utilization as the language of instruction right up to the tertiary level.  Recognition should therefore be given to the importance of the Malay language as  a means of communication in South-East Asia, if not as one of the important ian-  guages of the world. Unesco should play a more active role to help further develop  this language, especially in the effort to enhance the standard of its literature,  in the production of books and in the translation of works of the world's famous  writers.
22.16 Mr President, I would like now to turn to the subject of the freedom of  communication. Certain circles have argued forcibly for absolute freedom in com-  munication without concern for the realities of particular societies. Such people  view this freedom without subscribing to the fact that absolute freedom does not  exist even in countries considered as the most liberal. Communication is now  generally accepted as an important tool for national development as it helps to  mould, among others, social values, ways of life and cultural and political as-  pirations. The utilization of such an important tool cannot be allowed without  proper direction in line with national policies and a sense of responsibility  to serve national aspirations. It is thus imperative that the communication policy  of each nation should be modeled on the country's own background, experience and  desired values.
22.17 Another area of concern in the field of communication is the increasing  imbalance in the flow of information between the developed and the developing  nations. This imbalance is likelytoworsen unless the developingnations strengthen  their political will to accelerate thedevelopment ofthe existingmedia, facilities  and training to operate any new communications and inrormation system and keep  abreast of the latest advances in communication technology. The establishment of  the International Programme for the Development of Communication (IPDC) promises  to be of mxch assistance to Member States in this regard. It is heartening to  note that the IPDC will undertake projects to further the creation and deuelopment  of news agencies in several parts of the world. It will also help to identify the  needs and priorities of communication development planning in developing countries.  Considering the crucial importance of communication to developing nations, I would  say that there is a need to urge both Unesco and IPDC to redouble their efforts.
22.18 Malaysia fully subscribes to Unesco's dominant role in the struggle to  establish a new international information and communication order. Our commitment  stems from our belief that information and knowledge are power, and that the na-  tions that control the facilities and media technology to communicate information  and knowledge have that power to dominate the 'global village'. At a time when  developing countries try to decolonize, to condition the minds of their peoples  towards self-reliance and to eradicate the vestiges of colonial mentality, such  domination in information and communication would be inimical to this sincere  effort. The decolonization of minds is the prerequisite towards opening the  intellectual doors of perception among the intellectuals of developing countries  to enable them to accept the basic facts of local conditions and work to meet  national needs within the limits of their resources and capabilities. The present  imbalances in information and communication could result in cultural imperialism  and it will inhibit the talent, creativity and intellectuality of the peoples of  the developing countries. Such domination means that political freedom which was  won more than a generation ago is far from a reality. Secondly, we are strongly  committed to our belief that communication and informatyon are an integral part  of the overall development process of the developing countries. Communication and  information are the basic elements in the efforts to eradicate poverty, to combat  illiteracy, to control population growth, and to meet the basic needs of the poor  and cultural development. Finally, communication and information are primary means  of sustaining the national independence and national sovereignty of any nation. No  nation, and especially no developing country, should be deprived of the indispensable  means to comunicate.
22.19 Malaysia's commitment does not confine itself to mere rhetoric but ex-  tends to active participation. On the regional level, Malaysia has helped to  establish the Asia-Pacific Institute for Broadcasting Development, provide it with  its headquarters and, as the host country, contributed substantially to its opera-  tional costs. Malaysia is also an active member of the Organization of Asian News  Agencies as well as Asia-Vision Programme, a project under the Asia-Pacific Broad-  casting Union.
22.20 Mr President, at this juncture I would like to raise a matter that should  be of great concern to all of us - drug addiction and the illicit trafficking of  drugs. As we all know, drug addiction not only affects the individual and his  family but also could bec0r.e a serious threat to a country's security. We in  Malaysia have declared an all-out war on drug addiction and consider it the  'nation's number one enemy'. If this scourge of mankind is not properly contained,  it may undermine Unesco's efforts to make this world a better place for mankind.  All our programmes and activities will come to nought due to the deleterious  effects of drug addiction on our youth today. It is thus imperative that Unesco  address itself more emphatically to this global problem, and Unesco is certainly,  to my mind, appropriately placed to help combat this menace through its activities  in education, the social sciences, culture and communication. The recent Unesco  travelling seminar for educators and specialists of the mass media on drugs was of  great benefit to countries in South East Asia.
22.21 Mr President, the decentralization policy has been in operation for some  years now. The posts of Regional Co-ordinators at the Assistant Director-General  grade were established in 1981 to further assist in the co-ordination and harmoni-  zation of Unesco's programmes and activities in the region. We appreciate Unesco's  wise move in this direction. However, in my view, the potentialities of these  posts have yet to be fully realized. Perhaps one of the reasons for this is that  thedecentralizationpolicy has not yet, seen. a significant transfer of authority  from headquarters to the Regional Offices. In my view, the Regional Co-ordinators,  as senior staff officials, should be given authority commensurate with their res-  ponsibilities, thereby ensuring more effective implementation of the decentraliza-  tion policy.
22.22 Mr President, distinguished colleagues, ladies and gentlemen, it is indeed  most gratifying to know that despite the differences and difficulties prevailing  in our world today, we are able to meet together in a spirit of goodwill, co-  operation and harmony to share our concerns, our worries and our aspirations. And  this spirit of togetherness I believe, Mr President, will spur all of us to greater  efforts to strive for a better world for all mankind. Thank you, Mr President.
