1 mm    supports very willingly the proposal presented by the United States delegation. We believe, indeed, that it is essential to establish effective collaboration between all the universities of the world in order to realize Unesco's aims. But we would like to put forward again another project in the same connexion which we submitted at the last Conference. We would like this collaboration between universities to take the following form : that in certain univer- sities, perhaps three or four or five throughout the world, there should be an Institute of International Studies attached to the university. The French delegation would like to give its support to the United States proposal and also to the idea that these meetings of university authorities, rectors and deans, should study not the problem of an International University (for I believe this might entail very serious difficulties) but rather the problem of establishing international sections in the main universities throughout the world. The universities could be chosen after consultation between Unesco and the committees which are pro- vided for in the United States motion. These uni- versities would then have a section where professors coming from abroad could teach for three months, for six months or for several years, depending on the agreements made under Unesco’s auspices between the universities and the professors. This would have the great advantage of securing collaboration between professors of different countries, and also of encouraging students from all over the world to go to these universities where all the resources of the world would be at their disposal and where, furthermore, they would find teaching not given from the national point of view of the country in which the university was situated. I do not think the problem can be solved imme- diately. I should like its study to be one of the purposes assigned to the proposed meetings of university authorities. The very fact of its study would, it seems to me, have important consequences from the point of view of international university relations. We should no longer be thinking in terms of unifying diplomas and programmes but of bringing together a team of scholars and educators from all parts of the world who would teach in nation- al universities. That is one of the aims which might be assigned to the conferences called for by the United States proposal. The fundamental problem of Unesco is the pro- blem of the training of the new generation. I ask myself whether if, in the interval between the first and second world wars, Germany had prepared the coming generation for peace, the results might not have been quite different. But Germany pre- pared for war and we all know the tragic results. Thatis whylbelieve that the problem should be tackl- ed in relation to the training of the coming genera- tion rather than as an attack on this or that aspect— the problem of education of backward childrenor, as was very aptly said some time ago, of the post-war child. For us the most urgent task is to attend to the education of the new generation, to attend to the problem of youth, for in what other way can Unesco carry out the tasks which it has set itself in accor- dance with its Constitution? My delegation, there- fore, presents to the Programme Commission a plan which we think feasible and necessary. We must look to the education of youth,as is called for by the first Article of the Constitution, in which there is a sort of agreement ofall countries to orga- nize education with a view to peace and security. This article must be made explicit and put into practice in such a way that this education can be effectively organized, for we must not forget that it is the coming generations that will make war or main- tain peace. We have given ample thought to the educational problem. We have taken full account of adults, but we know too well that education given to adults is a limited education. Its limits are already fixed. On the other hand, if we give proper attention to the training of youth, I believe firmly that Unesco will have grappled vigorously with this problem of education for peace. In reply to a question by the delegate of the United Kingdom in the Working Party, I must make it clear that, when I speak of the education of youth, I do not refer exclusively to school education but to education in all its aspects, education outside schools and beyond schools; but all the same I am of the opinion that the school can be a very effective instrument for the attainment of these goals.
