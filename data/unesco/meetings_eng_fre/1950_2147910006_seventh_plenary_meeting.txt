XIII. Discussion of the Director-General’s  Report (continuation).  [1] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] 1 declare the meeting open. 1 call upon  the delegate of Mexico.  [2] Mr. Antonio CASTRO LEAL (Mexico) —  [Translation from the Spanish] Mr. President,  Mr. Director-General, fellow-delegates, this is the  second time that I have had the honour to com-  ment on the Director-General’s Report at a session  of the General Conference of Unesco. Eight  months have passed since the Conference in Paris;  eight months of intensive work by the Director-  General, the Executive Board and the whole staff  of the Secretariat. During that time I have close-  ly followed the work of the Organization and  its relations with the various Member States,  and have had opportunities of appreciating the  efforts made to increase Unesco’s activities and  direct them into the most useful channels. It  was necessary to establish, in the vast field of  science, education and culture, a system of prior-  ities, of grades of urgency and importance, and,  within that system, to direct the Organization's  efforts to the strengthening of weak points and  the solution of the most pressing problems.  During the time that has passed since the Paris  Conference, Unesco has never ceased to exercise  and extend its influence, to work to the best  advantage and to direct its action into the most  useful channels. Unesco has emerged from these  eight months stronger and with a better reali-  zation of the detailed implications of its aims. It  has also established closer and more active work-  ing relations with the Member States. Unesco's  ideals, proposals, projects and schemes have,  indeed, to be carried out at the national level,  through the activities of Member States and, In  particular, through the governmental or private
1 0  organizations concerned with education, science  and culture with which Unesco co-operates.  Unesco’s role is sometimes confined to the en-  couragement, assistance and guidance of those  organizations. Once a plan of work has been  thought out and launched by Unesco, it is for the  Member States to ensure its continuation, for the  benefit not only of themselves but also of the  common ideal of a peace based on education,  science and culture.  [3] There has been an increase in the number  of National Commissions, those essential links with  Member States, and new and improved methods  of contact have been brought into use. The  world is made up of many differing countries;  in its relations with those countries, in seeking  to implant and develop in them the spirit of  mutual understanding, Unesco must take account  of varying national and regional characteristics.  In the past eight months Unesco has set up work-  ing and liaison centres away fom the Organ-  ization’s headquarters—such as the Regional  Office at Havana—as well as special departments  within the Secretariat responsible for ensuring that  measures for co-operation are better adapted to  the interests and characteristics of each country.  [4] An equally important development has taken  place in another direction. Unesco has reviewed  its relations with private organizations concerned  with education, science and culture. The Ex-  ecutive Board has worked hard and long, assessing  the results of co-operation at this level. Through  the application of stricter criteria, it has been pos-  sible to draw up a list of organizations likely to  collaborate more enthusiastically and effectively  in tasks that are in harmony with the ideals of  Unesco.  [5] The foregoing activities, of which I have  been an interested spectator, are all described In  the Director-General’s Report. The work done by  the Director-General, the Executive Board and the         Secretariat has been intense and of a very high  order. In addition to the concentration of  Unesco’s work and the pruning of its programme,  the devising of a more effective system of relations  with Member States, and the review of working  relations with private organizations, they have had  to do the preparatory work for this Conference,  draw up a draft budget for 1951 and also the gen-  eral programme of the Organization. This work,  as I have just said, has been admirably done, and  Member States should be satisfied with it; it has  been done as well, as efficiently and as energetical-  ly as possible.  [6] But, in his report, the Director-General puts  the question whether we have achieved, or are  achieving, all that was expected of Unesco. He  is concerned—and very rightly so—at the tardiness  with which Member States are co-operating and  undertaking the activities incumbent upon them.  Once the staff of Unesco has examined a problem  and found a solution for it, once a committee of  experts convened by Unesco has confirmed or  improved on that solution, and it has been  approved by the General Conference, it is for the  Member States to give practical effect to the scheme  they have approved. Unesco can then do no  more than stimulate the activities of the States  themselves. In his report, the Director-General  emphasizes this point; and it is obvious that,  once the States have decided on their line of  action, they must follow it consistently. Changes  have been introduced in the actual wording of the  resolutions, in order to bring home to gov-  ernments the fact that the execution of the various  resolutions is within their sole competence. More-  over, the advisability has been considered of  investing some of the resolutions with the form  of a treaty or international convention, to ensure  that they shall be carried out at the national level  with the force of such an instrument behind  them. This seems to be a very suitable idea, of  which I think a good deal more may be heard.  It would be an excellent procedure to group  resolutions of a like nature into a single instru-  ment and so deal, in a series of basic conventions,  with points on which agreement had been  reached. In that way principles of capital 1m-  portance would be brought out and given the  character of categorical international obligations.  It would be for Unesco to decide which principles  might suitably be incorporated in an international  convention of this kind, and how far this pro-  cedure could effectively be applied to the various  items in its programme,  [7] Between 1945, when Unesco was founded,  and 1950, the year of our present Conference,  many grave and significant events have occurred.  Unesco was born in the atmosphere of optimism     which followed a long and costly war; all the  peoples wished to live in concord; all desired that  education, science and culture should lead them  towards peace, towards a peace which they fondly  hoped would be permanent. Five years later,  however, the peace that first seemed to be so  assured was found to be precarious; the rivalries  between the nations, so far from disappearing,  seem to be becoming more acute. The longed-  for peace seems to be in peril; there is a feeling  that it may disappear at any moment. The  Director-General is entirely right to ask that  Member States should face this situation and con-  sider the problems it raises for Unesco’s existence  and programme. At the time when Unesco was  born, there seemed no reason why peace should  ever again be threatened; there seemed to be  ample time at the world’s disposal, and no cause  for thinking that governments would not be able  to carry out their plans in their own time. But  now that peace has turned out, so it seems, to be  one of those summer skies that are so often the  prelude to a storm, how should Unesco set its  course? No one will deny that the Director-  General has every reason for his anxiety; no one  will claim that he has not been right to call our  attention to the situation that confronts us, and  to ask us to consider whether the programme  evolved by Unesco at a time when peace seemed  secure is still valid now that peace appears, after  all, to be ill-secured. Unesco was established so  that it might combat war with the weapons of  science, education and culture; now that peace  has, as it were, been crushed into a small space,  are those weapons still of service today? Is the  initial programme still to be followed out? It 1s  for the nations present at this Conference to con-  firm the programme or to alter it, to maintain or  to modify the methods by which it is 1m-  plemented.  [8] Science, education and culture are one of  the ways in which the mind has moulded the  world. Once nature casts itself in the forms that  the mind has created—whatever be the scientific  constancy, the material analysis, the thought pro-  cesses or the art forms involved—man gains in  quality and in spiritual stature. Science, educa-  tion and culture can, however, only operate In  accordance with their own nature, helping to  create in the mind of man a less superficial, more  peaceful, richer and more harmonious world. It  is this world that each of us must set against  disorder, anarchy and war. Unless this ideal  world of harmony can be established in men’s  minds, nothing can save us. But, like the pro-  cess of life itself and the stars in their courses,  it has its own rhythm, its own essential period  of incubation and development. Given the pre-
1  i  —   =         sent world of unbalance and menace, Unesco must  continue to fight with its own arms—science,  education and culture, and everything that forms,  in man’s mind, that impenetrable shield through  which no falsehood or violence can pass. While  Unesco may be unable to change the rate at which  each of these weapons gains its ascendancy in  men’s minds, it can, with the help of Member  States, shorten the time required for preparing  effective action, the carrying out of joint plans,  the initiation of new projects, and the study of  the speediest means of bringing to all parts of the  world the message of revelation that can only  be deciphered at the rate for which the human  mind is equipped. The triumph of Unesco can  only be ensured if that message is properly  understood by all. The message is of value In  itself, and should it not have been completely  deciphered at a moment when Attila’s hordes  descend upon the world, others will certainly  retrieve it from the dust when peace has returned  and draw fresh inspiration from it.  [9] I do not think that the Director-General, who  is a humanist and a man of letters, views the  matter in any other light, though he has un-  doubtedly done right to ask us to examine our  consciences. This is an occasion when we ought  all of us to promise solemnly to do all that lies  within us to hasten the implementation of every  recommendation, project, resolution and plan that  Unesco has formulated to establish in the world  a peace rooted firmly in education, science and  culture. I would close my remarks by expressing  the Mexican delegation’s satisfaction at the develop-  ment and progress of Unesco, as revealed by the  Director-General’s excellent report.  [10] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] 1 call upon the delegate of Brazil.  [11] Mr. Clemente MARIANI BITTENCOURT  (Brazil) — [Translation from the French] 1 am  happy, Mr. President, to pay my respects to you  on behalf of the delegation of Brazil, of its Gov-  ernment, of which I am a member, and of its  people, whom I have the honour to represent in  our National Assembly. By way of illustrating  the bonds uniting our two countries, it is enough  to refer to modern Italy’s contribution, from an  ethnic standpoint, to the development of our  voung nation. The Latin civilization of which  we are both proud is not the least of the ties be-  tween us. Our continent as a whole has wel-  comed people from every part of this older con-  tinent who have come in search of new ways of  existence. There is no part of it that does not  bear the mark of Latin culture. All the peoples  in these far-off lands have sought their common
122  ideals in the shade of Rome, cast upon them  across the centuries and across the oceans. And  it is, perhaps, because of this common spiritual  and racial origin that there lie buried here,  beneath these green Tuscan hills, some hundreds  of our countrymen, the first soldiers of Latin  America who came to the sacred soil of Europe  to fight and die for freedom. Having crossed the  rich plains of the Po, seen throughout Emilia and  Tuscany the grim marks of the ravages of war  and of the sufferings our soldiers had to endure,  and having renewed acquaintance with these now  war-scarred streets, bridges, houses and time-  honoured monuments, with the pleasant hills sur-  rounding them, I rejoice, Mr. President, that  Unesco’s aims—the elevation of man through  culture, and the disarmament of the mind—are,  and will remain, those of my own country. We  must hope that Unesco, whose democratic ideals  are so well framed, will be able to rescue man  from the mistakes of the past, by enabling him to  create new conditions of life for this world, which  prejudice and ignorance have all but annihilated.  [12] Scientific impartiality and objectivity, which  are at once a method and a moral attitude, impel  us to be sincere, and to have faith in the ultimate  fruits of unity. This is the first lesson to be  drawn from the report of our eminent colleague  Mr. Torres Bodet, the Director-General of Unesco.  He has not been content merely to describe events;  what he has aimed at is a real searching of con-  science, a comparison between the Organization s  responsibilities and its resources. This approach,  which is as inspired as it is well-advised, calls, I  think, for an equal searching of conscience on  the part of all delegations, as to how far each of  our countries has contributed to the success of  our joint enterprise.  [13] There are two general points in his report:  a better definition of the responsibilities of the  Secretariat and of the Member States, and the pre-  paration of a long-term programme accompanied  by a parallel short-term programme. As to the  first point, it would be difficult for everything to  be foreseen, defined and prescribed. Though  there are many valuable lessons to be learnt from  what the International Bureau of Education had  done in the past, Unesco’s task had to be envisaged  from fresh angles—it was, for instance, an im-  perative and urgent duty to restore media of cul-  ture in the devastated areas. It is this that may  have given rise to the erroneous but widespread  idea that it was for Unesco not merely to provide  technical aid but itself to engage in educational  activities in the most varied areas and in every  country. It would be a mistake to judge Unesco's  work in the light of that idea. Unesco will never  have the resources or the staff required for the               execution of such a task. It is not an entity over  and above the Member States; it has simply been  created by them, for well-defined processes of  co-operation. What is required is to organize the  National Commissions, with a view to that pro-  gramme of co-operation being carried out. The  varying political and administrative conditions  obtaining in each country are a bar to any uniform  solution; but experience will show, objectively,  the best course to be followed, according to cir-  cumstances.  [14] As regards the work of the National Com-  missions, however, two questions arise. In the  first place, Unesco must be in a position to help  the leading spirits in each country, so that they  in their turn may be able to act on the masses  of the people and direct their aspirations towards  cultural progress and international understanding.  If, in this field, Unesco is to achieve its objects  with the necessary freedom, the Commissions in  each country must, as the report emphasizes,  organize themselves with a view to facilitating a  rapid exchange of ideas and information, of value  both to each country and from the point of view  of the common cause. Encouragement for the  work of scientific and cultural institutions, and  the co-ordination of local activities with the  activities of international organizations, are tasks  that rightly fall to the National Commissions.  But are these in a position to act likewise with  regard to the normal, regular services concerned  with education and the dissemination of culture?  Here, the influence and the competence of National  Commissions are variable factors. Some coun-  tries, though for educational purposes their organ-  nization may be adequate, have no cultural ser-  vices so organized as to permit of their effective  co-ordination with the work of Unesco. Any ex-  pansion in the number of services would merely  involve pointless expense. Moreover, as our  Director-General reminded us in his fine speech  to the Conference, there are always national  policies in education that have to be taken into  account and that reflect political and other con-  ditions governing the lives of the countries con-  cerned. Culture, in its widest sense, can only exist  on an unrestricted basis. But education, though  it seeks to free the individual, is subject to the  circumstances and conditions attendant upon the  evolution of each social milieu; it operates within  the framework of ‘national contingencies’, such  as the political structure, the form of religion, etc.  —In a word, the permanent and stable com-  munity existence characteristic of each country;  there will always be room for technical improve-  ments. Such improvements cannot however be  based on general, abstract principles; they must  have realistic, though certainly progressive pur-  poses, in view.  [15] Here is one of those delicate problems that  merit the attention of the Secretariat and the tech-  nical commissions of this Conference—namely,  how to stimulate educational programmes in each  country by co-ordinating them, if possible, with  the programme of Unesco. Unesco already has  praiseworthy and fruitful work to its credit in this  field, despite the difficulties with which it has  had to contend. We in my own country's  Ministry of Education, for our part, are consider-  ing the possibility of setting up a suitable body to  co-ordinate the Government's activities with  Inesco’s programme, and so facilitating the task of  our National Commission. The problem, there-  fore, may be stated in definite terms. On the one  hand, educational questions, when raised to a  world level or analysed with a view to a general  solution applicable to every place and time, lose  much of their actual substance, content and reali-  ty. On the other hand, viewed from a practical  standpoint, education aims at the training and  elevation of man, adjusted to the various attendant  circumstances. Moreover, as we have pointed  out, educational services give rise to a form of  culture within the framework of certain social  circumstances which must be accepted as they  are. The equating of practical problems by an  international organization will merely amount to  the stating of very general and hence vague and  ineffective formulae; alternatively, it will aim  at concrete solutions that may be suited to some  countries, peoples or areas, but not to others.  [16] Unesco has, it would seem, recently tried  two ways of avoiding this dilemma. The first 1s  the holding of regional seminars, and the second  is the establishment of regional study and action  centres, of which the initial ones, as the Director-  General's report tells us, are being set up in Latin  America: these are the Regional Centre at Havana,  and the Training and Production Centre and  Office for Fundamental Education in Mexico City  and Washington. l am glad to recall that, ever  since my country joined Unesco, this is the course  that we have always favoured, as the only one  calculated to ensure that all quarters benefit from  the advantages that Unesco is in a position to  offer. We have noted the good results secured  from the regional seminars, like that which took  place in Brazil—the inter-American literacy and  adult education seminar held at Petropolis, near  Rio de Janeiro, in which all but two of the Amer-  ican States took part. Apart from the comparison  of ideas and technical processes for which it  afforded an opportunity, the seminar undoubtedly  helped to bring into relief a new conception of           education, in which our distinguished Director-  General has always pioneered: the education of  the masses. The practical results of the seminar  were at once felt in various countries, several of  which, including Brazil, have already embodied  in their educational policy, and even in their per-  manent legislation, the conclusions that their  experts have carefully worked out under the  auspices of Unesco and of the Organization of  American States.  [17] These facts provide the best possible jus-  tification for the comprehensive and thorough-  going plan presented in the report I have in  mind, in particular, the need for a “long-term”  programme, parallel and co-ordinated with a  “short-term” programme, the latter however being  more flexible and adaptable to any special con-  ditions that may arise. Though this may at first  sight appear strange, the two programmes will, to  a large extent, be based on the work of depart-  ments other than that of Education. 1 may men-  tion, for instance, the forthcoming conference of  experts on education, which is being organized  by the Social Sciences Department of Unesco.  A study of the general circumstances, internal or  external, in which industrialization is accompanied  by social tensions, will provide material for stand-  ing directives for the practical organization of  educational systems in each country. Further-  more, the great health campaigns now being  undertaken in my country against endemic dis-  eases, tuberculosis and infant mortality have al-  ready begun to exert a direct influence on edu-  cational programmes.  [18] In the field of education, Brazil is carefully  following Unesco’s investigations and is making  use of their results, adapting them to her parti-  cular circumstances. Thus, as regards funda-  mental education problems, to solve which my  country has taken action even ahead of the  Organization's appeal, as long ago as 1947 we  began to carry out a programme that was com-  pletely in line with Unesco's directives. 1 was  fortunate enough to be in charge of this work;  thanks to it, we have been able not only to make  fresh progress in education in Brazil, but to give  effect to our Constitution, which lays down that  everyone shall have the right to education.  The work has also produced a profound change in  the public conscience so far as education 1s con-  cerned. What strikes us is not so much that we  have been able, in barely three years, to place  two million pupils on the educational roll, or that  we have been able to provide over one million  adolescents and adults with the elementary tech-  niques of culture. What impresses us more, so  far as the results of this popular education move-  ment are concerned, is the change in the mentality  of the community, which is brought out by actual  facts, such as the immediate increase in the average  number of children undergoing education or the  rise in the rate of entry for primary education in  general, the figure here having increased, for the  whole of Brazil, from three and a half millions in
1945 to 4,700,000 in 1948 and very probably to  over five millions in 1949. These results are  mainly due to the Government’s determination  and to the circumstances attending the restora-  tion of democracy within the country. But we  are happy to recognize, that Unesco, by its encour-  agement and its activities, has facilitated the steps  we have taken to bring about this remarkable  degree of progress in our educational system.  [19] An intensive programme of school-building.  regarded by Mr. King Hall, of Columbia Univer-  sity, as the greatest current educational movement  in the world, has enabled us to build, in four  years, 7,000 rural schools, with houses for teachers  attached, in areas where lack of communications  and a small population render living conditions  very problematical. We have also built 500  schools, of three amalgamated classes, and 50 ru-  ral teachers’ training colleges, not to speak of the  effort that has been made by the various States  of the Federation. But it is not solely in the field  of fundamental, and especially adult, education,  or of school buildings that we have tried to carry  out Unesco’s programme in our country and, in  that connexion, to give an example to the world.  In an educational bill now before Parliament (for  a law prescribing directives and principles for  national education, as enjoined by our Constitu-  tion) many passages reflect Unesco’s creative ideas,  which also inform various constitutional provisions  and administrative and legislative measures, like  the earmarking, for educational purposes, of 10 per  cent of the Federal revenue and 20 per cent of the  local taxes: the exemption from taxation of teach-  ers, educational establishments, and paper used  for books and papers; the extension of the net-  work of secondary education institutes; a grant  for establishments that devote themselves to the  education of adolescents with slender financial  resources: the foundation of universities (which  numbered only four in 1946 but which now num-  ber 11), and the maintenance, by the Federal Gov-  ernment, of some dozens of faculties at the uni-  versity level, involving the possibility of improving  their equipment, reducing education fees or even  providing education free of charge, as well as  social assistance for students with no financial  means. All this shows that the Government of  my country has continued, within the limits of  its powers, to proclaim and put into practice the  ideals and aspirations to which all of us here  present subscribe. I would like to refer here, as

Let us pay a tribute to his high  skill constructive idealism, and faith in the per-  fectibility of man through culture. I should also  not omit to mention the work of Professor Paulo  Carneiro, that man of learning and educationist  who has made Brazilian co-operation such a reali-  ty in the Executive Board; nor should I pass over  the contribution made by other Brazilian experts  who have so often been called upon by Unesco to  take part, on a permanent or temporary basis, in  its technical work. Under the principle of geo-  graphical distribution as regards the recruitment  of the Secretariat, we hope to continue to serve  the ideals of Unesco, so that its collaboration in  the peace mission of the United Nations shall, as  the Executive Board desires, “be carried on  vigorously, now as in the future”.  [20] For this purpose, ladies and gentlemen,  Mr. Torres Bodet has all the dynamism, clear-  headedness and grasp for which we could wish.  [ shall not, I think, be wrong in describing our  Director-General as symbolizing that young Amer-  ican outlook, which is bold and courageous but  at the same time characterized by moderation and  accuracy, both of which are essential if our work  on behalf of world understanding is to be carried  to a successful conclusion. However ‘“dionysiac”  and “cosmic” the New World, as is claimed, may  be, it none the less aims at attaining order, modera-  tion and beauty, of which Florence—municipium  splendidissimum, “bellissima e famosissima fi-  gliola di Roma”—is par excellence the externaliza-  tion. In this magnificent atmosphere, redolent of  the creative genius of the Latins, we become  more than ever aware of our duty not to fall into  the error of those who, in Dante’s words, “si son  disfatti per lor superbia”—fell because of their  pride—but to put forth all our efforts to achieve  what Unesco demands of us—the improvement of  the world through culture, and the disarmament  of the mind. In that way we shall pay tribute  to the ideals of all men of good will, those men  who, on the day our Saviour was born, were pro-  mised ‘““peace on earth’. 125  first time that the voice of Guatemala has been  heard in Unesco. (Guatemala is a small country  in terms of American geography, but it nourishes  a full measure of faith and hope that the world  will, on the basis of freedom and democracy,  build up a true and lasting peace. Through its  Government and its old-established University, and  by reason of its humanist traditions, Guatemala  has brought to international discussions, in all  fullness, clarity and good faith, its message on  behalf of human dignity and a practical, generous  and all-embracing peace—a peace not imposed by  the strong, nor by those who are ever avid of  economic power, nor by technicians with no con-  cern for ethics, nor yet by those proud and pre-  judiced quarters whose idea it is to establish an  ideology with a racial or nationalist label that  sacrifices small and heterogeneous civilizations  and, clothed in a wealth of falsehood, aims at  “planning” all the more important forms of human  activity. Guatemala sincerely supports and great-  ly admires the dynamic and well-directed efforts  of the Director-General, Mr. Torres Bodet, that old  combatant on behalf of the education of the Latin  American peoples, whose clear vision impels him  to say that Unesco can never have a part in     intellectual ““planning’, since the mind is syno-  nymous with freedom.  [23] We must also express our sincere thanks  to the Italian Government for having furnished  so delightful and, indeed, so incomparable a venue  for this Conference. No city can provide such  a background as Florence for free creative activ-  ily, grace, harmony, calm meditation, or true  universality. Speakers from various countries,  especially Italy, Yugoslavia and Canada, and yes-  terday India, Lebanon, Colombia and many others,  have spoken of fine achievements based on respect  for all-embracing truth and on a desire that the  eternal, transcendental values should be revived.  [24] It is the keen desire of the Latin American  countries (and especially my country), expressed  through their universities, that it should be  Unesco’s object, in its forthcoming programme,  to carry out a real crusade for the humanization of  science, the humanization of scientists and tech-  nicians; in that way new prospects will be opened  up for peace. Within the hills surrounding this  Tuscan city—divinely protected, and a fruitful field  for all branches of learning—Sarton has already  evolved and formulated the new ‘humanism of  science”. If we wish to free ourselves from com-  plexes and banish the spectre of war, which, for  the ‘Pharisees’ of peace, already has form and  substance, Unesco must work for the humanization  of the peoples, and for the regeneration of all those  barbarian intellects that have made the power of  technology into one of the most formidable    weapons to be used against culture. Science loses  all educational value, and becomes dangerous, if  it is not tempered with the supreme values of the  mind.  [26] It must be Unesco’s task to inform and  educate the masses from youth upwards, in every  sphere, directly or through their leaders, with a  view to the full development of the human per-  sonality, and to respect for human rights and the  fundamental freedoms, all the people being given  the right to participate freely in the benefits of  culture and the historical aspect of the various  communities being preserved, without prejudice  to the universal aspect. This is the line we have  been assiduously following in the so-called Charter  of Guatemala, subsequent to the Latin American  University Congress.  [26] If Unesco steers clear of all influences,  interference and centralization and makes no dis-  tinction between the various ideologies, it will  be able to do good work through “unity in  diversity”. The world—and this was never so  true as since the last war—teems with practical,  “positive” men who are deaf to the voice of the  spirit and who, closed as they are in a sub-  terranean cell, see the universe only through the  grating in the roof. After nine months he will feel and see some gleam  of hope. After twelve months he knows that, if  he does not die first, he has a chance of doing  something that is worth while. And after eighteen  months, if he is lucky, he comes out and finds  himself the master of new skills and with a new  faith in the Organization and in his colleagues—a  little sadder perhaps, but with a faith no less  strong and much more real than that with which  he went in. Maybe I exaggerate this life history,  maybe it is a personal thing, but of one thing |  am sure, and that is that every member of the  Secretariat at some time in his period of office  feels a longing for the concrete and the real and  the stable job at the national level. It is quite  unreasonable, I know, but he gets a sense of  unreality about his work. He feels that he 1s  lost in a world of words. Now, it 1s perhaps of  no great significance to the Conference, but my  ex-colleagues in the Secretariat may find some  little comfort from the fact that I have now gone
138  back to the firm earth of national administration  and I sometimes now find myself wondering if  the concrete work I am doing is anything like as  real as the work that is being done in Unesco,  if many of the solid and homely tasks within our  national borders can have any meaning at all, if  the things for which Unesco stands should fail.  I find myself asking myself, what can it matter  if the education system of New Zealand is ten per  cent better or ten per cent worse, when countless  millions in our neighbouring lands of Asia have no  access to education of any kind.  [81] But what Professor Piaget said two days ago  —I think his phrase was *‘the moral dissatisfaction  within the Secretariat”—is not a thing to be dis-  missed by polite and easy phrases. There is there  from time to time an unhappiness, a malaise,  almost a miasma, that descends, either indivi-  dually or collectively, upon the Organization. I  have tried a score of times to analyse it and to  find out what is the cause; and the causes are, l  am sure, complex. There are certain unavoidable  factors. There are many people there who are  working in a language that is not their own and  who feel the constant strain of that. There are  others who are in danger of losing their roots in a  national system, those roots which still to us, poor  mortals, seem to be essential for mental health.  But there is more to it than that. Professor Piaget  spoke of the tempo of the work, of the tight cycle  of operations within which the Secretariat works.  And that, I am sure, is one of the dominant fac-  tors. It is not so much the amount of work to  be done; it is the tension that comes from con-  stant fears of crisis following crisis, of deadline  following deadline. Now a deadline can be a  stimulating and an exciting thing if it is used,  and can be used, infrequently. But when deadline  follows deadline in an endless chain it is no more  exciting and no more stimulating than a series of  epileptic fits. Now, it would be easy, in looking  for the cause of this, to blame the Director-General.  It would be easier still perhaps to blame the much  abused administrative system and its Bureau of the  Budget. It would not, however, be fair, if I felt  that the blame, or the main blame of any sort, lay  there, for me to speak of it today. I do speak of it  because I feel that the responsibility for much of  the state of affairs does lie with us here, with this  General Conference. We can do more than anyone  else to reduce the intolerable strain under which  our servants are working. Mention has been made  of the fact that we have had three Conferences in  eighteen months. May I be personal? I entered  this Organization in April 1948, and the first day,  as Dr. Huxley will remember, I was rushed into  a meeting to discuss the Programme for the Beirut  Conference. And from then on a very large pro-           portion of my time, and my staff's time, was taken  up with making programmes, budgets, work plans,  working papers, reports on the work in 1948. And  no sooner had we returned from Beirut than we  were rushed into the business of preparing for  the Paris Conference, which was no more a short  working Conference from our point of view than  this one is. And the moment we had finished with  Paris—and before we had finished with the Paris  Conference, while you were sitting in the Paris  Conference—we were working on the Programme,  if you please, for 1951. With the work for 1949  not half done, with 1950 almost forgotten, we had  jumped a gulf and were working on the Pro-  gramme to be presented here at this Conference.  In between, we worked for Executive Boards, we  made reports on this and that, on the work we  had done, the work we were going to do, the work  we could not do, and when we ran out of all that  we made reports on other people’s reports.  [82] Now this is an impossible state of affairs  for human beings to do creative work. Our only  comfort 1s that an organization that could stand  eighteen months of that can stand almost anything  that it is likely to meet in the future. 1 need  not labour the point. I know my good friend the  Director-General will agree, if not with every  word, at any rate with a large amount of what I  say. The miracle is that in spite of all this, some  results have been achieved. Extremely important  results were achieved, and members of the Secre-  tariat did find time, from the writing of reports  and the preparing of programme documents, to do  valuable work, but at a cost, Professor Piaget said,  which is too great for us to demand in the future.  Now there are practical applications for the New  Zealand delegation and for all delegations, I think,  following from this. We of the New Zealand  delegation will do certain things. We will sup-  port, we will listen with sympathy to, the  scheme of the United States delegation to alter  our Constitution to allow of two-yearly Confer-  ences. We know that this may mean a  change in the functions of the Executive Board.  We know that it may mean the developing  of other methods of keeping closer contact with  national States in the intervening years. We will  support anything that makes for fewer and shorter  meetings of the Executive Board, for any change  in regulations that will enable that Board to free  itself from the mass of detail and to devote its  high abilities to matters of equally high policy.  We will exercise, I trust, a fierce restraint on  ourselves in asking at this Conference for special  reports. It is a chastening thought that every time  you ask for a special report, every time you ask  for the presentation of material in a new way,  you are taking up scores and hundreds of hours  that should be spent on the development of the  programme. We shall support any movement for  fewer programme items. This multiplicity of  items in itself can be extremely exhausting to a  staff. We think that there should be fewer pub-  lications and that the publications that come out  should be of the highest possible standard. Half  a dozen good publications in the year are worth  scores of second-rate ones.  [83] We would support also, wherever possible,  a reduction in the number of internal checkings,  checks within the Organization. Do not let me be  misunderstood here: there is a need for these  checks. If you had worked in the Organization with  people with so many different ideas of administra-  tion, with some people with no idea of administra-  tion at all, who had never had any experience, you  would realize that some of these controls, which  may seem within a national system to be unduly  onerous, were at one stage in our life entirely  necessary—though not all perhaps; we must keep  a balance in these matters. We, you and I, are  responsible to our governments for the expenditure  of public monies. We are responsible to see that  there is no unreasonable waste within the Organ-  ization. Yet we must remember that in an Organ-  ization whose fundamental purpose is inspiration  and creation, the greatest waste of all may be that  efficient and systematized waste that comes from  using creative minds perpetually on routine work.  I am even tempted—not to be misunderstood  thereby—to say that in some ways 1 would even  stand for less co-ordination. 1 think it was a  tired member of the United Nations Secretariat  who invented the phrase, which may not be  translatable, that co-ordination is the thief of  time. Co-ordination, of course, is necessary, but  co-ordination does not consist in attending one  another’s meetings and writing one another's  reports. Co-ordination between all our Specialized  Agencies and co-ordination within the Secretariat  is not some super kind of activity carried on by  a superior individual. Co-ordination consists in  creative human beings working together on a  common concrete task that has meaning to all of  them.  [84] There is another factor, a more subtle one,  I think, that is a cause of this curious tension and  periodic unhappiness that tends to sweep the Secre-  tariat. And that is the gap that exists between  our high purposes, our high ideas, on the one  hand, and the means at our disposal, on the other.  And here, I am afraid, I am not speaking of  financial means. Even from 12,000 miles, the cold  eye of the New Zealand Treasury prevent my  speaking rashly there. I am speaking of the  techniques, of the tools, of the skill that we have  (I am speaking of myself as if I were still in the  Secretariat), that the Organization, as a whole, has.    The members of the Secretariat know, as we know,  that there is a sick world to be cured, and what  can they do about it? They can call a Conference,  they can write a paper, they can organize a  seminar, they can send a mission, they can make  a study; and even these skills they have only half  learnt. The conscientious member of the Secre-  tariat 1s all too often liable to feel that the world  expects him to raise a tower to the high heavens  and all he has to do it with are a few poor stone  tools. He feels sometimes as an atomic physicist  would feel if he were expected to split the atom  with nothing but the mental and physical equip-  ment of the alchemist of the Middle Ages. Now  here, 1 think, the professional Unescan does  himself an injustice. The Secretariat is doing in  the main a completely new kind of job. It is  doing a job which has been done in part before,  but which, in the main, has not been done before,  and of course the skills are inadequate. But one  of the most encouraging things about Unesco, to  us who have known it from its birth, is the  amazing advances we have made in the last four  years, just in the skills of our job, in the skills of  our crafts. Consider, for example, the organization  of this Conference. With the organization of our  first two Conferences, due to nobody's fault, we  just had not the skill; the amazing skill that is  now shown by the members of the Secretariat  is a thing that was impossible four years ago.  The very budget document that you have before  you, you have only to compare, in its form and its  reasonableness, with the kind of documents that  were the best we could produce four years ago.  Consider our skills in such things as the organ-  ization of seminars, in the exchange of persons;  the skills that consist in bringing human beings  of different countries, of different creeds together  in such conditions that they will like each other  better instead of worse, as a result of meeting.  There are certain tools that we do not yet know  how to use thoroughly. I have never felt, for  example, that we yet know how to use expert  committees to the full. The development of these  tools and these skills represents a very great
140  achievement, and one which we of the New  Zealand delegation feel must not be jeopardized by  anything else at all. I was interested to hear  Mr. Hardman’s remarks on these proposed short-  term plans. I have listened with interest to various  suggestions for our short-range programme, of  items more directly related to peace and war.  They stir the imagination and I shall listen eagerly  to concrete details. But 1 hope that our enthu-  siasm for these new and exciting ideas will not  reduce our effort to develop those solid skills  without which our noblest dreams will remain  just dreams. And when 1 hear each proposal, I  shall ask myself: will it interfere with the develop-  ment of the use of the fundamental skills which,  together with our faith and our friendliness, still  constitute the most valuable asset of Unesco? Now  this may seem a rather timid and pedestrian point  of view. I do not see it so. In these humble  skills that are developing, in the growing body of  men and women like ourselves, from all countries,  who know and understand how most effectively to  work together in the fields of education, science  and culture, I see the beginnings of a new and  exciting hope for the world.  [85] I remember, and the Chairman of the  Board will certainly remember, how in the Paris  Conference of 1946, a few of us played at the game  of nominating the gifts that we would ask from  some fairy godmother to this infant organization  that we had just seen born. If we were to play  that game again I should ask for this lusty four-  year-old nothing more spectacular than two or  three years of calmness, security and hard think-  ing, to learn to leave alone the things it cannot  yet do, and to do still better the many things that  it 1s already doing so well.
