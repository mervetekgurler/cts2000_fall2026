SEVENTH MEETING  Monday, 5 June 1950, at 9.30 a.m.  (Chairman:  Progress of the work of the Session (con-  tinuation).  [1] Mr. SEYDOUX (Chairman, Programme and  Budget Commission) recalled that the Programme  and Budget Commission had been allotted
24 meetings, but that he had asked from the outset  that there should be 28 in order to complete the very  heavy agenda. He proposed, therefore, to have two  meetings on Saturday, 10 June, and two on Tues-  day, 13 June, as well as three night meetings, Wed-  nesday, 7 June, Tuesday, 13 June and Wednesday,
14 June.  [2] He also asked for an accelerated procedure  to handle some 222 resolutions for the 1951 Pro-  gramme and some hundred amendments to new  proposals. The Executive Board had already gone  through the various proposals and new amend-  ments, and it was proposed to ask its Chairman  to present each new amendment as it came up  and to give the Board’s advice; the delegation  submitting the amendment would be asked briefly  to present its viewpoint. A vote would then be  taken as to whether the Commission wished to  discuss the draft amendment, or whether it should  be postponed until the following year.  [3] Mr. DELBOS (France) proposed the par-  llamentary practice of having one speaker for,  and one against, any amendments.  [4] Dr. CHAND (Chairman, Administrative  Commission) said that the Administrative Com-  mission would probably finish its work in the  next two meetings. It had already sent its resolu-  tion on the budget to the Programme and Budget  Commission, and it was for that Commission to  fix the ceiling of the budget. However, what-  ever ceiling was arrived at, Dr. Chand pointed out  that about 10 per cent of the assessed revenue  would have to be taken as unrealizable. The  Administrative Commission was also examining  the question of local salary scales; it was possible  that some saving might be effected there. The  largest defaulting country was China, and the
7 per cent allotted to that country might be said  to be unrealizable. There were also countries  which had not paid since 1947.
263  Count STEFANO Jacint (Italy)  [6] Sir John MAUD (Chairman, Executive  Board) thought that, if the Administrative Com-  mission could that afternoon let the Programme  and Budget Commission have a further report on  the matter, it might help the latter Commission  to fix a total figure. The particular point on  which he thought the Programme and Budget  Commission had not been clear was this: If the  full Programme of the Executive Board (for a  budget of $8,200,000) was actually to be carried  out, what total should Member States be asked  to contribute? Was it $8,000,000, $9,000,000,  $8,600,000  [6] Mr. SEYDOUX (Chairman, Programme and  Budget Commission) agreed with Sir John. But  a second question to which the Programme and  Budget Commission would like a reply was: if the  total budget was fixed at $8,200,000, could the  delegations be certain, in planning for 1951, that  this sum would in fact be available? In practice,  they knew that this was not so. If a ceiling of  $8,200,000 were voted, it would be fictitious,  since it was known that some $500,000 to  $600,000 would be lacking. The Commission  would have to discuss how the necessary sum  could be found—it might be by increasing the con-  tributions from Member States, it might be by  saving here or there.  [7] Dr. WALKER (Chairman, Nominations  Committee) agreed with Sir John that the Ad-  ministrative Commission should submit its report  that same afternoon. He also thought it desirable  that that Commission make a provisional report  regarding the savings that might be effected by  adopting local salary scales.  [8] The DIRECTOR-GENERAL was not very  optimistic about the reductions to be effected by  adopting local salary scales for certain grades in  the Secretariat. It was a delicate and serious prob-  lem for the Organization.  [9] Three years previously, the Conference had  fixed the salaries for personnel recruited in Paris:  in his opinion they had at that time committed  an error, for they had established a principle and  not applied it. The result was that, for the past  three years, the personnel in question—about 60        per cent of the Secretariat—had been receiving  salaries equal to those paid by the United Nations  in New York. Now reductions were asked for for  that personnel. He had had long discussions with  the Staff Association and he was now being  asked to make too hard a decision, because it was  inhuman.     [10] If the Conference decided to reduce those  salaries, they would be inconsistent, since the  argument for continuing to accept Nationalist  China as a member of the Organization was that  the Organization should not act before the United  Nations had acted. In applying local salary  scales, the Secretary-General of the United Nations  had decided to apply them to new posts, but not  to the existing personnel. Yet the present Confer-  ence was asking Unesco to go ahead of the United  Nations. Such conflicting decisions weakened  each other.  [11] The Director-General did not think that the  savings, if effected, would be very considerable.  That was why the Administrative Commission  should say to the Programme and Budget Com-  mission: “The figure of $8,200,000 cannot be a  real budget figure, because there will be a deficit.  You must vote for a ceiling of $8,500,000 in order  to have a real figure of $8,200,000.” To finance  a budget of $8,500,000—or whatever the figure  might be—they would either have to enlarge the  Organization’s reserve fund or ask Member States  for a loan to cover the deficit. Those were the  questions to which the Administrative Commis-  sion must reply.  [12] It should be remembered that each delega-  tion formed an entity, and that the decisions of  the other Commissions presumably would not  greatly differ from those taken in the Admini-  strative Commission. From the point of view of  procedure, it was certainly correct for the General  Committee to ask the Administrative Commission  to convey their decision to the Programme and  Budget Commission; in practice, however, the  Director-General did not think the decisions of the  Administrative Commission would be noticeably  different from those of the Programme and Bud-  get Commission. Their discussions on Natural  Sciences, on Education and other points would  remain ineffective as long as the total budget  figure was not known. The meeting rose at 10.45 a.m.  EIGHTH MEETING  Wednesday, 7 June 1950, at 9.30 a.m.  Chairman:  Work of the Programme and Budget Com-  mission.  [1] Mr. SEYDOUX (Chairman, Programme and  Budget Commission) stated that, after fixing the  provisional budget ceiling for 1951, the Pro-  gramme and Budget Commission had completed  its consideration of the programme for education  and embarked on the chapter covering natural  sciences. Only three questions relating to that  chapter had been settled, since most of the  technical experts were attending meetings at the  Palazzo Vecchio, and Mr. Seydoux was therefore  prepared to comply with the wishes of several  delegations and to begin consideration of the  whole chapter afresh; it could be completed that  day, and the Commission could then begin on the  social sciences chapter at an evening meeting and  continue with it the following day. If the discus-  sion progressed satisfactorily, the Commission  would be able to complete its work by the evening  of Tuesday, 13 June or by the morning of Wednes-  day, 14 June at the latest.  [2] Mr. Seydoux suggested holding the meeting  of the Joint Commission—Official and External  Relations, Programme and Budget in the after-  noon of Thursday, 9 June; it was essential that  representatives of the Allied High Commission  should take part in the discussion of Unesco’s  activities in Germany and they were due to leave  Florence in the evening of 9 June. The matter  had already been discussed by a sub-committee of  the Executive Board and would be taken up again  by the Board itself on 8 June.
