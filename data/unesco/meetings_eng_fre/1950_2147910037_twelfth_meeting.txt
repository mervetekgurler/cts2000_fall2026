Mr. Benton had attended the Prepara-  tory Conference in London and had headed his  country’s delegation to the Conferences in Paris

As Secretary of State  for Cultural Affairs, Mr. Benton had strongly en-  couraged all work connected with Unesco and had  personally contributed to the development of the  United States National Commission.  The Chairman called on Mr. Benton to present  a draft resolution [document 5C/59] framed by  the United States delegation and requesting the  adoption by Unesco of exceptional measures to  cope with an exceptional world situation.  The Chairman was sorry he could not open a  discussion on the draft that day, but nevertheless  thought it worth the Commission’s while to hear  Mr. Benton's statement forthwith.  [2] Mr. BENTON (United States of America)  regretted that it had been impossible for him to  join earlier that particularly important Session of  the General Conference. In recent weeks the  United States Senate had approved the extention  of ERP (European Recovery Programme), and  had discussed President Truman’s Point Four  proposals, in which Unesco would have an impor-  tant share. Part of a Senate resolution he had  been sponsoring called upon all Member States to  assist an acceleration of Unesco’s work so as to  make it a significant and perhaps decisive con-  tribution to peace. He urged a return to funda-  mentals and pointed out that in Unesco, educa-  tion, science and culture were not ends in them-  selves but means of making a direct contribution  to peace. This contribution, however, could  only be made by strict concentration and integra-  tion of the Programme. He quoted from Article I,  paragraph 1 of Unesco’s Constitution, and from  Articles 19, 26 and 27 of the Universal Declaration  of Human Rights, and emphasized that those in  Unesco who denied these fundamental principles  should not be permitted to retard the Organiza-  tion on the way to its great objective.  The political struggle raging in the world was  an intellectual, moral and educational struggle;  Unesco could not remain indifferent to the pres-  sures by which the minds of men were assailed  from all sides. Mr. Benton hoped the Conference  would agree with the Director-Generals argu-  ments for Unesco’s right to an active role in world  politics in order to ensure the implementation of  Article 26, i.e., “to promote understanding, toler-  ance and friendship among all nations”. The  occasionally expressed theory that Unesco must  soften its policies so that it could be a bridge be-  tween East and West was a chimera. The present  world situation called for measures over and  above the normal Programme and Budget; they  should represent a constructive and positive  affirmation of freedom for the sake of freedom  and an effort to bolster the fundamental freedoms  wherever they were threatened. Unesco must find  a way to tap all the resources of the free world,  and learn to use every means of mass communica-  tion to tell the glorious story of freedom.
