17.10 Next, I wishto dwell briefly on some of the issues on which the opinion of the General  Conference is sought. First, the aims of Unesco, We agree that the main concerns of  Unesco should be to further the pursuit of peace, to assist in development and to promote  human rights. We wish to observe, however, that it is impossible to consider the promo-  tion of human rights and the struggle against racialism and colonialism apart from the  problems of world peace. Indeed, peace, development and human rights are separate mani-  festations of a trinity.
17.11 First, then, there is the question of peace. The Director-General reminds us that  "the ultimate purpose of the Organization is to contribute to peace through international co-  operation in education, science, culture and communication", As he himself agrees, inter-  national co-operation in the various sectors of Unesco's activities is not enough, for with-  out tackling the root causes of friction and dissension, such co-operation will be but a mockery.  To achieve an atmosphere of peace is a very difficult task, too difficult for Unesco to at-  tempt alone. Member States must take positive steps to assist Unesco in this task by mini-  miz/ng the causes of tension between States.
17.12 Two of the main causes of international conflict are undue interference inthe affairs  of other nations and racialism. In its various manifestations, racialism makes it impos-  sible for people to live together in peace and harmony. It is racialism that makes nations  like Portugal refuse to give independence to peaple who are fighting and dying for the right  to be their own masters and order the! own affaire, It is racialism whichleads toiniqui-  tous actions, like that taken recently by the South African Government, of detaining people  found guiltless by well-constituted courts of Law - of detaining them, not because of what  they have done, but because of what it is feared they might do. How can peace existinthe  midst of such injustices, which are the direct result of racialism? It is Member States of  Unesco who must bear the brunt of the work of establishing peace in the world by courage-  ously condemning racialism wherever it exists, by refusing to practise it, by championing
10  the cause of oppressed peoples, and by genuinely supporting all United Nations programmes  of international co-operation. Peace cannot be achieved through resolutions alone. Two  days ago, on 14 October, to be exact, the United Nations General Assembly celebratedthe
10th anniversary of its resolution condemning colonialism. It is true that since 1960, when  the resolution wae passed, many former colonies have achieved political independence, but  it is equally true that many still groan under the yoke of colonialism in its various forms.  Resolutions, therefore, are not ensugh: Peace can be achieved only through positive action  by States. In this effort, we see Unesco's role as that of providing moral leadership and  initiating international action within its fields of competence, and in this rôle, Unesco has  already established an impressive record. But as we all know, there is still much to be  done.
17.13 The problems posed by development are frightening. The main problem of develop-  ment is that of equitable distribution of the world's riches and this question is one of philo-  sophy and conscience. As long as nations refuse to accept responsibility towards other  nations, exploitation of the poor by the rich will continue, the gap between rich and poor  nations will wider, and the world will sink deeper and deeper in misery. Here, as in the  pursuit of peace, Unesco can provide leadership by co-operating with nations ready to aid  development in developing countries. It is encouraging to learn that at least one great na-  tion has recently declared its intention to reduce the volume of bilateral aid and encourage  more aid through international organizations; we salute the courage of this nation and hope  that others will follow.suit. We support the Director-General's proposals for helping Mem -  ber States to programme international assistance, for we agree with him that "aid, of what-  ever kind, is meaningless and ineffective, if it is not integrated with national efforts”.
17.14 With reference to types and fields of action to be given priority, my delegationfeels  that time has not yet disproved the wisdom of the decisions of the General Conference of
1960 and 1964 which accorded priority to education and science respectively. We would  echo the Director-General's views that "the immediate needs of development in education  and science outweigh all others". Developing countries need education and the application  of science and technology to develop their national resources, to feed their people adequately,  to provide them with medical services and thus arrest the march of death, to make it pos-  sible to appreciate, when the wants of life have been satisfied, the pleasures of philosophy  and culture, We agree that soclal sciences and culture are important; indeed Unesco should  improve its programmes in these sectors. But the time to elevate either of them tothe  priority list has not yet arrived.
