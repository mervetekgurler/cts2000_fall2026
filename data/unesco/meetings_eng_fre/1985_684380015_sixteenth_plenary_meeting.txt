2. Le PRESIDENT :  Nous reprenons maintenant le débat de politique générale. Je donne la  parole à M. Jérémie Bonnelame, secrétaire principal au Ministère de l'éducation  et de l'information et chef de la délégation des Seychelles.
3.1 M. BONNELAME (Seychelles) :  Monsieur le Président, Monsieur le Directeur général, Excellence,  Mesdames et Messieurs, ma délégation resent un vif plaisir d'être ici aujoud'hui  afin de réaffirmer le constant soutien du gouvernement et du peuple des  Seychelles à l'Unesco. Elle voudrait aussi vous féliciter, Monsieur le Président,  de votre élection et vous souhaiter tout le succès possible au cours de ces  importants débats. Elle voudrait présenter également l'expression de ses sen-  timents les plus respectueux à notre distingué Directeur général, M. Amadou-  Mahtar M'Bow.
3.2 Sa profonde gratitude s'adresse également au gouvernement et au peuple  de la République populaire de Bulgarie pour leur chaleureux accueil de même que  pour toutes les facilités mises à la disposition de la Conférence générale de  l'Unesco pour le bon fonctionnement de cette vingt-troisième session.
3.3 Monsieur le Président, il y a maintenant quarante ans qu'une certaine  partie du monde passait du mortel désespoir de la guerre à l'espoir d'une  renaissance. L'Unesco fut conçue à cette occasion pour oeuvrer en faveur de  l'égalité entre les nations, égalité qui visait à créer pour l'humanité un monde,  un avenir commun. Nos similitudes paraissaient alors avoir plus d'importance que  nos différences, nos intérêts communs plus d'attrait que nos aspirations indi-  viduelles. En s'attelant à la tâche, l'Unesco fit du bien-être du monde  l'objectif fondamental de son activité et au cours de ces quatre décennies,
161 nations - qu'elles fussent riches ou pauvres, puissantes ou faibles -  s'engagèrent à travailler pour le bien commun de l'humanité, engagement qui  sembla transcender les différences de mode de vie, de croyance et d'organisation.
3.4 I1 n'est pas étonnant qu'un idéal comme celui-là ait souffert des  tensions d'un monde bien réel, confronté à de difficiles problèmes et à des vues  différentes. Ce qui est remarquable c'est qu'en dépit de la modération q'uil a  fallu apporter à nos grands espoirs avec le temps, l'Unesco ait pu être un moyen  efficace pour faire progresser nos causes humaines. Elle a ainsi combattu l'ana-  lphabétisme, formé nombre de nos instituteurs, fourni une aide technologique et
16  scientifique ; elle s'est portée au devant des catastrophes naturelles ; elle  nous a permis aussi de nous faire une opinion sur bien des questions essentielles  du devenir humain. Ce faisant elle s'est montrée suffisamment souple pour pouvoir  répondre aux besoins des périodes différentes.
3.5 Pendant les années qui suivirent la guerre, elle a travaillé à la mise  en place d'une coopération entre nations qui favorisait la paix. Au cours des  années 60 et 70 et jusque dans les années 80, elle a aidé à transformer des  sociétés colonisées en de nouvelles sociétés indépendantes. Elle a accordé, par  exemple, son soutien moral et financier aux efforts que faisaient les Seychelles  en faveur de la démocratisation de l'enseignement, de la production locale de  matériel pédagogique et de l'adoption de leur langue maternelle comme langue  véhiculaire d'enseignement, et à ceux qu'elles faisaient pour promouvoir leur  culture et pour mettre les sciences et la technologie au service du développement  national. Plus récemment, l'Unesco nous a aidé à accorder plus d'attention à la  nécessité de nouveaux ordres mondiaux, y compris le nouvel ordre mondial de  l'information et de la communication, dans le but d'atténuer d'une façon  rationnelle, et par une bonne planification, les déséquilibres existant au lieu  d'attendre pour le faire que de sérieux conflits éclatent.
3.6 Après quarante ans, l'Unesco se voit secouée par des forces de division  qui, paradoxalement, vienn'ent clairement démontrer que le monde a plus que jamais  besoin d'elle. En effet les principes sur lesquels l'Unesco a été fondée sont  toujours valables. Notre monde a soif d'instruction soif d'esprit de coopération,  soif de culture. Nos cultures locales subissent le choc des cultures dominantes  par la voix omniprésente des médias et risquent même de s'en trouver annihilées.  La science et la technologie ont tendance, quant à elles, à déformer nos sociétés  en les entraînant vers l'âge postindustriel tout en laissant entier pour l'homme  le problème de son adaptation à un avenir qui échappe dangereusement à son  contrôle. Avec le temps, notre besoin de l'Unesco n'a donc pas diminué.
3.7 Ce qui nous semble avoir diminué au fil des ans, Monsieur le Président,  c'est notre capacité de percevoir que le bien commun est dans notre propre  intérêt. Certaines nations ne semblent pas se rendre compte que les intérêts de
1 'Unesco sont finalement compatibles avec leurs propres intérêts, voire complé-  mentaires de ceux-ci. Certaiens d'entre elles rejettent même le principe de  l'égalité des nations.
3.8 I1 n'y a pas de solutions faciles aux différents problèmes auxquels  l'Unesco a à faire face. Pour notre part nous croyons que la meilleure approche  est de continuer de construire une organisation qui consoliderait les engagements  des Etats membres, de promouvoir les changements nécessaires au bien-être de  l'humanité et de concevoir des programmes spécifiques qui seraient bénéfiques à  chaque Etat membre. On fait déjà beaucoup dans ce sens, mais beaucoup reste à  faire. A ce stade, ma délégation voudrait formuler certaines observations sur la  direction que de tels efforts devraient prendre.
3.9 D'abord, ma délégation pense qu'il faut que nous continuions d'apporter  notre soutien sans faille au Secrétariat et à son Directeur général. Pour les  mesures déjà prises afin d'améliorer encore les systèmes d'administration et de  recrutement et les procédures budgétaires, l'Unesco doit être au-dessus de tout  reproche, si cela est possible. Nous ne devons pas donc permettre à quiconque de  déceler des fautes ou des faiblesses qui leur serviraient de prétexte pour saper  toute l'Organisation et affaiblir notre engagement. Nous devrions tous prendre  note de l'initiative prise par le Directeur général de créer cinq groupes de  travail chargés d'étudier les questions de l'évaluation, du personnel, de  l'information du public et de la budgétisation et de mener à bien un examen  complet du programme lui-même. Cette initiative a eu un impact favorable sur la  rédaction du programme et du budget pour la période 1986-1987.
3.10 Nous devons néanmoins, parallèlement, poursuivre nos recherches et nos  échanges sur les questions qui nous divisent actuellement. Nous ne pouvons pas  nous permettre de reculer devant cette tâche sous prétexte que ces questions sont  litigieuses. La stabilité de notre monde dépend de l'établissement d'équilibres  plus justes et de nouveaux ordres, y compris un nouvel ordre de l'information et  de la communication. Vu les succès que nous avons obtenus ensemble dans des  domaines comme 1 ' éducation et les sciences, nous avons beaucoup d' espoir qu' avec  le temps et dans l'intérêt de l'humanité, nous finirons par réunir un consensus à  propos des questions qui nous divisent aujourd'hui.
3.11 Nombre d'entre nous ont, par exemple, pris l'engagement de soutenir le  nouvel ordre mondial de l'information et de la communication, qui permettrait aux  pays en développement d'échanger des informations, de diffuser leurs propres  nouvelles dans le monde, d'interpréter d'une façon qui ait un sens dans leurs  sociétés les informations internationales et de protéger leurs propres intérêts
591    dans le domaine de la communication. L'ouverture d'esprit, de constantes dis-  cussions et un programme d'action sont essentiels pour l'établissement de ce  nouvel ordre. C'est pourquoi l'organisation mondiale qu'est l'Unesco doit per-  sister dans sa résolution de promouvoir cet ordre plus juste pour le plus grand  bien de l'humanité tout entière.
3.12 L'adoption d'un système de classement selon leur priorité des programmes  de l'Unesco devrait faciliter l'établissement d'une base commune - même lorsqu'il  s'agit de questions litigieuses - sur une base sur laquelle nous pourrions tous  nous entendre pour accomplir quelque chose de valable. Cette innovation devrait  aussi nous aider à mobiliser nos énergies sur la mise en oeuvre de programmes de  durée précise, plus concentrés et visant des buts réalisables.
3.13 Enfin, Monsieur le Président, un dernier mot sur le Programme de par-  ticipation. Ce 'Programme a permis en particulier aux pays en développement de  recevoir une aide adaptée avant tout à leurs besoins. I1 a ainsi permis la  réalisation de projets ayant une valeur concrète pour notre peuple. C'est ce  genre de participation qui permettra de renforcer l'Unesco et il nous faut à tout  pris l'élargir. Bien que les fonds pour le Programme de participation doivent  être augmentés substantiellement en 1986-1987, ils ne constituent que 5 % seu-  lement du budget total. Cela n'est pas suffisant, surtout si nous prenons en  considération les priorités énoncées de décentralisation et de meilleurs équi-  libres entre projets d'études et de recherches d'intérêt global et projets d'un  caractère plus opérationnel.
3.14 Au cours de cette Conférence, Monsieur le Président, nous allons  débattre d'importantes questions. Ne perdons cependant pas de vue la première  raison qui nous a conduits tous ici : faire en sorte que l'Unesco puisse pour-  suivre ses activités dans toute la sérénité possible. Nous pouvons avoir des vues  différentes sur l'Unesco alors que nous abordons les années 90. Certains d'entre  nous voudraient réduire son rôle, pour l'essentiel, à celui d'une Organisation  d'aide aux pays dans les domaines strictement' limités de sa compétence. D'autres  préféreraient voir en l'Unesco également une superstructure, à une échelle  globale, pouvant inspirer des projets majeurs d'ordre international dans l'in-  térêt de toute l'humanité.
3.15 Ma délégation se rallie à cette deuxième position. C'est d'ailleurs le  rôle qu'assume actuellement l'Unesco : tout en intensifiant le soutien qu'elle  apporte aux Etats membres pour les aider à résoudre des problèmes de caractère  national, elle se concentre sur des projets d'intérêt global. Monsieur le  Président, nous avons pu en 1946 oublier nos différences pour créer l'Unesco.  Quarante années plus tard nous nous devons d'affirmer notre volonté de faire  abstraction de nos différences afin de permettre à l'Unesco de remplir pleinement  sa mission. Je vous remercie, Monsieur le Président.

I would like to submit, Mr President, that the draft pro-  gramme and budget before the distinguished delegates reveals beyond any doubt  that the wide-ranging consultations carried out and the comprehensive guidelines  laid down by the Executive ßoard at its 120th session have been effectively and  closely followed.
5.3 Mr President, allow me further to express through you to the Government  and people of Bulgaria my delegation's sincere appreciatioq and gratitude for  their kind hospitality to all delegates and Unesco staff and for the wonderful  arrangements they have made to enable this Conference to be successful.
5.4 Mr President, allow me also to convey to you and the distinguished  delegates greetings and best wishes from my President, General Tito Okello Lutwa,  and from the Government and people of Uganda. I wish to take this opportunity to  inform you and fellow participants that the recent change in government in Uganda  was brought about as a result of a prolonged struggle for peace, human rights and  development which this Organization stands for.
5.5 The Government of Uganda has invited all contending groups to lay down  their arms and join together to form a government of national unity. This call  has been responded to by four fighting groups so far. The fifth group is holding  talks with government in the capital of Kenya, Nairobi, about its participation  in the current government. My country would like to register her appreciation to  our neighbours, Kenya and Tanzania, for the role they are playing to bring about  peace in my country. My  delegation further welcomes the greater prominence given to training activities  and the substantial emphasis laid on the participation programme.
5.7 Mr President, my delegation believes that decentralization offers the  only way of bringing Unesco into closer contact with its Member States, with non-  governmental organizations and with individuals. For this reason, we welcome the  new drive to decentralize the administration of the Organization along the lines  of the recommendations of the Temporary Committee of the Executive Board. In  particular, we would like to see the Regional Offices entrusted with more admin-  istrative and financial power to plan and implement all regional and subregional  co-operation programmes as well as projects carried out in Member States under  contract. In addition, the Regional Offices should be empowered to handle all  small requests from Member States under the participation and regular programmes.
5.8 After these brief remarks, allow me, Mr President, to react to some of  the major programmes and related activities. Major Programme I, Reflection on  world problems and future-oriented studies, is of paramount importance in the  preservation of peace and the promotion of development. Man must not be con-  trolled and directed by the world: man should control and direct the world to  benefit mankind. I consequently submit that Major Programme I offers the best  opportunity of preparing for that noble task, and Uganda supports its continu-  ation up to the end of the Second Medium-Term Plan when its effectiveness can be  assessed. My government appeals to those Member States which do not see the need  for this major programme to bear with the majority view and give the programme  some benefit of doubt.
5.9 Conscious of the limited resources of the Organization, my delegation  fully supports option 2 for Major Programme I as recommended by the Executive  Board in paragraph 36 of document 23 C/6. We believe that this option offers a  good compromise. In particular, we welcome the establishment of a co-ordinating  committee to be responsible for determining the research topics proper to the  network and co-ordinating its activities: working out inter alia, as the network  spreads, its operating rules and procedures for co-operation among its members.  In accordance with section l(c) of paragraph 01306 (document 23 C/5) relating to  the analytical research network, my delegation wished to place on record the  readiness and willingness of our national institutions to participate in this  undertaking.
5.10 Mr President, Uganda attaches a great deal of importance to Major Pro-  gramme II for we believe that education is the right of every citizen and the hub  and pillar of development. We also believe that through the approach of edu-  cation for all we can eradicate 'illiteracy in our society. However, current  world inflation has made education very expensive, especially in developing  countries where most education materials have to be imported. In Uganda, where  education is not yet free, the burden of paying school fees has become a deter-  rent factor to poor parents in sending their children to school, thus making the  attainment of universal primary education a difficult task. Nevertheless, the  current government policy on school fees has introduced a 50 per cent subsidy on  primary school fees to enable as many children of school-going age as possible to  receive primary education. This, to us, is a major step forward towards the  universalization of primary education.
5.11 At the post-primary level, a number of brilliant students are often  unable to continue with their education due to lack of finance. The government  has therefore'decided to meet 75 per cent of the cost of maintaining a student in  a day-secondary school and 65 per cent of the cost of maintaining a student in a  boarding secondary school at both 'O' and 'A' levels. The government has also  decided to offer free education to all orphans and fully subsidizes primary edu-  cation for children from the disadvantaged arid area of Karamoja.

Finally, my  delegation wishes to record its appreciation of the proposed increase in the  resource allocation for adult education. As educated adults appreciate education  of their children more than illiterate ones, this increase will, in the long run,  lead to acceleration of the education of the youngsters.

It would therefore  only be fair for the United States to assess Unesco's attempts at self-renewal  positively and return to assist in the task.
5.24 Mr President, let us all renew our faith in Unesco and rededicate our-  selves in the service of the Organization in order to achieve the meaningful  reform that is being called for. It is the contention of my delegation that  problems of Unesco cannot be solved by reducing its universality. Thank you for  your attention.

We are proud and  appreciative of the Director-General who, rising to the challenges of the moment,  has demonstrated so much of his qualities of leadership in ensuring the continued  functioning of. our Organization and preserving its integrity. As we approach the fortieth birthday of the United  Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, it is the sincere hope  of my government that the feeling of maturity, to which we are now entitled, will  dominate this twenty-third General Conference, leading us towards the enlightened  consensus which we all desire, and away from the confrontation to which we have  been exposed since our last General Conference.
7.7 The Organization has been criticized on the grounds perhaps that the  ideals which heralded its birth are no longer acceptable in the radically changed  times in which we now live, or perhaps because it is thought that its state of  health will not permit it to accept the challenges with which we are faced today.  Mr President, I submit to this Conference that the ideals are still relevant, and  that the fitness course which has been undergone in the past 18 months, together  with the bill of health which has been given by a large number of responsible  bodies, belies this position. Now is the moment, therefore, for us to reflect  together, inside the Organization to which we belong, and reach conclusions which  measure up to the needs of humanity as a whole, and which put to full use the  experience, the organizational capacity, and the knowledge which it has accumu-  lated over those 40 years.
7.8 The Unesco Constitution declares, most correctly, that 'ignorance of  each other's ways and lives has been a common cause (. . . ) of suspicion and mis-  trust'; and I think that it is most appropriate that I should ask your indul-  gence, at this moment of apparent differences, to offer you a view of the way in  which we, as one of the least developed members in the world community, see  Unesco. I represent here today a country, the vast majority of whose populations  are still far from participation in educational programmes as they are understood  in the industrialized countries; who have only minimal contact with the science  and technology which is available elsewhere; and who, in their daily lives, have  little contact or communication with the wider world. On the other hand they have  a profound and rich cultural heritage, largely unknown to the outside world,  which with very limited resources we are striving to maintain at a time when  modernization, although carrying with it the seeds of danger to cultural roots,  is the order of the day. We have placed great trust in Unesco to assist us in  preserving the material evidence of our cultural heritage which is an important  part of the cultural property of humanity as a whole. In our relentless effort to  provide access to cultural publications and to expand educational opportunities  at a rate which has any real meaning to the vast majority of our people, we  believe Unesco can help us develop indigenous capacity appropriate to our current  situation. Much as we have to rely on our own resources, we shall continue to  turn to Unesco for inspiration and encouragement, and for technical assistance,  as one of the mechanisms available to us for the presentation and explanation of  our urgent material needs to the world community.
7.9 External support for our national effort has been limited by the con-  straints of which we are all aware, but it has been provided in ways which are  important to us. Allow me Mr President, to give some examples. The continued  moral and the material support provided by Unesco to our National Campaign for  the Eradication of Illiteracy have been vital factors in bringing this campaign  to a point where the majority of our people are now participating and benefiting.  Support has taken many forms. It has stretched from the personal intervention of  the Director-General in his International Appeal to the .organization by the  Secretariat of a number of professional and technical meetings from which our  operational personnel have derived great benefit; and it includes the publicizing  of our national efforts by Unesco, which has brought significant material support  from the international community. We are similarly encouraged and supported by
16  Unesco, in co-operation with other international organizations such as UNICEF, in  the very serious challenge to universalize primary education. We have the prac-  tical support of Unesco, through the regular programme, in our efforts to main-  tain our historic monuments and to preserve our national heritage. Most recently  we have had very positive evidence of the ability of Unesco to respond most mean-  ingfully to the needs of Member States. The decline of ecological conditions, in  the aftermath of the most serious and sustained drought and famine which has  afflicted our country, has obliged us to set in motion a rehabilitation programme  designed to offer a new chance in life for large numbers of the survivors of this  human tragedy. Unesco responded immediately by sending a technical term for the  preparation of projects which will provide basic educational facilities to com-  munities now being established in new locations. I am happy to report to this  General Conference that the project document has just been completed and will be  made available to Member States and funding agencies for their sympathetic con-  sideration. All this support by Unesco, which is largely related to action at the  national level, has a value to us for transcending the budgetary sums which have  been expended.
7.10 There has been much talk recently, Mr President, of maintaining a more  satisfactory balance in Unesco's programmes between action and reflection.  Although we in the developing countries are very much preoccupied with practical  needs, and the promotion of effective action in all areas which fall within the  competence of Unesco, we are also conscious of the importance of reflection and  the need for a continuing emphasis upon thought and analysis in Unesco's pro-  grammes. If Unesco does not continue to fulfil its original task of providing  world intellectual leadership, and if the intellectual activity which supports  this does not relate to the actual realities of our world situation, then we are  indeed lost.
7.11 I can provide two qeneral illustrations of the practicality of this  thought.
7.12 First, the circumstances in which the developing countries are  struggling today to modernize their social and economic systems and provide a  more satisfactory basis of life for their populations, who together represent the  vast majority of mankind, are very different from the historical circumstances in  which the advanced world reached its present level of development. This situ-  ation, coupled with the fact that there is a desperate need to identify strat-  egies which will result in an accelerated rate of development, very clearly  suggests continued reflection and study.
7.13 We are anxious that we should continue to be offered the opportunity of  participation at the international level in the processes of thinking which can  led to the identification of more viable and effective strategies. We therefore  place great importance on Major Programmes I and VI11 and on these aspects of  Major Programmes IV and IX in education, science and technology which relate to  these issues. Thus, in Major Programme I, which deals with current world problems  and their projection into the future, it is clearly not possible to separate the  elements in this study. It is absurd to study the future without defining the  present, and it is purposeless to study the present without drawing conclusions  about future prospects for our global community. Major Programme VI11 is the one  area in Unesco's proposals for action which offers an opportunity for an inter-  disciplinary approach to the very challenging issues which face the developing  world. In the education sector, Major Programme IV recognizes that many of the  situations with which the developing world is faced are new to government  experience, and that effective functioning depends upon the exchange of new  experience which Unesco can promote. Then, we have the unique possibility with  the United Nations system under Major Programme IX of treating science and tech-  nology as part of the total process of development and of relating work in this  sector to the real needs of society.
7.14 Unesco support for reflection is not only important at the international  level. We are also finding that a process of reflection, embodying Unesco par-  ticipation, can be applied to the more specific needs arising at regional and  national levels. As we are all aware, the former Is an important aspect of the  process of 'decentralization'. At the national level in Ethiopia we have derived  great benefit from processes of general review, together with Unesco expertise,  of various developmental issues. We have had a professional review of our third-  level system of education, of our mechanisms and strategies for the promotion of  science and technology, and of the ways in which movement towards the universal-  ization of primary education and literacy can be accelerated. We are about to  embark on a general discussion of problems and possibilities in the field of  adult education with professional assistance from Unesco. We regard all these as  exercises in reflection, but their value and their success depends upon the  ability to continue to draw upon an expanding reservoir of knowledge and under-  standing derived from the wider processes by which Unesco provides intellectual  leadership.
7.15 Secondly, practical experience in the developing world has shown that  our efforts are constantly frustrated by the irrationalities and the disequi-  libria of gen'eral world conditions. I am now opening the dangerous discussion,  which some feel has resulted in the so-called 'politicization' of Unesco. We must  not talk, we are told, of peace, disarmament, the new economic order, trans-  national corporations, the improvement of global communication conditions or even  the evil of apartheid, because these burning issues may raise the question of the  rights of peoples. We raise them in Unesco because they have a direct impact on  our ability to implement programmes to which we have all subscribed in Unesco.  Every year we must export more bags of coffee to build and equip the same primary  school which in the past cost less. Every year we must pay more for the expert  assistance which we rely upon for the transfer of technology. Every year we find  that the conditions under which we can acquire and maintain essential equipment  have deterior,ated. Every year we are obliged to borrow additional funds on  increasingly unfavourable terms to support the minimal plans which have evolved  in this very forum. Thus every yea? we work harder but we earn less and our debt  increasingly becomes burdensome. When disaster hits - as it has done in the  droughts in Ethiopia and other African countries, most of us have neither the  reserves nor the resilience to recover without great cost and sacrifice.
7.16 For our part, in the developing countries, we are struggling to mobilize  material and human resources to secure the basic conditions of life and to estab-  lish the possibility of climbing out of the pit of dependence towards a higher  level of self-reliance and progress.
7.17 Mr President, since we last met in this General Conference, the United  Nations in New York has seen fit to consider a special report on 'The Critical  Economic Situation in Africa' and this led to resolution 39/29 of the General  Assembly calling upon the world community to consider the specific problems which  face our continent. It is very clear that, in common with other developing  regimes, we are victims of the general environment of world economic mismanage-  ment, and that this is related to the whole atmosphere of persistent distrust and  antagonism in which there is increasing insecurity and mounting levels of  armament.
7.18 This is why, Mr President, we place considerable importance on and  attach priority to Unesco's Major Programes I, XII and XIII. And this is why we  find it difficult to understand that a discussion of these larger issues, which  might lead to a more rational world order, and which might reduce the very  practical constraints we face in carrying out programmes agreed at the General  Conference, is resisted in certain quarters and labelled as 'politicization'. We  seek only the continuing intellectual inquiry which is a concern of all peoples,  and which offers us the only possible way out of this impasse. We welcome,  Mr President, the occasion of the International Year of Peace as an opportunity  to tell the world that current behaviour not only puts us in the shadow of  nuclear holocaust, but is a current and practical constraint on efforts to raise  the conditions of life of the large majority of the inhabitants of this globe.
7.19 Mr President, for the people of Africa, and the whole of progressive  humanity, nothing would seem to stand in sharper antithesis to the noble objec-  tives of Unesco than the evils of the system of apartheid in southern Africa. In  South Africa itself, mass arrests and political murders are being carried out  almost daily by the racist regime to suppress the ever-growing popular resistance  against its rule. With impunity, the racist regime in Pretoria has waged a cease-  less war of aggression against Angola and the other Front-line States combined  with acts of sabotage and terrorism. In defiance of insistent resolutions of the  United Nations, the Organization for African Unity and the Non-Aligned Movement  and in utter contempt of international public opinion, it continues to tighten  its colonial grip over Namibia and strengthen the structure of apartheid.
7.20 If it is necessary to break the backbone of the regime through economic  sanctions, which the international community overwhelmingly demands, we must also  be aware that the struggle must be waged in the minds of men to combat the  doctrine that provides its ideological underpinnings. Apartheid, therefore, con-  stitutes a threat to peace and security in southern Africa and to the world in  general. It is in this context that we place great importance on Major Pro-  gramme XII.
7.21 Mr President, in this plenary session, we are concerned with some broad  and fundamental questions. I have given you an honest statement of our position  because I feel that this is a necessary background to the points I am about to  make on the major issues which face this twenty-third General Conference of  Unesco.
7.22 Our first thought is that it has not necessarily been unproductive for a  large organization like Unesco to have been forced into a re-examination of its  programmes and its modus operandi. While we regret that this was done in a way  which threatened to challenge the very principles of international co-operation  within the United Nations system, we believe that the rethinking of many issues  has produced a positive basis for a new consensus.
7.23 The outcome, which is presented to this General Conference, has been the  result of a large amount of additional work and study on the part of the  Director-General himself, the Secretariat, the Executive Board, the Temporary  Committee of the Executive Board, and the five consultative working groups set up  by the Director-General, to mention only the major operations which have been  undertaken. Our delegation acknowledges the timely distribution of the documents  which have stemmed from these actions, and records our thanks to the Secretariat  for the efficient way in which it has shouldered this additional burden.
7.24 Having studied the various special reports together with the deliber-  ations of the Executive Board, our conclusion is that all that can be said about  major issues has already been said - and most eloquently. This General Confer-  ence, as the sovereign body of our Organization, has every right to explore  further as it sees fit, but it must be recognized that rarely has the ground been  prepared so thoroughly for decision-making. This fact, Mr President, seems to  have been appreciated by delegates to this Conference as our time has not been  occupied by a detailed restatement of positions which are already clear. In broad  terms, what has emerged is a profound vindication of the Second Medium-Term Plan  and its co-ordinated approach to continuing work in the fields of activity within  Unesco's mandate. In no sense has the conceptual framework of this Plan been  seriously challenged. There have been suggestions for a modification of emphasis,  and long debates about terminology and phraseology. In our opinion, a serious  case for a narrower view, or for the elimination of major objectives for study  and action, has not been established. This is gratifying, Mr President, because  the medium-range planning approach was a great advance in the Unesco strategy for  action. It took many years of careful deliberation to develop, and it stands as a  pioneering achievement in the United Nations system.
7.25 The real argument has focused rather on the translation of the Plan into  biennial plans for action, and in particular on the proposals for 1986-1987, the  middle period of the six-year strategy, in the context of unexpected financial  constraint and a great deal of controversy over some specific actions. These were  not easy questions to resolve. It is, however, the firm position of my government  that the proposals submitted to this Conference by the Executive Board represent  a very realistic and a very positive framework for consensus. The options identi-  fied show where real alternatives exist, and the proposals for priority, in  response to budgetary constraints, emerge as a clear invitation to this Confer-  ence to exercise its sovereignty in a responsible manner. But the options and  priorities do not detract in any important way from the original purposes of the  Medium-Term Plan.
7.26 The Government of Ethiopia has reservations on some of the proposals  which are now submitted by the Executive Board. One major issue is the proposal  for zero growth in the budget. Our case would be that the real and urgent needs  of the developing world could not be met even if Unesco's budget was to be calcu-  lated on a positive growth rate, let alone on the proposed zero growth rate. We  are also very concerned that a proper balance should be maintained between  frontier and applied research, on the one hand, and attention to the development  of effective mechanisms for the dissemination of existing knowledge and skills to  the developing world, on the other. We are not entirely happy that this is the  case in the proposed funding of programme actions. We find, in a number of  places, that the budgets allocated for the basic actions, which can have a large  multiplier effect, compare very unfavourably with much larger budgets, allocated  to more sophisticated programmes, for which there are viable alternatives outside  Unesco. We are also not convinced that regional allocations in these major pro-  grammes match actual needs. However, these are points to be taken up in the  Commissions.
7.27 Finally, Mr President, al.low me to comment on the processes of change  which have been initiated. Unesco has been unduly criticized by various Member  and ex-Member States of the Organization, and the publicity arising from such  criticism has been exploited by some news media without any restraint from gross  distortion and abuse. Against the latter, it is commendable that both the  Director-General and the Secretariat have stood firm, and have chosen instead to  respond positively to issues raised by Member States and their representatives.  An evaluation of the degree to which justified criticism has been met by correc-  tive measures is one of the tasks to which this twenty-third session of the  General Conference must address itself. Our own view is that, over the whole  range of adjustment which a consensus would justify as necessary, a positive
599    c  trend has been set in motion. Real attention has been paid to the reorganization  of the Secretariat and to the strengthening of decentralized operations, while  maintaining the global unit of the Organization and its programe. The programme  itself has been subjected to the most searching review ever, and has preserved  its integrity of purpose and action. In the process it has certainly emerged with  an improved form of presentation which reveals clearly the degree of concen-  tration which has been achieved.
7.28 We realize on the other hand, Mr President, that the withdrawal of a  Member State has bequeathed to our Organization a host of problems involving  financial obligations and personnel issues which we believe are susceptible of  legal clarification.
7.29 We anticipate that discussions on the budget will crystallize in a form  which will ultimately represent consensus. One factor in this situation is the  economic disparity which grows wider every year and which prohibits the Member  States from the developing world from making a larger financial contribution, but  also places them in a position where their needs for support from the Organiz-  ation grow larger every year.
7.30 The, view of my government is that Unesco, covering as it does in an  integrated strategy for develoment the related questions of human resources, the  application of science and technology, the raising of social and cultural stand-  ards and the improvement of communications, is in a unique position to assist the  world in the initiation of new trends. A truly co-operative programme between  North and South based on a real appreciation, derived from the broader studies  which Unesco is following, of the situation in which humanity now finds itself,  can only contribute to a higher level of welfare and a greater degree of self-  reliance and self-sufficiency in those countries which now need a large volume of  assistance.
7.31 Mr President, the central issue before the present session of the  General Conference concerns the destiny of our Organization, which has so far  withstood the shock generated by the withdrawal of a Member State. I have  advisedly used the word 'shock' and not 'crisis'. In our view, prior to the with-  drawal of the Member State, there was no situation that could have been charac-  terized as crisis within Unesco. As in all other world bodies, Unesco has  debated - and with all the greater reason as an all-embracing institution with an  intellectual vocation - the great issues of our time: peace, development of human  rights. The crisis would seem to lie not in any attempt to come to grips with  these issues but rather in a refusal to engage in debate on them.
7.32 I am confident, Mr President, that our deliberations will result in the  triumph of the ideals in pursuit of which Unesco was founded and in its restor-  ation to the authentic grandeur it deserves. To that end, I pledge to this Con-  ference the full co-operation of my delegation. Thank you.

2 M. Robert Harris, président du Comité permanent des organisations internatio-  nales non gouvernementales ayant le statut consultatif (catégories A et B) auprès  de l'Unesco.
11.1 Mr HARRIS (Standing Committee of International Non-Governmental Organ-  izations in Consultative Relationship (categories A and B) with Unesco):  Mr President, distinguished delegates, I have the honour of speaking on  behalf of the Standing Committee of Non-Governmental Organizations, elected by  the international non-governmental organizations having category A or B consul-  tative status with Unesco.
11.2 At this time of reassessment of Unesco's role and effectiveness, some  have posed the question: 'What was the unifying feature which justified the  formation forty years ago of a single United Nations agency to deal with the  multiplicity of issues inherent in education, science and culture?' Centuries  ago, John Amos Comenius proposed that peace be pursued through co-operation  between the scholars of nations. But what an audacious act in the twentieth  century to create a specialized agency whose primary field of action was to be  the human mind! For the unifying feature which justified the formation of this  new agency was a profound belief in the value of sharing and developing knowl-  edge - of the value of learning to individuals and to societies.
11.3 The Specialized Agency so created fitted the pattern of the post-war  United Nations system. It was composed of Member States; it was an agency con-  cerned with the mind, but directed by governments. The potential difficulty was  recognized. From the outset the founders seriously considered other structures  which would have made Unesco non-governmental rather than intergovernmental.  Finally, they decided to establish in the Constitution provision for close co-  operation with international non-governmental organizations, 'and provision for  National Commissions, in the hope of reaching out beyond governmental structures.
11.4 Let me add here a comment on terminology. Isn't it strange that we  define these organizations in terms of what they are not! They are not govern-  mental, so we call them non-governmental organizations or NGOs. What they are is  organizations of people - people who have something in common, which has led them  to associate together locally, nationally, regionally, internationally. And the  linking of people together through associations which reach across national  boundaries is a striking new phenomenon of our time.
11.5 Some 550 of these international organizations have sufficient identity  of interest with Unesco to have sought, and to have been awarded, consultative  status. Through the interventions of their representatives - and you will hear  them during the Conference, in the commissions in the plenary - not only at the  Conference but also regularly throughout the year, Unesco receives the views of  the people whose activities touch on every aspect of its programme. Through co-  operation with these associations, Unesco is able to extend many times over the  impact, the outreach of its limited resources. For these are the workers of the  mind - the scientists from every learned discipline, the teachers, the writers,  the journalists, the artists, the museum and library workers, those in cinema and  television, and many others.
11.6 The membership of these organizations may be limited and highly special-  ized or it may be extensive - reaching out to the minds of millions of trade  unionists, of women, of young people. Their organizational identity may be  defined vocationally, or it may be defined in terms of issues - peace, human  rights, the struggle against racism, the conservation of nature. And these  organizations of people reflect all the philosophical, cultural, religious and  ideological diversity of our human societies.
11.7 Their role is crucial to development. For they mobilize people; they  enable people to participate in their own development and that of their  societies. This was the theme of a symposium convened by the Standing Committee  on the role of NGOs in developing countries. We seek to extend that work of the  regional level during the next biennium, in close co-operation with National  Commissions.
11.8 That NGOs with a diversity of memberships can work effectively together,  and with the Secretariat, has been demonstrated by the work of the collective  consultations on youth and literacy, both of which have prepared joint statements  for the General Conference, by the youth round table convened by the Standing  Committee prior to the Barcelona Congress, and by our working groups on the  status of women and on communication.
11.9 That NGOs can actually help to bridge the gaps which may exist between  Unesco's own sectors has been shown by our working groups on human rights, action  against racism, and peace - concerns which must be reflected throughout Unesco's  programme.
11.10 More than this, we have endeavoured to focus on that aspect which is so  easily overlooked in the process of multilateral diplomacy which characterizes
603    large international conferences. I refer to the real human needs to which  Unesco's programme should respond. This was the rationale for a recent symposium  on the family and development. Moreover this response to real human needs is the  consistent theme of our discussions with the Secretariat on ways of improving  public information about Unesco - its goals, its achievements, its ongoing  activities. One of the most fundamental human needs is the need for knowledge,  the need to learn, the denial of which perpetuates the most profound inequity and  discrimination.
11.11 And still we search for ways of better articulating, highlighting and  explaining the mission of an organization devoted to the extension of the human  mind. Next spring we tackle the question of scientific and artistic creativity -  once again, an intersectoral theme.
11.12 Mr Pr'esident, these few examples of collective NGO activity could be  multiplied: they must be multiplied many times over, with the activities of  individual NGOs, in order to give a picture of the totality of the work being  undertaken. Yes, these Organizations of people are vital to the implementation  of Unesco's programme, whether as operational vehicles, as generators of ideas,  or as a means of providing feedback on the effectiveness of programes. Unesco  cannot do without them.
11.13 The modalities of co-operation between Unesco and the NGOs have become  well-established. The strong support for maintenence of these modalities evinced  by the Director-General and the Member States is appreciated.
11.14 But the time is fast approaching when we must undertake a review of the  whole nature of relations between Unesco and organizations of people. The  phenomenon of associations is a dynamic one. People are organizing themselves at  all levels of society - from the local to the international - on a scale never  seen before. Quite frankly, Unesco is far from using the existing capacities of  these organizations, let alone responding to the future possibilities which are  rapidly developing.
11.15 Permit me a broader reflection - a personal reflection, but one which I  think accurately reflects the experience of NGOs in their co-operation with  Unesco. Perhaps because of the inherent diversity contained within its fields of  competence, Unesco seems weighed down by its decision-making procedures. It has  been the experience of NGOs that the money-value of secretariat person-hours  entailed in reaching a decision on even a small-scale project will often exceed  the grant contracted for that project.
11.16 On a more general level, many NGO representatives witness the six-week  long General Conference with some questions in their minds. With the greatest  respect, some of us are, frankly, amazed at the detail entered into by delegates.  Some of us are frankly appalled at the quantity of'paper. What we do know is  that for long periods of the year, the Secretariat is tied up in the decision-  making process of the governing bodies of the Organization. Those of us who are  in regular contact with the Secretariat know how hardworking it is. But we also  know that the present decision-making process leaves precious little time to  actually implement the decisions taken. The sad irony of the United States with-  drawal is that with less funds available, the Executive Board has had to sit for  longer hours to approve or disapprove the first or second priorities, and the  Secretariat - with reduced staff - has been more tied up than ever.
11.17 Surely it would not be impossible to devise by the beginning of the new  Medium-Term Plan a procedure which will enable the Member States to lay down  broad policy lines in half the time, with less than half the paper, and allow the  Secretariat to get on with the job of implementation, naturally with proper  reporting. I suggest that such an approach would not be in contradicton with the  concept of accountability, properly applied.
11.18 Mr President, perhaps it is easier for an NGO representative, neither a  delegate from a Member State nor a member of the Secretariat, to make such an  observation, and also to observe in a similar vein that the modern process of  multilateral diplomacy, for all its importance in promoting international CO-  operation, may have become too bound up with symbols. Perhaps that is why  Unesco - the organization mandated to concern itself with ideas - became the  first target of certain frustrations with the process of multilateral diplomacy.  The acts of withdrawal and note of withdrawal are acts whose significance is  primarily symbolic. I would submit  that to attack the principle of the independence of the international civil  service is a poor response to other attacks on the principle Öf the universality  of our international institutions. These are all examples of posturing in the  process of multilateral diplomacy.
11.19 Had as much effort been put into improving the capacity of our inter-  national institutions to deliver effective programmes as has been put into those  symbolic acts, many human beings, in many countries, could have looked to a  better future.
11.20 NGOs have declared themselves, through their conference, firmly in  support of the universality of Unesco. National NGOs are working to reverse  recent political decisions in their own countries, to provide their publics with  accurate information about Unesco, and in this they are supported by their inter-  national bodies. We NGOs want to see Unesco programmes of quality. It is not a  question of reflection or action. That is a false juxtaposition. It is really a  question of quality, of bringing together the best minds of this planet and of  all its cultures, and linking their concerted reflection to the implementation of  effective programmes out there where it really counts - among the people who make  up our societies.
11.21 As individuals, as societies, we have to learn to cope with a changing  world - to be the masters, the beneficiaries of change rather than its victims.  The mission of Unesco is to help individuals and societies to help each other in  that learning process. And one of the roles of the organizations of peoples for  whom I speak this morning is to ensure that the mission is fulfilled. Thank you
