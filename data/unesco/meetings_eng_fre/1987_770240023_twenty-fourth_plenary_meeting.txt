1. Le PRESIDENT :  Nous reprenons cet après-midi le débat spécial sur l'alphabétisation.  Je donne la parole au délégué de la Thaïlande.
2.1 Mr SABHASRI (Thailand) :  Mr President, Mr Director-General, distinguished delegates, ladies and  gentlemen, the Thai delegation considers it a privilege to be one of the speakers  on behalf of the Asia and Pacific region in this special debate c)n Unesco's draft  programme for International Literacy Year. As a country which has long been  involved in the struggle against illiterqcy as well as in providing continuing  and lifelong education for all, we welcome this important initiative for the  launching of an international year for this purpose.
2.2 Mr President, the Thai delegation regards literacy work as one of the  fundamental missions of Unesco, as stipulated in its Constitution. And, indeed,  Unesco has been faithfully implementing this mandate since 1946. Yet in spite of  all efforts, Unesco's figures for 1985 indicate that 889 million people, more  than a quarter of the world's population, remained illiterate. The magnitude of  the problem therefore requires a new innovative strategy to be adopted and  implemented worldwide, while at the same time regional co-operation could be  strengthened to allow problems to be solved in accordance with the different  socio-cultural situations.
2.3 But what can Unesco do to provide a new impetus and fresh approach which  can contribute even more to the strengthening of the struggle against illiteracy  the world over? This question has been deeply pondered in the various Unesco  documents distributed to this Conference, particularly documents 24 C/67, 24 C/78  and the in-depth study conducted by the Special Committee on Unesco's literacy  work. The Thai delegation is very happy to note a general line of agreement in  all these papers to the effect that in this programme of great importance and  magnitude Unesco cannot and must not act alone. It must, in accordance with its  mandate, however, serve as a prime mover and a launcher of the worldwide  campaign. It must motivate and mobilize the commitment and the co-operation of  the entire international co!nmunity, including particularly the Member States, the  United Nations agencies and the mass media.
2.4 Mr President, the Thai delegation agrees that illiteracy is a problem  deeply rooted in the socio-cultural situation and environment. We therefore  support the regional and interdisciplinary approach. Eradication of illiteracy  cannot be achieved by education alone but is also dependent upon the process of  socio-economic and cultural change and development. III this respect co-operation  with other agencies, particularly with WHO, FAO, UNEP, UNICEF, etc., is indeed  indispensable and TCDC co-operation is also necessary.
2.5 Furthermore, International Literacy Year should be linked with the World  Decade for Cultural Development, as it is evident that literacy is one of the  most effective means of preserving and disseminating endogenous culture and of  ensuring popular participation in cultural development.
2.6 Mr President, International Literacy Year should serve to provide a new  focus for a worldwide literacy campaign. This campaign should be conducted by  motivating networks of regional, national and local campaigns in all parts of the  world. The Thai delegation believes that Unesco should give priority to mobiliz-  ing world public opinion in support of the eradication of illiteracy, as well as  to generating popular support at the national and local levels. We believe that  the commitment and co-operation of public figures, from the prime minister down  to mass media personalities and village headmen, must be sought, while the  diverse illiterate targets must be identified and reached, so as to ensure that  they are properly and adequately motivated. In all these people involved in the  literacy campaign, a deep sense of awareness ánd urgency must be instilled and  promoted. If rock singers can be mobilized to raise funds for hunger -and fatal  diseases, then why not for the eradication of illiteracy, which is indeed a  social evil?
2.7 The target population, namely the illiterate themselves, must be made to
748    understand that their co-operation and determination are the key to the success  of this programme. From Thailand's experience, many of these functional illiter-  ates can be easily overlooked by the campaign authorities. National and local  campaigns must therefore reach out to serve illiterate groups, both male and  female, such as factory workers, prisoners and convicts, military conscripts, as  well as the disadvantaged groups in rural areas and urban slums.
2.8 Physical requirements for the literacy campaigns, on the other hand, are  also important factors to be considered. From the experience of Thailand, which,  I believe, might be relevant to most developing countries, the teaching and  learning materials for illiterates and neo-literates are- grossly inadequate, to  the extent that efforts and investment for universal primary education can result  in a partial waste in the long run, since school-leavers have no further access  to, and no need for, proper reading materials. The Thai delegation in this  context wish to share the experience of the Thai Government in setting up village  reading centres in all villages throughout the country in order to provide non-  formal and continuing education, as well as to prevent relapsing into illiteracy.  This programme has received the support of the general public, the mass media, as  well as the provincial and village authorities as regards the regular supply of  reading materials. However it is found that many of the materials donated are not  relevant and suitable to the needs of the neo-literates in the rural and urban  localities. Therefore in this respect the Thai delegation would like to suggest  that Unesco should work in close co-operation with other sister United Nations  agencies, such as WHO, FAO, UNICEF, in the preparation of reading materials which  are suitable to the needs and the daily life of the people.
2.9 Mr President, the launching of International Literacy Year is indeed one  of the most important challenges for our Organization. It is ambitious to try to  eradicate illiteracy from the world by the year 2000. Unesco Is statistics , how-  ever, show us that the rate of illiteracy has been reduced from 44 per cent in
1950 to 27 per cent in 1985 in spite of the rapid population growth, and this can  be considered proof that the illiteracy problem is indeed surmountable. Yet the  Thai delegation strongly feels that the real challenge for Unesco lies not only  in the quantitative.aspect of literacy, but more so in its quality. Literacy for
1990 must be defined differently from functional literacy in 1950. Scientific,  technological and industrial developments must also be taken into consideration  in the redefinition of functional literacy in the modern world and in the re-  launching of international literacy work.
2.10 Mr President, before I conclude allow me to briefly review the situation  in the Asia arid Pacific region. We are proud that the 1987 Noma Prize was awarded  to the National Literacy Campaign in Thailand, and its honourable mention to the  Women's Association of the Hunan Province of the People's Republic of China, and  that the Nadezhda Krupskaya Prize went to a schoolteacher in central Java in  Indonesia. However, the general literacy situation in Asia and the Pacific  remains serious, with three quarters of the world illiterate population,' or
666 million out of the world total of 889 million, living in this region. The  Asia and Pacific is in fact a region of contrasts - rich and diversified in its  cultural heritage, yet war-torn and poverty-stricken, and with illiteracy as one  of the social and economic setbacks impeding development in our region. Yet the  ministers of education and those responsible for economic planning at the  relevant regional conference have proposed many resolutions and recommendations,  which have prompted the Director-General to launch the APPEAL Project this year.  It is hoped that this Project will serve to strengthen regional and national  efforts, which should stimulate self-reliance within the region. We therefore  welcome Unesco's brave initiative in taking up this historic challenge of embark-  ing upon International Literacy Year. On behalf of the Thai delegation, I pledge  full support for and co-operation with Unesco and all the member countries in the  worldwide effort to overcome this predicament of mankind. Thank you.

4.1 Mr ORDONEZ (Philippines) :  Mr President, distinguished delegates, ladies and gentlemen, the  Philippine delegation joins its voice with those of the other distinguished  delegations who have already spoken so forcefully and so eloquently about the  problem of illiteracy and the need for intensive and systematic efforts towards  its eradication. For we, too, recognize the importance of linking the innovative  concepts of literacy programes to general education for all. We, too, recognize  that, even from an economic development point of view as the 1987 World Develop-  ment Report has documented, 'returns on investment have generally been higher in  education than in physical assets ... lack of education is a greater obstacle to  industrialization and development than lack of physical assets'. We too recognize
749    that as the family of nations launches the World Decade for Cultural Development,  the preservation and dissemination of the cultural heritage is hampered by  illiteracy.
4.2 While we share these sentiments with all others, the Philippine  delegation nevertheless expresses a note of urgency and some impatience at the  speed with which the eradication of illiteracy is being carried out. The head of  our delegation, the Philippine Secretary of Education, expressed this view at the  start of the session, when she said, 'This Organization is not short of words or  wisdom to formulate programmes to combat illiteracy ... We submit that there is  now sufficient knowledge about its nature and fundamental causes. What we need  further is the wherewithal for doing what must be done to succeed in bringing  about its eradication. We should now do more about getting rid of illiteracy than  making studies of its results'.
4.3 In line with the above sentiment, the Philippine Government has launched  an intensive drive to combat illiteracy. Permit us then, Mr President, to inform  the delegations of this honourable assembly of the three major steps we have  undertaken: (a) we have redefined our definition of illiteracy and further  identified the geographic, economic and sectoral groups of illiterates to design  better programmes for them; (b) we have built into our Education Department  bureaucracy the organizational anã administrative infrastructures that will carry  out and sustain our literacy and non-formal education programmes; (c) we have  initiated or co-operated with national and regional programmes that target the  eradication of illiteracy in specific areas within specific time frames.
4.4 A few words of explanation about each may be useful. Regarding the  definition of illiteracy, we felt that a more realistic picture of the true state  of illiteracy in our country was needed. Our national statistics had previously  proclaimed literacy rates in excess of 90 per cent for several years. While it is  true that the legacy of universal elementary education from the United States  colonizers at the beginning of the century had given the Philippines the highest  literacy rates in Asia in the past, nevertheless the pressures and priorities of  our previous government led to the gradual proportionate diminution of resources  for education and a consequent erosion of educational achievement and literacy. A  generalizeä and less rigorous definition of literacy therefore served to soften  the realization of these deleterious effects.
4.5 With the advent of the*new Aquino Government, brought in on the wings of  peace and people-power in 1986, and its renewed emphasis on the primacy of  education, a further study of the true state of literacy has been undertaken. New  instruments for nationwide testing have been designed, not just to discover the  ability to write one's name, but rather to measure functional literacy which  include basic numeracy and a working understanding of written directions. One to  three years of formal schooling were no longer automatic indicators of literacy,  since reversion to illiteracy among this cohort group, especially in rural areas,  was empirically noted. A new nationwide. survey under mere rigorous conditions  brought our official illiteracy figure to 84 per cent with significant variations  among geographical, economic and sectoral groups.
4.6 Secondly, the provision of an organizational infra-tructure for literacy  made the educational system into one of the largest benefit iaries of the advent  of the Aquino Administration. Recognizing the primacy of edc :ation in rebuilding  our nation, President Aquino immediately authorized a sign. ficant increase in  teachers' salaries and a structural reorganization of the Education Department  that would, among other things, address the large number of the nation's out-of-  school youth and adult illiterates through a non-formal education mechanism that  would carry the threefold thrust of literacy, livelihood skills and values  education. In addition, in response to an explicit provision in our recently  ratified 1986 Constitution to 'give education the highest budgetary priority',  additional resources were released to provide adequate staff to meet the re-  organization requirements for general and non-formal education. As a result, the  Education Department is now so organized that every regional office, every city  and provincial division, and every school district, now has a unit for non-formal  education to focus on this threefold thrust and thereby alleviate the load on  teachers in the formal school system by relieving then of this added and often  neglected responsibility.
4.7 Thirdly, as regards implementation of literacy programmes, the  Philippine delegation is proud to report its aative and fruitful participation in  Unesco's Asia-Pacific Programme of Education for All (APPEAL). Specific achieve-  ments and increases in literacy rates have been attained in Bicol, Mindanao, and  other less developed areas in the Philippines. We also note with gratitude the  sympathetic reception from Commission IV of the Philippine proposal for a  literacy .programme for abandoned urban street children and have, at its  suggestion, formally submitted this for support under the participation programme
24  for this biennium. We hope to report on favourable action, speedy implementation  and a production programme to this august assembly at the next opportunity.  Limitations of time prevent us from a more detailed sharing of other innovative  literacy progress.
4.8 Mr President, we cannot conclude this brief report on the progress made  towards the eradication of illiteracy in the Philippines at present without a  word on the prospects of these programmes in the future in general and in the  future with Unesco co-operation in particular.
4.9 The Philippine delegation pledges to continue to extend its fullest co-  operation to all international and regional Unesco programmes on literacy,  including those in connection with the forthcoming declaration of 1990 as Inter-  national Literacy Year. Since internal resources for literacy and education are  never limitless, the Philippine Government will unceasingly seek areas of inter-  national co-operation and assistance for present and future projects aimed at  eradicating the illiteracy remaining in our country. Unesco constitutes a major  international source, not only a financial source, but also a human expertise  source, and a policy research and information base source. The Philippine Govern-  ment recognizes this and, just as we in this brief time have shared with you our  lessons on redefining norms of literacy, organizationally restructuring to  liberate specific human resources to combat illiteracy, and launching innovative  literacy programmes for specific groups, so also we may share and learn from the  experience, expertise and resources of the other distinguished delegations  gathered here and of the mighty Unesco Organization itself. Mr President, I thank  you.
5. Le PRESIDENT :  de Sri Lanka.  Je remercie le délégué des Philippines et je donne la parole au délégué
6.1 Mr GURUGE (Sri Lanka):  Mr President, Mr Assistant Director-General for Education, Excellencies,  ladies and gentlemen, the distinguished delegates who have so far participated in  this special debate have analysed the problem of illiteracy from all salient  angles. Our attention has been drawn in particular to its magnitude and its links  with poverty, low productivity and underdevelopment with all their concomitants  in the form of hunger, malnutrition, disease, ignorance and social inequalities.  We have also been given glimpses of the valiant efforts made at the national  level to grapple with the ever-increasing mass of illiterates in absolute  numbers, even where some significant progress is visible in percentage terms. My  intention during this brief intervention is to concentrate on a few issues which  should receive the earnest attention of the international. community as it embarks  on an International Year of Literacy. I shall present them in the form of  questions.
6.2 The first question is whether we have an adequate understanding of the  phenomenon of illiteracy. What factors in society exacerbate illiteracy? What  motivates people to learn to read and write?
6.3 Literacy is a basic skill and, like all basic skills, it is directly  related to the needs created by the environment. With the innate proneness in  human beings to conserve energy, they do not attempt-to master skills unless such  skills are indispensable to their survival and well-being. Thus, people who could  thrive well enough without the skills of reading and writing in a given environ-  ment will not be motivated to acquire these skills - specially as their acquisi-  tion entails a substantial expenditure of time and energy, besides some invest-  ment in terms of money or foregone income. In such an environment, even those who  have acquired literacy skills through participation in a few years of primary  education would soon lapse into illiteracy.
6.4 I have considered how a few countries in Asia - particularly my own,  namely Sri Lanka - could, despite their low income level, achieve and maintain  very satisfactory rates of literacy. Did the schools and literacy programmes come  first, or did the recognition of the indispensability of literacy skills by the  people precede them? If the people can get on somehow without literacy skills,  they will no doubt take the path of least resistance.
6.5 Illiterates are in no way different from those of us who claim to be  literate. We are all literate only in our chosen environments. In others we can  be total illiterates. How many of us would be motivated to master Chinese,  Japanese, Gurmukhi, Burmese or Javanese scripts if we can get about without the  inevitable effort to be literate in them, even in places where written communi-  cation is only in these scripts.
6.6 What I observe from our experience in Sri Lanka is that reading skills
751    to begin with, and writing skills later on, had become essential for a respect-  able life at least a century ago. Without these skills one could not participate  in the everyday life of society with dignity and esteem; that was adequate  motivation for anyone to gain literacy. But this social factor evolved gradually  over a long period in the history of the country.
6.7 At some stage in our culture, the concept of an educated person evolved  from a very early phase of being 'a person who had heard much' (that is, from the  oral tradition of learning) to being 'a person who is well lettered'. Even in  remote villages of Sri Lanka, to be called a person who cannot read even an  obituary (Mala pate akurak vat nodat) is to be insulted as being someone ignorant  and stupid.
6.8 Perhaps, the reliance on the written word goes far back into our  history. It is not by accident that the Buddhist Canon came to be reduced to  writing in Sri Lanka 21 centuries ago, after it had existed in India for four  centuries in oral tradition.
6.9 Apart from over 4,000 rock inscriptions dating from the fourth  century BC with Sigiri graffiti in refined poetry (of the seventh to the tenth  century AD) as splendid specimens, Sri Lanka has a vast literature comprising  several thousand works, the earliest of which date from the ninth century.  Religious books in Sinhala, which were produced in the thirteenth and fourteenth  centuries, are read today as the Sinhala language has evolved only very  imperceptibly and the older idiom has not become obsolete.
6.10 People love to hear these classical works being read, if they do not  read them themselves. And most of them were written to be read aloud to attentive  audiences. In the wake of this literary development, a folk literature, mainly in  poetry, came into existence; and, in its most ubiquitous form a few decades ago,  the most popular reading material of the masses was the news-sheet in verse on  sensational events. In such an environment, each family had to have at least one  literate person as a window on the world, on the one hand, and as a mark of  social respectability on the other.
6.11 The direct outcome of reliance on reading has been written communi-  cation. Long before paper was even known in the country, messages were written on  metal plates and inexpensive palm-leaf. Written communication among the people  ranged from the native physicians' prescriptions for decoctions to posters and  notices. As far back as I remember, one could not, even in a remote village, get  about one's ordinary business without being able to read or write or without  having close enough access to one who could. When printing was introduced and, in  due course, became commonplace, the newspaper and the periodical provided further  impetus to literacy.
6.12 Literacy is a skill with an insatiable appetite. Once a critical mass in  a community has become literate the demand of those concerned for reading  material knows no bounds. On the one hand, they promote the production of reading  material and, on the other, the number of literates increases progressively until  the entire society moves towards a reading and writing society.
6.13 This is how the phenomenon of literacy evolved in Sri Lanka and the same  might be said of several Asian countries which are fast approaching universal  literacy. How much of this development is applicable to another situation?  Certainly, the long-ranging historical advantages could be unique to Sri Lanka  and a few similarly placed countries. But one fact becomes very clear. A fair  degree of reliance on written cormunication by a society as a whole is a sine qua - non before that society puts a premium on literacy skills. The question which  emerges from this observation is whether this reliance could be developed and if  so how?
6.14 It is my contention that national literacy campaigns must have at least  three well-defined phases. We hear, however, only of two. Both the current  literature and eloquent exponents of literacy emphasize these two phases as  literacy and post-literacy. The phase which I am highlighting is one that should  come right at the beginning. Let me call it pre-literacy.
6.15 The main thrust of the activities of this phase should be to make people  convinced that their survival and development are substantially curtailed by  illiteracy. What is done today in the form of awareness-raising involves no more  than groups of already convinced and converted people getting together and talk-'  ing to each other on the indispensability of literacy. It is undoubtedly futile  not only that the vast mass of books and learned papers written to urge people to  become literate are accessible simply to the very few highly educated educators,  socioaogists and the like but also that their elaborate arguments, amply  supported by sophisticated mathematical correlations between illiteracy and
752    poverty or infant mortality or productivity or family planning, are hardly  intelligible to any but a small charmed circle.
6.16 The people to be converted are the illiterates. The illiterates, of  course, have more important things to do like eking out an existence from an  unyielding socio-economic situation. They will learn only what is immediately and  directly relevant and useful to their existence.
6,17 Should we not evolve activities in the context of a pre-literacy phase  which create a demand for literacy among these people? Perhaps, all that we have  to do is to undertake most of what we call the post-literacy phase long before  the literacy phase begins. What I am thus emphasizing is simply this: do not make  the people literate and then go about producing reading materials to prevent them  from relapsing to illiteracy; instead, reverse the process, make people dependent  on literacy skills and let them acquire them on the basis of their own  motivation.
6.18 This suggestion might not appeax feasible on the face of it. But let us  consider a few innovative approaches. Consider a rural radio broadcasting pro-  gramme which requests listeners to write a postcard to obtain samples of seed  material, equipment, fertilizer or even information on more lucrative outlets for  farm produce. Visualize a popular television drama which progressively increases  the viewer's reliance on written messages on the screen. Now that the radio is  accessible tu almost the entire illiterate world and some of the most inaccess-  ible illiterate populations, especially women in newly affluent countries, spend  long hours before a television, these two media could figure prominently in the  pre-literacy phase.
6.19 I have a further thought on what needs to be done in the pre-literacy  phase. This concerns primarily the Asian countries which have traditionally  preserved scripts of their own. To this category would belong all countries other  than Malaysia, Indonesia, Viet Nam and the Philippines which have Romanized their  script.
6.20 In the Indian sub-continent, for example, written communication is in  not less than ten different alphabets. In several states of India such as  Gujarat, Punjab, Bengal, Andra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Kannada and Kerala, a student  who studies the regional language and the two official languages - Hindi and  English - has to grapple with three different alphabets. In Sri Lanka, too, the  three languages, Sinhala, Tamil and English, have each a different alphabet -  that is, three different codes or symbols to master before one reads or writes.
6.21 This problem of having to master several alphabets is further compli-  cated by the fact thât practically all these traditional scripts have complexi- ,  ties which have come. down from times when a pedantically oriented élite wrpte  these scripts in manuscript. Joint letters, contractions and calligraphic  refinements, on the one hand, ana a puritanical orthography of little use other  than to etymologists, on the other, have two major disadvantages: they make  typing and printing a very complex task: they frighten the adult learner while  making the acquisition of reading skills a nightmare to primary-school children.  It is for this reason that I urge reforms in scripts as a pre-condition of  universal literacy.
6.22 Here, again, Sri Lanka presents an interesting experience. The full  Sinhala alphabet comprises in the range of 56 letters. When the form which each  consonant acquires in conjunction with a vowel is considered, the number of  characters to be learnt increases to over 250. To this must be added joint  letters - which were the inventions of the ola-book writers - and the number  could rise by another 10 or 15. This is a daunting number. But over the  centuries, folk literature had begun to rely on just 32 letters with no joint  letters and no aspirates.
6.23 Simplication of alphabets is to my mind a matter of very great import-  ance. It will not be easy. In no other field have I encountered greater  opposition than in attempting to make writing simple and reading easy. Why should  we continue to tolerate this in-built obstacle in many languages to universal  literacy?
6.24 The next and the last question I wish to raise relates to the impli-  cations of the time-bound plans to eradicate illiteracy. With the dawn of the  twenty-first century on the horizon, policy-makers - both national and inter-  national - have postulated the year 2000 as the target date for universal  literacy. How serious are we with this date? If we really want to eradicate  illiteracy by this date, we have to concentrate the major part - if not the  whole - of our resources to this purpose. The drive has to be in no way different  to what WHO undertook to eradicate smallpox. But illiteracy as a scourge is more
753    difficult to control and eradicate. There is no vaccine; nor could we trace the  line of-contagious infection. With a target of a billion people to be made  literate in just over a decade, what are the resources which Unesco, for example,  is prepared to devote to it? Will the entire Education Sector consider the  possibility of utilizing all its human and material resources on a single-pointed  programme of eradicating illiteracy? I will not be surprised if the response to  this question is a resounding 'no'. Not only Unesco but every government could  respond to the question in an identical manner. This to me is the biggest  question: How do we put our money where our mouths are?
6.25 As I stated at the very outset, 1 only wished to raise some questions.  My own belief, borne out by my country's experience, is that people will become  literate when they are convinced that literacy skills are indispensable to their  very existence. If we can create this pre-condition in societies where illiteracy  is most rampant, khen only shall we succeed in eradicating it. This can be done  and that is an essential pre-literacy phase before we proceed to set up literacy  campaigns. Thank you.
