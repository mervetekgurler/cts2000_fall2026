1948. As regards Part I, which contains an  interesting appreciation, from the Director-  General’s personal viewpoint, of the record of the  past year and the future prospects of the Organ-  ization, no comment 1s offered.  The material presented in Parts II and Il}  the Report shows that, on the whole, the Secre-  tariat has followed closely the decisions reached  by the Second Session of the General Conference  at Mexico City, and the priorities laid down by  that Conference.  Part II of the Report is divided into six sections  corresponding to the six parts of the Programme  approved at Mexico City. Before commenting on  each of these six sections, I desire, on behalf of  the Board, to stress the progress that has been  of             made in bringing together, for the purpose of  Unesco’s programme, people from many different  countries and cultures. In particular, attention  should be drawn to the fact that workers in the  fields of education, science and culture from both  Eastern Europe and Western Europe have attended  Conferences and meetings organized by Unesco,  and that some of our international meetings have  been successfully held in what is nowadays called  Eastern Europe. Our activities have also brought  together, on many occasions, representatives of  the cultures of all five continents. Although our  membership is not yet universal, we are already  advancing appreciably the mutual knowledge and  understanding of peoples.  At the First Session of the General Conference  two years ago, it was decided that the Recon-  struction of Education, Science and Culture in  war-devastated areas must be one of the main  preoccupations of Unesco for some years. The  Conference recognized that the funds placed at  the disposal of Unesco by Member States would  not be sufficient to meet more than a small  fraction of the needs of war-devastated countries;  and it was therefore decided that Unesco's main  effort should be directed towards encouraging  voluntary contributions from the people, as well  as from the governments of Member States. Dur-  ing 1948 campaigns sponsored by Unesco have  yielded contributions amounting to a total of  about $45,000,000 in fellowships, supplies, ser-  vices and money, which have been placed at the  disposal of the war-devastated countries. In  addition certain funds will be available from the  Lord Mayor of London’s Fund, and from Canada.  Although this assistance is not large in compar-  ison with the needs of these countries, it has been  greatly appreciated by them; and the campaigns  that have yielded these contributions have helped  the contributors to understand better the edu-  cational, scientific and cultural needs of the  devastated countries. This activity of Unesco  must continue, and still greater effort must be  made to present clearly and accurately the needs  of war-devastated countries and to obtain adequate  reports of the use that has been made of these  donations, so that further contributions can be  stimulated.  Within the limits of Unescos budget, the  General Conference has provided some money for  emergency grants for relief and reconstruction.  During 1948 the total spent by Unesco in this way  will be $405,000. In addition, the Executive  Board, at its October meeting, approved the  transfer of a further $100,000, to be added to  the sum available for emergency grants. (Translation): 1 call upon  The DIRECTOR-GENERAL : Mr. President,  members of the Conference, may 1 first express  my sincere appreciation of the remarks of the  Chairman of the Board about myself and the  Secretariat, and of the comments which the Board  has made and their understanding both of our  successes and of our difficulties. May I also say  how grateful we are for the constructive criticisms  which they have offered.  Before coming to the matter in hand, may  [ take this opportunity—the first which has been  available to me—to say, in this hall, how pleased  and happy I am that this Conference is being  held here in Beirut. I had the privilege of visiting  Lebanon and, indeed, almost all the countries  of the Middle-Eastern region during the spring,  in preparation for this Confeernce, and I saw even  then the great interest and keenness with which  the Lebanese Government was tackling the pre-  paration of the Conference. I must say that, even  in spite of that, I really sometimes wondered  whether this great effort could be brought to  fruition in time for us to meet here in these most  excellent physical surroundings. When I was  here, this building, in which you are now listen-  ing to me, was a series of trenches in the ground;  there was nothing above ground. I do wish,  personally and, I am sure, on behalf of you all,  to pay a tribute to the immense effort which the  Lebanese Government has made in providing  these facilities. I would like also to say, as I have  already mentioned, that I then visited almost the  whole of the Middle East—and by that area, 1  mean not merely the seven States of the Arab  League but also the East from Egypt in the South  to Turkey in the North West, to Iran and Afgha-  nistan in the East—and everywhere was received  with great courtesy, and not great courtesy alone,  but what is much more important, great interest  in the work of Unesco. I am quite certain that  the convening of Unesco’s Conference in another  important region of the world—we have already  met in Europe, and in Latin America, and now  we are meeting in the Middle East—is a very good  omen and will bear important fruit.           I regret that my Report could not be sent to  Member States earlier but, owing to various  circumstances—partly my own very heavy schedule  of travelling, partly the delay in fixing the Confer-  ence date, and then the enormous pressure after  it was fixed on 15 September—it was delayed,  and could only be sent to Governments in October  in provisional form. It is now before you in  printed form, although 1 regret to say that the  index is still to come. The Report is up to date  until 15 September, though I have put in a few  points which have come up since then. What  | propose to do at this juncture is merely to bring  the Report up to date in a few particulars, when  it is specially necessary. 1 would like to have  the opportunity, at or after the end of the dis-  cussion of the Report, to make some sort of a  general reply and summing-up.  The only general point that I would like to  introduce here is to re-emphasize and reiterate  what I said in my Report—my strong feeling that  Unesco has really made good progress this year,  that it is really moving. It is moving out of the  stage of planning into the stage of action and  realization, though I would remind delegates that  in certain cases—such as, to take just one example,  copyright—the matter is so complicated and  concerns so many organizations and governments  that is has been impossible, physically impossible,  up to the present date to do more than planning.  In those cases where action has not been possible,  I hope you will agree that the planning has been  sound and should bear fruit, as the Chairman of  the Board said, in the comparatively near future.  I would also like to stress that this forward motion  of Unesco to which I have referred appears to me  to have actually accelerated in the two months  since the Report went to the printer. I would  mention now some of the things which have hap-  pened since then, so that you may be brought up  to date.  On the subject of tensions affecting inter-  national understanding, which may well become  one of the key points of Unesco’s programme,  there was a very good meeting at Royaumont,  near Paris, chiefly on techniques for changing  psychological attitudes. This meeting was inter-  esting in another respect, namely that it was  the first we have had in which we asked National  Commissions to designate the experts who took  part.  Secondly, as regards educational missions, we  have received enquiries from three countries con-  cerning the possibility of sending them such  missions. This device of sending a mission is a  very valuable one which has worked very well in  other fields, in agriculture and in technical  matters (planning of water supply and so forth),  but these will be the first, so far as I know, in the  field of education. We are hoping that three  missions will go out during the course of next  year to the Philippines, to Hungary and to  Afghanistan.  In the third place, I would like to mention  that on a project which, I may say, is very dear  to my heart personally—namely, the project for  a cultural and scientific history of mankind—we  had one of the most successful meetings that I  have ever attended in Unesco, of distinguished  experts who approved in general the ideas worked  out by the Secretariat and added a number of their  own, the results of which will be laid before you  in the Conference paper—I hope for your approval.  Then, in the fourth place, we had a very  important meeting—l think it is going to be  important in its fruits—early in November, of  eighteen directors of radio programmes from  important networks all over the world. It recom-  mended among other things, that Unesco should  sponsor a fifteen-minute weekly programme on  subjects of general Unesco interest, and all present  pledged themselves to do all they could to get  such programmes across on the air. Unesco is  now beginning to work on this.  We have another project on the social impli-  cations of science and its popularization. Here  too—and again I would like to report this spe-  cially because it seems to me a fruitful method  of working—there has been most valuable co-  operative work by our Department of Natural  Sciences and our Department of Mass Communi-  cation. They have jointly worked out plans, in  the first place, for organizing discussions on a  given subject throughout Member States; and  secondly, airing that subject, disseminating it  though the radio in particular and also through  other mass media.
