Report on the organization of the Secretariat  (continued) [document 5C/ADM/13].  [15] The DIRECTOR-GENERAL explained that  the organization of a Secretariat such as Unesco’s  was a very difficult and complex problem, in  which theory was far removed from practice.  Unlike certain other organizations, Unesco had  a very wide field of activity, which had involved  the creation of a number of departments to carry  out the Programme. Within these departments,  each of which was concerned with a particular  discipline, it was necessary to recruit qualified  specialists; while secondly, the execution of the  Programme required considerable administrative  services; for instance, a Unesco without a docu-  mentation or a statistical service would be incon-  ceivable.  It thus followed that the task of the Director-  General was essentially one of co-ordination, and  he was daily obliged to examine and discuss prob-  lems from fourteen widely differing services or  bureaux.     s s   =  de   LF   d L )   a n   =    [16] A number of systems had been considered  with a view to securing the best possible co-  ordination:
(1) The Director-General could have the as-  sistance of a Deputy Director-General and a num-  ber of Assistant Directors. In practice, this solu-  tion had proved unsatisfactory, as it was difficult  to place several disciplines under a single spe-  cialist;
(2) There could be two Deputy Directors-Gen-  eral, one to be responsible for assisting the Dir-  ector-General in the execution of the Programme  and the other to assume all administrative re-  sponsibilities. This solution had also been re-  jected, as it would tend to create two rival  authorities within the Secretariat, between which  the Director-General would constantly have to  arbitrate;
(3) What had been felt to be the only pos-  sible solution for Unesco had been the strengthen-  ing of the Director-General’s Office, with a Dir-  ector (Grade 19) and a Senior Executive Office  (Grade 17).  [17] The Director-General, who was at the  centre of all Unesco’s enterprises, needed exten-  sive direct assistance within his Office in the con-  sideration of the problems submitted to him,  which it was physically impossible for him to  deal with single-handed. Secondly, part of the  Director-General's duty was travelling, and he  would need to travel still more if the General  Conference met biennially only. In such cases it  was essential that the Deputy Director-General  should be able to replace him; and the Deputy  Director-General would himself be required to do  considerable travelling. All this was only pos-  sible, both for the Director-General and for the  Deputy Director-General, if they had the help of  a strong Office.  The Office of the Director-General was not, as  had been said, a screen between the Director-  General and the Departments; on the contrary it  was a bridge by which liaison was maintained.  It was, in fact, that Office which classified and  examined problems from the twofold standpoint  of balance in the Programme and administrative  practice. This resulted in the Director-General  receiving full documentation on the various prob-  lems, so that he could take decisions in the light  of full knowledge and a clear general picture.  Secondly, the Director of the Office of the Dir-  ector-General acted as secretary of the weekly  meeting of Heads of Departments and Services  and ensured the co-ordination and consolidation  of the reports presented by the departments.  Planned on these lines, the Office was still far  too small, and all its members were heavily bur-  dened.
640  [18] As regards the External Relations Service,  it too had to perform a wide variety of duties,  maintain contact with Member States, National  Commissions and non-governmental organizations,  and secure the necessary co-ordination with the  United Nations. Only with the help of this Ser-  vice could the National Commissions develop as  all delegates hoped they would. It was this Ser-  vice, too, which ensured that specialists sent on  missions to Member States were supplied with the  necessary documentation and which gave them  directives, so as to avoid a new crop of regrettable  experiences due to the inadequate preparation of  such missions.  The Director-General felt that the organization  of Unesco must not be judged by the standard of  a Ministry, or even of other Specialized Agencies  like the International Labour Organization, which  had many years of practical experience behind  them.
