5.5 Two years ago my observations were concerned primarily with the contribution that  Unesco should make towards achieving the aims of the United Nations Development Decade.  I expressed the view, at that time, that the conception and implications of the Development  Decade had not yet made their full impact upon the work of our Secretariat. The programme  submitted to us now gives many indications of the progress made in this respect since the  last General Conference.
5.6 I also urged in my speech two years ago that Unesco should take a fresh look at the  needs of developing countries in the field of science. Already at that time the attention of  the Economic and Social Council had been drawn by the Secretary-General of the United  Nations to the need for what he called a reorientation of the world's scientific programmes,  and preparations at that time were in full swing for the United Nations Conference on Science  and Technology, which was held in Geneva in February 1963. What rôle, I asked, did  Unesco aspire to play in ensuring that that conference would be followed up by constructive  action? Here again the past two ycars have seen a major effort to define Unesco’ s task  and to equip ourselves with the organization needed to carry out that task.
5.7 For the Australian delegation, and I am sure for many others, the present Conference  is above all an occasion for general atock-taking; for an examination of how Unesco is  facing up to the challenge of our time, not merely in expanding its programme but also in  adapting its activities and its methods to realities and the problems of economic and social  development and also to the evolving possibilities of co-operation within the whole system  of United Nations organizations. Now there is no doubt that Unesco has undergone and is  undergoing very important changes, and it {8 by no means casy for delegations to gain a  full and a clear picture of so diversified and extensive a programme as we are embarking  on. But at the same time it must be said that, thanks largely to the Director-General's  remarkable capacity for intellectual synthesis, we are presented at this Conference with a  more coherent and better organized programme than ever before. The Australian delega-  tion, like others, may have some criticisms to make on particular matters in the Pro-  gramme Commission and in other committees, but 1 wish to express our admiration for the  programme as a whole. It is indeed extremely impressive. It reflects a major effort to  adapt Unesco to the most urgent needs of our Member States. At the same time, in re-  sponse to the invitation we have had, I wish to offer some general observations on this  occasion.
5.8 The Director-General has drawn our attention to the growth of the Organization in  membership, budget, staff and premises. Indeed our members have grown from 76 in
1954 to 115 in 1964, an increase of over 50%, but this growth of over 50% in memberships  has not, of itself, increased our resources proportionately. Indeed, in so far as our ad-  ditional members were newly independent countries still at relatively early stages of their  economic development, their admission added more to the needs that Unesco is called upon  to meet than to the financial resources immediately at the disposal of the Organization. Of  course, all members pay their appropriate share of the budget, but the growth of the Or-  ganization, in size as well as in responsibilities has entailed an increasing contribution to  our overall resources from those having the greater capacity to pay. This in itself is an  indication of the greater degree of international solidarity that fortunately prevails in the  world today.
5.9 Unesco's capacity to meet the needs of its economically less developed members has  been greatly strengthened by the large development programmes of the United Nations, pro-  grammes financed almost entirely by the voluntary contributions of governments. I have  in mind, of course, the Expanded Programme of Technical Assistance and the Special Fund  of the United Nations, which, as the Director-General has told us, will, together with the  World Bank and IDA, contribute about half of the funds to be expended by Unesco in the next  two years. This situation, in which over the next two years we shall be spending about
100 million’ dollars - half of which is coming from these great United Nations development  programmes - raises several new problems for Unesco itself.
5.10 Firat, despite our autonomous constitution, half of our activities now have to he  undertaken within the framework of co-operation constituted by the United Nations and the  other Specialized Agencies, and must conform to common standards of administration and  performance. Secondly, like all the Specialized Agencies, Unesco finds itself increasingly  involved in serious problems of co-ordination both in the formulation of policy and in field  operations. Thirdly, Unesco is responsible, as are other participating agencies, for the  publie impact of these United Nations development programmes, not only in the countries  receiving assistance but also in the countries whose governments must furnish the bulk of  the voluntary contributions on which these programmes depend. Fourthly, there is the  question to which the Director-General has referred, namely, as to how far Unesco's  extra-budgetary programmes can be developed independently of the Regular programme; to  what extent the growth of the extra-budgetary operations should be shaped by the autonomous  Regular programme of the Organization, or on the other hand justify a similar growth in  the Regular budget.
5.11 In my opinion the importance of this last question of the relation between the Regular  and extra-budgetary programmes has been somewhat exaggerated. There is no doubt that  the extra-budgetary resources have opened up a much wider field for service by Unesco  than our Regular programmes and budgets could ever cover. Moreover, these extra-  budgetary resources have assisted Unesco to establish priorities and to concentrate our  Regular programme with much greater success thun we had attained previously. Now the  Regular programme no doubt has had primacy in the sense that the funds entrusted to Unesco  from extra-budgetary resources express an explicit recognition - by the United Nations, by  the World Bank and by the world as a whole - of the practical competence which the Organ-  ization had established in activities financed by the Regular programme. But the competence  that Unesco is acquiring in new fields, in which Its activities are financed mainly by extra-  budgetary funds, is certainly no less a factor in the present confidence which our Organiza-  tion commands among our Member States. And I am sure the truth is that the Regular pro-  gramme and the extra-budgetary programme inevitably react upon each other and influence  each other. I am inclined to think that the future development of Unesco will depend less on  any formal attribution of primacy to the Regular programme than on the solution of the other  problems that I have mentioned and on the maintenance of the present willingness of the  developed countries to help those that are less developed.
5.12 Now as regards the overall programme presented to us, we agree that the activities  proposed in the two priority areas - education and science - are along the right lines. We  endorse the emphasis given to assistance with educational planning and teacher training, and  we support the Director-General' s experimental approach to the problems of adult illiteracy.  Indeed, this experimental approach must underlie all our work in education because, as I  reminded the Conference two years ago, there are very serious, unsolved problems to be  faced, not merely in providing the resources for an expansion of education in the developing  countries but in adapting the methods of education to specifically local requirements.
5.13 The application of science and technology to development is of course a vast subject  which in several directions transcends the limits of Unesco! s competence. Unesco does not  enjoy a monopoly of science nor of its application. Several other Specialized Agencies,  such as the Food and Agriculture Organization, the World Health Organization, the World  Meteorological Organization, the International Labour Organisation, and the International  Atomic Energy Agency, all have important contributions to make to the application of scie ce  to development in their respective fields. And the co-ordination of this whole effort is a  matter for the United Nations and the machinery for inter-agency co-operation. Unesco's  rôle, however, is of fundamental importance, firstly because the level of scientific and  technical development of a country is closely related to its level of educational development,  and secondly because the organization of science, at the national level, requires a broad  interdisciplinary approach and the synthesis of more specialized interests. We strongly  support the emphasis given by the Director-General to the autonomous development of  science in developing countries, as well as to the continued improvement of arrangements  to facilitate international co-operation among scientists of developed and developing countries.

Now I am not suggesting that this attitude of evaluation is missing from the work  of our Secretariat, but I wonder whether, in our thinking forward into the coming years, we  can given special attention to the mechanisms and procedures by which this process of self-  criticism and of evaluation of the effectiveness of particular activities, including our activi-  ties in the field, can be carried through and the results brought to the attention of future  Conferences.

17.21 Finally, Mr. President, may I again stress the need of cultivating and improving the  institutional means of developing a continuing dialogue and mutual assistance between the  developing and the industrially already advanced countries. In this regard, I believe, all  countries regardless of their respective state of development have much to offer on the  basis of equality and mutual respect. International co-operation in the fields of education,  science and culture can only succeed if we recognize that each country is unique in its heri-  tage and that each can contribute towards the ideals of Unesco which we all share. The new  emphasis on science and technology, the literacy campaign and other Unesco activities as  related to development as being both practical and moral, must be developed in the light of  such a dialogue. It remains to us to appraise the situation and to ask ourselves whether the  present organization and the institutional means of Unesco do allow a continuing construc-  tive dialogue between all nations and in the interest of all.
