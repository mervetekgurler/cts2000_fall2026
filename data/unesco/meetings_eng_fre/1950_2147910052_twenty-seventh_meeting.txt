TWENTY-SEVENTH MEETING  Tuesday, 13 June 1950, at 4 p.m.  Chairman: Mr. Roger Seypoux (France)  Draft resolution submitted by Mexico and  Venezuela: Technical and financial assis-  tance for the restoration of the archaeological  and historic monuments of Cuzco (continued)  [document 5C/114].  [1] Mr. THOMAS (Director, Department of  Cultural Activities) reminded the Commission that  it had adopted draft resolution 4.42. The calas-  trophe which had occurred at Cuzco (Peru) was a  sufficient cause for sending a technical mission  under the terms of that item, subject to approval of  the resolution by the General Conference.  [2] Mr. GOROSTIZA (Mexico) expressed this  information and pointed out that paragraphs (a)  and (b) in the draft resolution in document 5C/114  involved no budgetary implications.  [3] The DIRECTOR-GENERAL announced that,  im 1951, Unesco would be in a position to  organize a mission to assist with the restoration of  archaeological sites or monuments as part of the  Technical Assistance programme. The Mexican  and Venezuelan delegations had proposed that the  mission should be sent to Cuzco. The President of  the Economic and Social Council of the United  Nations had sent a telegram to the Director-  General, asking Unesco to make some practical  gesture of international solidarity to Peru. If the  Commission thought fit to select Peru to receive  the first mission, and if item 4.42 were kept In  the 1951 Programme, there would be no obstacle  to carrying out the proposal of the Mexican and  Venezuelan delegations. It would, of course, be  necessary for the Peruvian Government to send  Unesco an official request. In that connexion, the  Director-General mentioned that part of the cost  of the mission’s work would have to be borne by  the country to which it was sent. As several del-  egations had indicated that their governments were  anxious that such a mission should be sent to them  under the provisions of item 4.42, the decision  should come from the Programme and Budget  Commission rather than from the Executive Board  and the Director-General.  [4] It was decided that, provided the Peruvian  Government submitted a request, the mission men-  tioned in item 4.42 should be sent to the city of  Cuzco in 1951, and the Director-General should be  authorized to notify the President of the Economic  and Social Council of that decision. [5] Dr. AWAD (Egypt) emphasized that, in sub-  mitting the document, no criticism was intended  of the action of Powers administering non-self-  governing territories, many of which had honour-  able records, as could be seen by the number of  such territories which had emerged from that con-  dition to become independent nations, members in  their own right of international organizations. It  was intended by the Egyptian amendment to direct  particular attention to a side of the well-being  and development of non-self-governing territories  which had not been stressed in the resolution  submitted by the Executive Board, and thus to  render the enquiry more complete.  Dr. Awad drew attention to the fact that the  fostering of minority languages might even hamper  development; the language to be encouraged  should therefore have the qualities of a national  language. The amendment necessarily had its  political side, like many other parts of the Unesco  programme; but, as the amendment did not imply  interference in the internal affairs of States, which  was forbidden by the Constitution, he had no  scruples in submitting it to the Commission.  [6] Mr. ABRAHAM (France) emphasized the  merits of Dr. Awad’s proposal. There was nothing  in the word “political” or the concept it covered  to cause alarm, and no one could doubt that the  Egyptian draft resolution was concerned with intel-  lectual “politics”. The text which had been  adopted before was the result of a request from the  United Nations that Unesco should study the ques-  tion of indigenous languages, through its Member  States. The Egyptian delegation’s proposal was  not entirely in line with that scheme, particularly  with regard to political administration and insti-  tution. The object of the proposal in question was
442                probably the same as that of the United Nations in  that it was designed to preserve the vitality of  indigenous languages, so that they might provide  access to a higher civilization.  The term “indigenous languages” was applied to  the local languages, but those were not the only  ones used in the non-self-governing territories.  The language of a great culture or civilization,  although not indigenous, should not be ignored by  Unesco. Mr. Abraham therefore submitted an  amendment to the Egyptian proposal, reading:  Instructs the Director-General to undertake an over-all  study of the question of the indigenous or national  languages of the native peoples.  The French amendment was seconded by the del-  egate of BELGIUM.  [7] Dr. JAMALI (Iraq) fully endorsed Dr. Awad’s  statement. In the United Nations, some concern  had been felt about the unduly slow development  of certain non-self-governing territories. One of  the means of hastening their development was  precisely the encouragement of a national lan-  guage. In the past, either national languages  had been ignored, or local languages had been  so encouraged as to divide, rather than unite,  the territory. The part to be played by the United  Nations and Unesco in leading the territories in  question to independence and self-government  implied adequate encouragement of national lan-  guages, but not of all indigenous languages. [17] Professor PHOTIADES (Greece) questioned  the value of the Czechoslovak proposal [document  DC/17], in view of the educational methods  employed, and the treatment of children in  general, in Czechoslovakia itself.  [18] Professor CARNEIRO (Brazil) paid tribute  to the ideas underlying the proposals under  consideration, but expressed doubts about the  procedure proposed. By virtue of its origin, its  Constitution, and its Programme, Unesco stood  consistently as an advocate of peace. He was afraid  that, if any one of the proposals submitted were  carried out, it might lead to a sort of analysis of  such and such a war or such and such a method  of warfare. The important thing was to strike at  the very roots of war and violence. Unesco’s doc-  trine was that of non-violence, of refusing to settle  disputes by warlike methods. It was Unesco 's duty  to eradicate the war spirit. Any study of the  means of conducting warfare might compromise  the success of that fundamental task. Unesco's  work for peace was a slow process, to be carried  out through the education programme, the study  of social tensions, and mass communication. It  represented the gradual day-to-day reform of the  human spirit.  Professor Carneiro agreed with the views  expressed by the Chairman of the Executive Board  when he had said that Unesco's work for peace  should be carried on as part of the process of  implementing the programme. No doubt the  National Commissions should be reminded that  they also had their part to play in that field and  that they should consult together to make their  work as effective as possible. The Organization  itself had to count on the moral and intellectual  impact of its programme and its presence in the  world.  [19] Dr. JAMALI (Iraq) welcomed Sir John  Maud’s statement and fully agreed with the  opinion he had expressed. If, after two world wars  in one generation, there was any intelligence left  to the world, all were alive to the danger of war.  The San Francisco Conference, in preparing the  Charter of the United Nations, has laid down a  basis for lasting peace. Action should follow to  prepare the ways and means by which the nations  could live together in peace, by removing the  causes of war.  [20] Professor DURING (Sweden) agreed with  Dr. Jamali and Professor Carneiro. Since 1814,  Sweden had not been engaged in any war and had  thus laid herself open to the charge of being too  peace-minded. It was her belief that, if nations  concentrated on the social, cultural and economic  development of their own lands and opened their  frontiers to all forms of contact with their neigh-  bours in order to get to know other countries,  knowledge would give confidence and trust where  lack of knowledge could only breed fear and mis-  trust. In its programme for promoting the free  flow of information, in its work in Germany and  Japan, through the Social Sciences programme and

By turning a blind eye to  the facts of the hour, out of a desire to cling to its  existing programme, the Organization was losing  sight of its responsibilities, a fault for which it  would have to answer at the bar of history. No  doubt the answer the delegations had given was  sincere, but it was the first time that there has  been such unanimous agreement on the efficacy of  the programme. The Director-General left to the  imagination of the delegates the feelings of the  peoples of the fifty-nine Member States at the  overwhelming difference between the budget of  $8,250,000 allotted to the programme for the  defence of peace, and the amounts to be spent on  armaments in those fifty-nine States themselves.  If it was thought that education, science and  culture were the best means of defending the cause  of peace in a world in which pacifism itself had  become suspect, two possible courses were open.  Either Unesco could be converted into a modest  office for intellectual co-operation, in receipt of the  sums necessary for the execution of its programme,  or, alternatively, there should be a determined  ‘return to the spirit of the Constitution and  vigorous action should be taken, accompanied by  the provision of appropriate resources.  Contrary to the hopes that the Director-General  had indulged, it seemed that Unesco was to be  held at the same point for several years to come,  and it was useless to contemplate improving the  programme for the future if the replies given by  the Member States were not to be more en-  couraging. Either Unesco’s Programme was good  and the manner of implementing it bad, or the  Programme was good and the manner of imple-  menting it passably satisfactory, but Member States  were in no position to make a practical contribu-  tion to the Organization's work. In either case,  the Director-General asked the General Conference  to find someone to succeed him for, believing as  he did in the principles of Unesco, he could no  longer place any confidence in the inadequate  activities of Member States.
