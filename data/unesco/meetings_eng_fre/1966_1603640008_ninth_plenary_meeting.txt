6,1 Mr, DIEPENHORST (Netherlands):  Mr. President, ladies and gentlemen, it is a privilege for me to address this fourteenth  session of the Gonoral Conference, which also marka Unesco's ‘l'wentieth Anniversary, and  to say a few words on bohalf of the Dutch Government abent attitudes In the Netherlands to-  wards the problems that preoccupy us all today, I feel the more privileged, in that this city  of Paris and this country of France have a spocial allure for Dutchmen, We remember how,  in an earlier century, this metropolis offered a refuge to Grotius, our greatest jurist, who  did so much towards bringing nations closer together, We are also aware of our apiritual  debt to France, Of the host of names that immediately come to mind, I shall mention only  ono, but one which has deoper significance for Unesco than many others, that of Blaise Pas-  cal, It is to him that we owe the axiom: "T.'homme n'est qu'unroseau, le plus faible de la  nature; mais c'est un roseau pensant' - "Man is only a reed, the weakest thing In nature;  but he is a thinking reed".
6.2 Personally, I feel slightly apprehensive of soliciting your attention, be it only for a  few minutes. As a politician, I know that politics is branded by its critics as the secund-  rate occupation of second-rate people; and as a professor, I have had the experience of ad-  dressing a happily dozing audience on more than one occasion! Indeed, it is only academic  introspection that has given me the assurance that, and I quote, "I am neither a horse nor  an ass", and which emboldens me not to break off my speech at this point.
6.3 The splendid introduction to the Unesco Draft Programme and Budget for 1967-1968  makes it clear to those who did not know already, how much progress has been made in the  two years that have elapsed since the thirteenth session of the General Conference, A  single shadow darkens the review of this period for my delegation: the fact that Professor  Sissakian, the 1964 President, is no longer with us, Nevertheless, the work has gone on.  The Director-General sets forth the projects to be realized with consummate strategy; then,  with the authority of an eminent physician, he feels Unesco's pulse and pronounces the Or-  ganization to be in excellent health, even though its heart may beat a little more faintly now  and then through lack of financial oxygen.
6,4 Mr, Maheu's observations, though not dissociated from the past, are nowhere engulfed  fn what has been. Nor does ho indulge in flights of fancy about what is to be. He keeps both  feet firmly planted in the reality of today. Not unnaturally, certain misgivings arise as he  discusses the opportunities that must be seized, What will happen, one may wonder, if,  not five or ten, but a hundred experts meet on the subject of educational planning? Can and  will a hundred first fiddles ever play in harmony? Pessimists will no doubt feel that one  Tower of Babel was enough,
6.5 A glance at the budget figures is sufficient to show how much importance is attached  to education, The fight against illiteracy is one of Unesco's major campaigns. Psycholo-  gists may advance the most complicated theories on what life is really all about; a more  contemplative mind may recall that Goethe, on his eightieth birthday, announced that he had  not yet learnt to read properly, Be that as it may, our concern is that people should be able  to exercise the simple art of using symbols as the vehicles of specific intellectual concepts.  But that 18 no reason for jettisoning science, Science employed in the service of mankind  can facilitate learning. A blend of theory and practice can, with the help of new aids and  methods, overcome obstacles that were once insurmountable. Thore is a great deal to be  achieved through what is known as graphic communication, In the Netherlands, we are busy  preparing for the establishment of an institute of this kind in the framework of our develop-  ment aid programme, This is not being done in the belief that the quantity of printed paper  produced per head of the population is the ideal parameter of a nation's culture - though it  certainly indicates something! The intention is merely to offer the underprivilegedincreas -  ed opportunities of fortifying and enriching their lives.
6.6 In his introduction, the Director-General alludes to the ever-widening gulf between the  developed and non-developed among the countries of the world. The impression one gains  is that what is acknowledged on the personal plane, namely - and I quote freely - "that the

6,7 Yet elementary education, rudimentary knowledge, is essential; and it requires exten-  sion, if only for the sake of our own survival and that of othera, Countries with advanced  civilizations sometimes regard a university education as the be all and end all, On the other  hand, Vanderbilt is supposed once to have sald: "You tell Lord Palmeraton from me that it  I had learnt education, I wouldn't have had time to learn anything else"; an instructive ut-  terance, 1 feel,
6.8 But the situation is in fact graver than it would appear to be from this statement. And
1 can't help thinking that it 1s Injudicious to wrangle - as the experts do - about proportions,  be it of illiteracy or hunger, The one sure thing is that dire poverty is still met with; and  that, while we have it in our power to alleviate it within the foreseeable future. Let me put  things a little more graphically: with technology progressing at a faster pace than the mi-  gery in the world ts spreading, the one must be able to catch up, overtake, and obliterate  the other, Somewhere it must be possible for our paths to cross, and rich and poor tofind  each other at last,
6.9 However, there are dangers attached to an otherwise well-meaning attempt to bring  about an improvement in the situation, In the first place, there is the haphazard, and thus  wasteful, deployment of forces. It is essential to know what one is about. It 1s unwise not  to have studied the nature and extent of the natural resources available to the receiving coun-  try. In order io forestall any reproof that this is arid theory, I would point out that the  Dutch Government is negotiating with Unesco about setting up an Institute for IntegratedSur-  veys in Delft, the town where Grotius was born and the seat of the I.T.C., the former In-  ternational Institute for Aerial Survey and Earth Sciences,
6.10 It would even be fatal to ignore the fact that technological progress does not consist  solely in the transmission of a modicum of skill and knowledge, in the mere transfer of  "know-how", but that is something quite different, and much more thanthat. Techniques  comprising specific manual skills are certainly part of it. Indeed, the idea of using mon-  keys for simple jobs within the space of a few decades is already being toyed with in cer-  tain quarters, But technology that purports to raise the overall standard of a nation must  needs be related to both the material and the spiritual aspects of life. ‘The literacy cam-  paign, and the imparting of elementary though farther-reaching vocational knowledge, must  not be effected on a plane possessed solely of intellectual dimensions,
6.11 It is a hard and oft-repeated truth that nowadays nations no longer really admire one  another and that, though not averse to meeting at sporting events - in the hope, of course,  of winning - they are less well-disposed to spiritual contacts more likely to promote their  overall edification. Nations that provide aid in the form of instruction and technical "know-  how" will nevertheless have to realize that, if their efforts are to be fruitful, they will have  to meet recipient countries on all fronts, partly for their own self -enrichment. Education  and technology are but parts of culture; it also includes ethical features,
6.12 In the everyday process of transmitting knowledge, and in the confrontation of nation  with nation, radio and, above all, television, play a major part these days. People require  to know what others are like, The spoken word and the broadcast image reveal more than  the written word. It would be a sad state of affairs if our most modern means of mass com-  munication were to present no more than a candy-floss picture of the world, instead of a  true picture, one that would provide a fillip to worth-while initiatives, a spur to fresh acti-  vity, and not least cultural activity. Again for fear of being dismissed as the plous theorist,  I would straightaway add that we in the Netherlands are actively working towards the estab-  lishment of a training centre for radio and television personnel from developing countries.  And our aim in so doing is certainly not to turn the television tube into an idiot box or a re-  pository for kitchen sink drama.

6,13 If I have rightly understood what the Director-General says about his budget and his  provisional budget ceiling, then Goethe is once again proved right in saying "Am Golde hängt  hier alles’ - everything depends on gold, Fortunately, the figures submitted show that  Unesco has so far aucceaded in avoiding what Lord Bryce once oalled "the habit of mistak-  ing words for facts and aspirations for achievements', Yet only the future will show whe-  ther we leave to posterity "bank records’ rather than "teaching", A complaint oftenheard  is that, in personal relationships, idealism, and the desire to holp that springs from idea-  lism, are being ousted by the "I'm all right Jack" attitude, It is to bo hoped that a different  frame of mind will persist within Unesco,
6.14 In accordance with the Preamble to the Constitution of November 1846, signed by my  Government in London, one powerful international organization was to advance the object-  ives of international peace and of the common welfare of mankind, through the educational  and scientific and cultural relations of the peoples. The wording chosen for the Constitu-  tion may be somewhat high-flown; none the less, the basic idea is clear, Knowledge and  conscience, science and ethics, are bound up with each other. To attain a higher purpose,  to eliminate wars, we grasp at the means within our reach, It has rightly been observed  that, though we travel to the ends of the earth in search of peace, it is to be found just  around the corner,
6.15 To avoid such vain peregrinations, I would add one last concrete point to the agenda,  one that reminds us that charity begins at home, Let countries with large numbers of fo-  reigners working within their frontiers, ask themselves what they can do to promote their  guests’ well-being, This might constitute a promising start to closer and stauncher rela- ’  tions among the peoples of the world.
6.16 In conclusion, I put it to you: what is our ultimate goal? Most of us are conscious of  the precariousness of our existence, and live in the ever-present shadow of impending des-  truction, For now, for the first time in history, we have the power virtually to annihilate  the world, and mankind with it. Many of our fellow-men are furthermore racked with hun-  ger. So let us heed the words of Dag Hammarskjbld, a man still mourned in this company,  on the subject of tha purpose in which we are united: "But common to us all", he observed,  "and above all other convictions, stands the truth once expressed by a Swedish poet, when  he sald that the greatest prayer of man does not ask for victory but for peace", I should  like to express the hope that the same desire for peace will inspire us all, so as to enable  Unesco in the coming years to fulfil ite lofty vocation to preserve life, In the world of to-  morrow may there be less darkness and more light.
