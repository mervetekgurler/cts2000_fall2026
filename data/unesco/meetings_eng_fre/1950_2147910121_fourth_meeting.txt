FOURTH MEETING  Monday, 5 June 1950, at 10.30 a.m.  Chairman:  [1] The CHAIRMAN mentioned that the Com-  mittee had already considered Items 1 to 7 of its  Agenda. With regard to Item 7 [Draft Amend-  ment to Rules 52 and 55 of the Rules of Procedure  (Working Languages), presented by the Mexican  delegation] the Committee s conclusions had been  embodied in a preliminary report communicated  to the General Committee of the Conference for  immediate transmission to the Programme and  Budget Commission.  The present extraordinary meeting had been  convened, at the request of the General Committee  of the Conference, for the consideration of the  following:
(1) Draft Rules of Procedure concerning  recommendations to Member States and inter-  national conventions [document 5C/OXR/16];
(2) Draft resolution on biennial sessions of the  General Conference, presented by the United States  of America [documents 5C/16 and 5C/16 Annex];
(3) Draft resolution concerning the status of  associate members of Unesco, presented by the  United Kingdom delegation [document 5C/20].  Draft Rules of Procedure concerning recom-  mendations to Member States and inter-  national conventions [document 5C/OXR/16].  [2] The CHAIRMAN stated that the Official and  External Relations Commission, which was to  consider the first of those items, was awaiting the  report of the Procedure Committee. He therefore  suggested adopting the same procedure as for  Item 7 and preparing a preliminary report. He  had just been informed that the final report, for  which the Official and External Relations Com-  mission had first been made responsible, would  be drafted by the Programme and Budget  Commission. As the first part of document
5C/OXR/16 explained the background of the  question, the Chairman suggested that the dis-  cussion should turn upon the practical sugges-  tions embodied in the second part of the docu-  ment (Draft Rules of Procedure concerning re-  commendations to Member States and inter-  national conventions, covered by the terms of  Article IV, paragraph 4, of the Constitution).
666  Professor Jako NierLsen (Denmark)  [3] Mr. SABA (Legal Adviser) submitted docu-  ment 5C/OXR/16. Speaking of the need for  taking sufficient time over the drafting of texts  involving international obligations and leading,  finally, to the adoption of legislative or regulatory  measures by Member States, he referred to  Unesco’s sole experience in the matter; the  agreement on audio-visual material had been  drafted too hurriedly and it had been necessary  to prepare a protocol, containing certain amend-  ments, for signature. The draft submitted  provided for the preliminary consideration of any  matter it was proposed to regulate and for con-  sultation with Member States, who would be  requested to send in their observations and com-  ments, on the basis of which the Director-  General would prepare the final draft for sub-  mission to the Conference. Provision was made  in document 5C/OXR/16 for the preparation of  conventions either by a standing committee or  by the Secretariat. The Official and External  Relations Commission had advocated adopting the  latter method for the time being.  [4] Dr. WALKER (Australia) stated that his  delegation attached great importance to the mat-  ter. Before the draft rules of procedure were  considered in detail, he thought he might use-  fully make a few general observations.  Although action had been very slow hitherto,  it was to be hoped that it would speed up now  that the Secretariat had a small group of legal  experts competent in the matter. From Unesco's  earliest days, action through international con-  ventions had been contemplated as one of the  most important means of regulating educational  problems on an international basis. The delay  was due to the fact that, during its first years,  Unesco had been fully occupied with the pre-  paration of its general programme. Whilst  Australia had not been one of the first States to  submit comments to the Secretariat, it none the  less attached the highest importance to the prob-  lem, and the Australian delegation intended to  present its observations thereon to the Confer-  ence.  [6] Under its Constitution, Unesco was pro-  hibited from interference in the domestic affairs                        ——  of Member States, yet it could promote the cause  of education by solemn recommendations and  conventions. Dr. Walker mentioned that the  agreement on audio-visual material was to be  replaced by a convention on the importation of  educational, scientific and cultural matrial. He  thought it essential ot adopt some definite policy  regarding the choice of subjects which might be  covered by future conventions. The distinction  between “recommendations” and “conventions”  was, in his opinion, of the utmost importance;  the Conference could do no more than “recom-  mend” that Member States should adopt any  particular convention which would not enter into  force until it had been ratified by Member States.  A recommendation had moral force only and no  legal validity; if too numerous, recommenda-  tions would lose their value. There were several  types, from the general recommendation (re-  cognizing the value of such matters as teaching  about the United Nations) to the more technical  recommendation advocating specific, effective as-  sistance to countries willing to co-operate. Every  question raised should be considered with a view  to determining whether it would be best re-  culated by a convention binding States or by  less rigorous recommendations. When some  activity undertaken by one State had repercussions  in other States, the obvious course was to adopt  a Convention, an excellent example of which was  afforded by the Convention concerning the Im-  portation of Educational, Scientific and Cultural  Material.  [6] Dr. Walker hoped that a more important  rôle would be assigned to the Executive Board  which should be made responsible for initiating  preliminary studies and deciding whether each  particular case should be dealt with by means of  a convention or a recommendation. For the time  being, the Official and External Relations Com-  mission was not favourable to the establishment  of a standing legal committee. He personally did  not feel there was any urgent need for laying  down procedure for the presentation of reports  by the Director-General and he suggested ap-  pointing a small sub-committee, of not more than  five to seven members, to revise the draft regula-  tions as soon as the general discussion was com-  pleted.  [7] Mr. COWELL (United Kingdom) shared  many of Dr. Walker's views. However, the  United Kingdom delegation would prefer to have  conventions framed not on specially selected  problems, but on those arising in the normal  course of the Organization’s work. Unesco's  fundamental principle was to encourage Member  States to make voluntary efforts for the advance-  ment of education, science and culture, and  except in cases where joint action was essential  for success, Mr. Cowell hoped that the progress  of Unesco’'s work would depend on general  agreement among its loyal supporters rather than  on legislative measures.  With regard to the practical proposals set  forth in document 5C/OXR/16, the United King-  dom delegation considered them a decided 1m-  provement on the previous draft, but was never-  theless critical of certain minor points; in par-  ticular, it could not agree to the establishment of  a standing legal committee. Whilst, therefore,  approving in general the contents of the draft,  Mr. Cowell supported the proposal that it should  be referred to a drafting sub-committee.  [8] The CHAIRMAN asked the members of the  Committee to submit, in writing, any proposals  they wished to make to the Drafting Sub-Com-  mittee before the evening of Tuesday, 6 June 1950.  [9] In reply to a question from Dr. ZEISSL  (Austria) as to whether originators of written  proposals could defend them in the sub-com-  mittee even if they were not members of the  latter, the CHAIRMAN said that that was for the  Sub-Committee to decide, though he felt sure it  would give due consideration to such a request.  [10] Miss PEDERSEN (Denmark) made a few  general comments. Unesco was tackling a tre-  mendous problem which was summarized in a  very condensed working paper, whereas volumes  would be needed to deal with every aspect of  the question. The Procedure Committee could  do no more than suggest the course to be adopted.  Under other provisions of its Constitution, besides  Article IV, Unesco could resort to the procedure  of conventions and recommendations. The  speaker shared the views of the delegate of the  United States of America, who, in the Official  and External Relations Commission, had spoken  against the establishment of a standing legal  committee; she also subscribed to the opinions  expressed by the delegate of the United Kingdom  with regard to the desirability of adopting the  procedure of recommendations, as they were less  complicated than conventions. Referring to the  Statement of Methods [document 5C/5 (I), Rev.,  pages 80 and 81], providing for the possibility of  “declarations” and even of mere “appeals”, the  speaker thought it advisable to keep to declara-  tions, the success of which depended primarily  on the persuasiveness of the text adopted by the  Conference. Very careful and costly preparation  was essential when any draft agreement was  being planned. Miss Pedersen  thought that a recommendation authorizing the  Director-General to enquire from Member States  after some interval of time why they had not  given effect to a recommendation adopted by the  Conference would be more effective than a con-  vention signed by a small number of States and  ratified by an even smaller number: a good  recommendation or, still better, a good declara-  tion would carry far more weight, especially as  it was difficult to express lofty ideas in legal  terminology (as evidenced by the International  Convention on Genocide and the Covenant on  Human Rights).  [11] Mrs. BRUNAUER (United States of Amer-  ica) congratulated the Secretariat on its work;  the document submitted to the Third Session of  the General Conference had not enabled delega-  tions to realize the full implications of the prob-  lem and to present useful observations.  Document 5C/OXR/16 covered three different  matters: procedure for the framing of conven-  tions; definition of recommendations and proce-  dure relating to them; and the establishment of  a standing legal committee. The delegation of  the United States of America would take a nega-  tive stand on the latter point. It would also  advocate making clearer reference to the part to  be played by the Executive Board. It thought  the time had come to lay down regulations for  conventions, a more effective method in that it  would be seldom used. With regard to recom-  mendations, the United States delegation thought  that Unesco should consider a procedure which  had proved very effective in the case of ILO; it  was necessary for such a procedure to be clearly  defined in a document and for the resultant re-  sponsibilities of Member States to be specified.  It should not be forgotten that recommendations  frequently had to be carried out by non-govern-  mental organizations. Mrs. Brunauer thought the  drafting Sub-Committee should give separate con-  sideration to the three matters dealt with in  document 5C/OXR/16.
668  [12] Dr. ZEISSL (Austria) agreed with Miss Pe-  dersen that recourse should seldom be had to  conventions; however, they were necessary In  certain cases, as for instance, recognition of the  equivalence of university degrees, or the draft  convention for the protection of monuments in  time of war, presented by the Italian delegation.  The first necessity was to arrive at a clear defini-  tion of the term “recommendation”, which would  win unanimous support and meet with the ap-  proval of legal experts. Dr. Zeissl thought there  was no need to adopt strict rules to determine  whether any particular problem should be regu-  lated by a convention or a recommendation: the  responsibility of deciding each case on its merits  should be left to the Conference or the Executive  Board.  [13] Dr. BEAGLEHOLE (New Zealand) fully  endorsed the views so clearly expressed by  Miss Pedersen. With regard to the question of  definition, raised by Mr. Cowell and Dr. Zeissl,  Dr. Beaglehole remarked that definitions were  already given under Sections 13 and 14 of the  Statement of Methods: he would be glad to know  whether that was sufficient for delegates, or  whether they required clearer definitions. Lastly,  he wished to know whether the Procedure Com-  mittee was discarding the idea of establishing a  standing legal committee.  [14] The CHAIRMAN pointed out that the Pro-  gramme and Budget Commission had deferred  consideration of Sections 13 and 14 of the State-  ment of Methods until it had the opinion of the  Procedure Committee on the matter of conven-  tions and recommendations. As regards the  establishment of a standing legal committee, it  was for the General Conference to take some  decision; the fact that the Official and External  Relations Commission had not approved the  establishment of a standing legal committee  would appear to authorize the Procedure Com-  mittee not to go too deeply into the question; it  was to be presumed that the General Conference  would also decide against the establishment of  such a committee.  The meeting rose at 1 p.m.
