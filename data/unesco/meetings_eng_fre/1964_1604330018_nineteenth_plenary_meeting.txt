6.1 Mr. Wilhelm EBERT (World Confederation of Organizations of the Teaching Profession  Mr. President, delegates of the General Conference, 1 wish to express my sincere  thanks for this opportunity to speak on behalf of the World Confederation of Organizations of  the Teaching Profession. Given the increasing operational programme of Unesco, we think  that teachers! organizations have to play a very important rôle.
6.2 We welcome with enthusiasm the special emphasis the Director-General has given to  educational planning, to the eradication of {lliteracy and to the training and status of  teachers. Our Confederation has particular interest in these activities, because we feel  they are sensitive indications of concern for the fast changing social factors of our times.  The problem we educators have to face is not only that of turning education into a major  force for accelerating economic growth, but of finding direction for the growing population  of teenagers, whose dynamism and idealism could be harnessed for the constructive pro-  grammes ahead of us. This is a live and urgent problem which the entire teaching profes -  sion has to face, in the course of their daily tasks. Whilst plans are being formulated,  while sociologists, psychologists, economists and educators meet and debate, millions of  anonymous teachers have to face this difficult problem, year after year, month after month  and even day after day. In the villages, in the towns, in sumptuous buildings, in zinc sheds  and mud huts, in Asia, in Africa, in the Americas and in Europe, in every classroom, the  teacher has to find a concrete and convincing solution to problems that have baffled  psychologists, sociologists and politicians.
6.3 The question here is, what have we done to arm these lone and anonymous fighters to  face a situation that could well change the very foundations and structure of the world we  live in? What status do we accord the men and women to whom we entrust the future of our  sons and daughters? In the fast changing societies of today, with the unprecedented  explosion of the school-age population, we are faced with the problem of providing educa~  tional opportunities to all - irrespective of race, class, ability or age. And at the same  time we have to safeguard the quality of education and the efficiency and the status of the  teaching profession. One problem is as urgent as the other. On the solution of these  problems, depends the very nature of the society we envisage for ourselves.
6.4 We note with satisfaction, Mr. President, Unesco's continued interest in the promo-  tion of activities designed to improve the status of the teaching profession. But the  recognition of the necessity of improving the present professional, social and economic  status of teachers has to be developed far beyond the stage of lip-service. It is our  sincere hope that the deliberations of the Expert Committee on the Status of Teachers,  convened by Unesco last May, and the preparation of an international instrument concern-  ing the status of teachers, will succeed in placing the teaching profession in the mainstream  of national and international educational activities. Dr. William Carr, the Secretary-  General of WCOTP, said at that meeting: " we are not here to celebrate the importance of  the teaching profession, except as an instrument for changing education to meet the needs  of mankind. Our purpose is not to build a greater place for teaching in itself, but only to  build a more secure place for teaching, in the light of the needs of humanity" .
6.5 We regret that in Unesco’ s educational programme for 1965-1966, not enough  emphasis is given to the provision of in-service training of teachers. We cannot stress  often enough the imperative need for regular and continuous programmes of in-service  training. I am not referring to crash programmes to fill current gaps in the school-  staffing table. It is not a question of a stop-go-stop policy. In both developing and in-  dustrialized countries, there is a pressing need for in-service training programmes. A  large proportion of the teaching staff in developing countries does not have adequate pro-  feasional training and, in some cases, 70 per cent and even 80 per cent is unqualified. On  the other hand, in both the developing and developed countries, teachers have to keep pace  with an ever-growing body of knowledge, and with new teaching methods and techniques.  The steady turn ove of new challenges to the teaching profession can be faced adequately  only with the provision of a widespread and systematic programme of in-service training.  We shall not rest content with throwing the challenge to national educational authorities.

We feel confident that we shall thus be able to help bring  to the educational systems of the world a more dynamic and efficient teaching staff.
6.6 Let there be no misinterpreta’{on that my plea for a due recognition of the status of  teachers, and for better and more permanent provision for in-gervice training, is un-  related to overall educational development. So far, educational planning has remained a  virgin area for the teaching profession. But the time is now ripe for a two-way traffic  between educational planners and teachers! organizations. Our contribution may be  classified under three general categories: First, dissemination of an understanding of the  concept of educational planning. Second the promotion of research by teachers' organiza-  tions on educational problems, so as to assist educational authorities in the formulation  and implementation of plans. Third, assistance to educational planning missions through  the services of our member teachers' organizations. Similarly, we strive for closer  participation of the organized teaching profession in the area of literacy and adult educa-  tion. Fully aware of the responsibility of teachers' organization in this field, WCOTP  set up a Committee on Adult Education as far back as 1959 to advise and assist us in  activities designed to promote these programmes. The trained teacher can be used pro-  fitably as an agent for this task, with further appropriate orientation. His acquaintance  with the immediate social environment and his accepted place as educator in the community  are invaluable in ensuring the success of such campaigns often conducted among sections  of the population which are prone to traditional resistance to progress. The World Con-  federation of Organizations of the Teaching Profession is capable and willing to mobilize  the forces of this readily available personnel to carry the campaign to far off places, often  inaccessible to outside personnel, both in the social and physical sense.
