[3] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] Gentlemen, we shall now go on to the  most important part of our work, namely, con-  sideration of the reports of the Commissions.  They have all been the subject of keen debate, and  so we have to avoid two dangers—I think equally  serious. Firstly, we must not begin the discus-  sion all over again, which would amount to  rendering the work of the Commissions negative  and would also keep us here for hours. The other  danger is the converse of the first and is by no  means negligible: it consists in the plenary Con-  ference becoming a sort of commission with the  task of ratifying and recording decisions, with-  out discussion; frankly, that is too humble a task  for a sovereign assembly such as this. 1 there-  fore venture to suggest a composite procedure,  which the General Committee has just approved  and which seems to me to meet the case. The  general lines of it are as follows: Rapporteurs to  be both entitled and required to address the  plenary Conference freely and without time-limit;  delegates in favour of the Rapporteurs’ conclusions  to be requested not to ask for the floor since their  views will have been embodied in those of the  Rapporteurs. Delegates against the Rapporteurs’  conclusions or wishing to present amendments to  be entitled to express their views and submit their  amendments subject to a time-limit—I suggest five  minutes; one can say much in that time. There-  after the Rapporteur would reply and the vote be  taken without further discussion. What I have  outlined is of course only applicable in cases
204                 where the Rapporteurs have definite conclusions  to submit. Where Commissions have reached no  definite conclusion or have left questions open,  it follows naturally that the matters in question  remain open for free discussion by the meeting,  though subject to the time restrictions I have out-  lined, i.e., no time-limit for Rapporteurs and a  time-limit of five minutes for other speakers. If  you agree, we shall conduct our proceedings along  these lines. Are there any remarks on this point?  There being no remarks, the procedure 1s  adopted. Frankly, 1  doubt whether there is a single Member State of       Unesco which is unable to find nationals who are  at the same time competent in Unesco matters  and can understand and make themselves under-  stood in at least one of the present working lan-  guages. I prefer to under-  stand a speaker directly, to get an immediate  impression of the strength of his convictions, of  the seriousness of his doubt, even if he has to  cope with difficulties of language. 1 have the  highest admiration for our interpreters, but a  translation can only convey the impression of a  personality to a very much reduced degree. In  meetings with consecutive Interpretations, of  course, the procedure can be slowed down dis-  astrously. Even in meetings interpreted simul-  taneously, I doubt whether the use of their own  language has been an advantage for many of our  colleagues who have lately started speaking  Spanish, even if they know that they express  themselves very cleverly in English or French. [100] Mr. Antonio CASTRO LEAL (Mexico) —  | Translation from the Spanish] Mr. President,  gentlemen, the question we are discussing has  been the subject of much thought, not in Unesco  alone (but in the United Nations, the Interna-  tional Labour Organization and the Food and  Agriculture Organization. Neither comparisons  nor allegations of differential treatment can be  validly made, because, in fact, the case of the  Spanish-speaking countries is a special one. We  are twenty states in all—twenty states with a  numerous population, occupying nearly the whole  of a continent. We have a great cultural tradi-  tion behind us and a great future before us; we  have ever played our part in all international  organizations since first we won to independence  a century ago. As I said, the question has been  discussed elsewhere. Assertions of respect for the  Spanish tongue and culture are universal; but  nevertheless everyone opposes the acceptance of  Spanish as a working language in international  institutions. All the arguments have already been  stated, restated, discussed and disputed, even in  Unesco at the Paris Conference of 1949.  [101] It is not wrong-headedness nor pride nor  any impulse to claim—or compel—the world’s  acceptance of our importance which makes us  want Spanish recognized as a working language  of the General Conference, but because we are  convinced that it is essential that Unesco should  complete what it began at the Beirut Conference  in recognizing it as a ‘spoken’ working language,  when, for good reasons given, its use was ap-  proved at General Conferences on all occasions  when simultaneous interpretation was available.  Yet we still lack acceptance of the written lan-  guage—of the use of Spanish as a written as well  as a spoken working language of the General Con-  ference. Doubts have been expressed about the  value of Spanish versions of a series of documents  of which some are entirely provisional and ephem-  eral. Nevertheless, as I have already pointed  out in the Procedure Committee, the General  Conference documents are of special importance  and their issue begins long before the opening  date of the Conference. The Report of the Director-  General, the opinion of the Executive Board, the  Budget and the Programme, are all part of the  Conference documents. All of them have to be  distributed to governments before the General  Conference, and all of them, if distributed in  French or English to Spanish-speaking countries,  run the risk of being less regarded and less  influential than they would be in Spanish. That  this need has already been remarked is proved  by the fact that on this occasion part of the  report—namely the Director-General's introduc-  tion—has been translated and distributed in  Spanish. My argument is equally applicable to  the series of additional documents submitted to  the Conference in the course of its proceedings  and to the resolutions passed by it and published  after its conclusion. These are documents of  vital importance for the understanding of  Unesco’s work and hence we of the Spanish-  speaking countries—who wish to co-operate with  Unesco and who want our governments and  National Commissions to take more interest In  Unesco’s work—seek to have all these documents  published in Spanish. Moreover, how are we to  set about “breaking down” a Conference's pro-  ceedings? While it may transpire, on publica-    tion, that some of the documents could have been  omitted, this inconvenience is more than offset  by avoiding the difficulty of effecting a classifica-  tion—in other words establishing in each specific  case a kind of norm by which to judge whether  the document should or should not be translated.  [102] In my view the acceptance of Spanish as  a written working language of the General Con-  ference is fully justified, not on the grounds al-  leged, as being for the satisfaction of a continental  bloc or national pride, but simply and solely as  a means of facilitating the co-operation of all  Spanish-speaking countries in Unesco’s work.  Furthermore, as I have already remarked, the  matter has already been settled by the United  Nations, of which Unesco is an organ, a working  Agency. It would be incongruous if, when the  United Nations had approved Spanish as a work-  ing language for their General Assembly, a simi-  lar decision were not taken by Unesco, since it  has, in my view, far more reason to do so than  the United Nations. The United Nations Organ-  ization is a political body dealing with govern-  ments rather than peoples. The influence of  Unesco reaches the people themselves through the  learned members of National Commissions and  even through the schools and teaching centres.  For this reason, I think it my duty to support  the draft resolution presented by the Procedure  Committee and ask that you approve it as it  stands in their report.
