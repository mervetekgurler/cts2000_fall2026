(4.1) Le PRESIDENT (traduit de l'espagnol) :  Avant de passer au débat de politique générale, je voudrais d'abord saluer  la délégation de Saint-Vincent-et-Grenadines, qui célèbre aujourd'hui sa fête  nationale.
(4.2) Je donne maintenant la parole, dans le débat de politique générale, à  S. Exc. M. Yayeh-Yirad Kitaw, ministre de l'éducation nationale et chef de la délé-  gation de 1'Ethiopie.
5.1 Mr KITAW (Ethiopia) :  Mr President, Mr Director-General, distinguished delegates, ladies and
12  gentlemen, it has been repeatedly said that this is the most momentous General  Conference session in the history of Unesco. Looking back through the records,  one could easily gain the impression that this evaluation has been applied to  many of preceding sessions. Nevertheless, this session is significant. We shall  be considering the final phase of our second Medium-Term Plan and the steps to be  taken in launching the third. Our responsibility is to ensure that these coming  eight years offer horizons of hope for the world community.
5.2 May I congratulate you, Mr President, on your unanimous election to the  high and demanding office in which you will be directing our discussions. The  delegation of the People's Democratic Republic of Ethiopia has every confidence  that you will lead the twenty-fourth session of the General Conference of Unesco  to a successful conclusion. It is timely that we should now emerge from the deep  controversy which has marked the past few years,. and reassert the clarity and  conviction which prompted the founding of our Organization 40 years ago.
5.3 My delegation, Mr President, has great pleasure in placing on record the  fact that we appear here today as the representatives of the first elected  government in the long history of the Ethiopian people. After widespread  discussion and debate, Ethiopia adopted a new Constitution which draws on its own  experience and the experience of mankind in defining the practical conditions  under which we will be able to pursue, with all vigour, the objectives for  social, cultural and economic well-being which Unesco itself has helped to  define. Earlier this year a nationwide ballot elected the members of our National  Assembly which met in September , and initiated the processes of democratic  government.
5.4 As Ethiopia takes this momentous step forward, it is appropriate to note  that Unesco has also moved a very long way, and very positively, during the first
40 years of its existence. Unesco began very much as an intellectual gathering  stimulated by the sad experiences through which mankind had then just passed. In
1946 , Unesco was largely the conscience of the inciustrialized world, arid  reflected the need to avoid future conflict between members of that world. But as  time went by, new issues appeared. In succeeding decades, Unesco was led by the  continuing march of history to consider more closely the condition of the whole  of mankind.
5.5 In 1946, there were 27 members, of which 17 were industrialized nations,  and, of the remainder, only one was an African Member State. Today, Unesco's  membership comprises 158, of which 113 are categorized as developing and 50 of  which are African. The position has therefore reversed since the year of  foundation when two thirds of the members were industrialized nations. This is a  sign, for the first time, of the possibilities for real global reflection and  effective co-operation.
5.6 Paradoxically, Mr President, the central challenge to the existence of  the Organization stems from the degree, during the past two decades, to wñich  Unesco has, with a large degree of success, endeavoured to face up to this  expanding global view of mankind.
5.7 Some 15 years ago, it was decided to move towards a longer-range  framework for an integrated, interdisciplinary and co-ordinated plan of action to  improve the global environment in those aspects for which the Organization had a  clear mandate. There is no doubt that the challenge was both enormous and  complex, but Unesco, through its ability to mobilize world intellectual  resources, led the way in several important respects. It carried out a thorough  situational analysis; and it identified needs and defined priorities, objectives  and programmes to meet them.
5.8 This was the first major exercise in planning to be conducted by a  United Nations agency. It resulted in the first Medium-Term Plan which was  launched ten years ago.
5.9 I submit, Mr President, that we must recognize the enormous contribution  to the processes of a deeply considered justification for action which has been  made by Mr Amadou-Mahtar M'Bow. It has been his dedicated elaboration and  application of the resolutions of the General Conference which has brought us now  to the threshold of the third Medium-Term Plan, in the face of the greatest  difficulties and even active obstruction.
5.10 Ethiopia, and indeed all the members of Unesco, will always remain  appreciative of his efforts to promote the development of education, science and  culture, pdrticularly in the developing countries. Our admiration and gratitude  goes far beyond his competent leadership, despite the numerous trying  circumstances, he has remained forthright and true to the ideals of Unesco's  Constitution.
5.11 Nobody would claim or pretend that this pilot exercise i.n long-range  planning and action has been perfect. Our agenda for this session in fact Offers  us the opportunity, among several mechanisms which have been developed, to assess  the wishes of Member States, to review past experience and profit therefrom in  the next planning period. It is our hope, therefore, that at this twenty-fourth  session which, together with the twenty-fifth, will set the scene €or Unesco's  action virtually to the end of this century, we shall have the courage and  resolution to maintain intact the major elements of a programme to which we have  all previously given our consent.
5.12 We should discuss the balance between reflection and action, remembering  Unesco's responsibility on the one hand for the 'intellectual and moral  solidarity of mankind' and, on the other, for 'instituting collaboration among  the nations', for more equal access to the benefits which human action can bring.
5.13 We should design or redesign modalities for action, and reassign  responsibilities, even away from Unesco, if we can be reasonably assured that  this will bring better results.
5.14 However, Mr President, I am sure that we will be condemned by future  generations if we allow adverse conditions or unreasonable pressures to result in  the erosion or disintegration of the internal consistency of our programme for  the betterment of mankind which has emerged after so much effort. It has taken 40  years for Unesco to develop a global potential for change in the quality of life  of the majority of mankind. Understanding that progress often provokes resistance  of a partisan nature, we must be sufficiently resolute to speak here with the  voice of the majority of mankind. We do not further the cause of mankind as a  whole by giving in to unreasonable and external pressures which are using the  power of the purse rather than reason and rationale. If we did succumb to such  pressures, we would be abandoning the very principles for which Unesco stands,  and we would, be initiating a return to the very situation which prompted its  founding in 1946.
5,15 Mr President, the collective voice of Unesco must be heard. It is a sign  of hope to the millions whom we represent here. But the voice must also be a  reminder to Member States that effective action is two-sided. On the one hand it  depends on the efficiency and effectiveness of the Secretariat, but it depends  equally on the commitment and active participation of Member States. This means  the selection and support for the country and regional level projects which will  produce lasting results.
5.16 Now, Mr President, with your permission I wish to addres; some of the  large issues which face us in the definition of the-third biennium of Unesco's  second Medium-Term Plan. This is our first practical task because, logically,  whatever we have to say about the third Medium-Term Plan must be based on a clear  understanding of what we wish to achieve during the next two years.
5.17 In the broadest terms, my delegation finds no element in the major  programnies to which we should not continue to give attention. There are, of  course, questions of priority, structuring of actions, and modalities for action,  for which the appropriate fora are the various commissions of this General  Conference.
5.18 We welcome the proposal to include in the participation programe for  the first time, actions which relate to Major Programme I. We see this as having  a potential contribution to the processes of regional reflection which have now  been initiated. We also support the proposals for greater attention to be paid to  the dissemination of the outcome of reflection and study carried out in this and  other major programmes.
5.19 Under Major Programe II we welcome the possibility that the celebration  of International Literacy Year could lead to a further strengthening of our  efforts in this field. In Ethiopia, for example, we are faced with the great  challenge of addressing ourselves to the problem of servicing around 10 million  literates who are now seeking paths for continued learning. The effective  retention of new literacy skills is now an issue of overwhelming importance.
5.20 We welcome the proposals for rationalization of action between Major  Programmes III and XI, but we wish to add the footnote that care should be taken  within Major Programme III not to diminish the basic task of supporting the  growth of communication capacity among and between our Member States.
5.21 We also see many ways in which, at regional and subregional levels, more  active collaboration between Unesco and other international agencies could result  in a conservation of scarce resources and, at the same time, bring the advantages  of intersectoral action.
5.22 To implement the conclusions of CASTAFRICA II, and to relate these to a  forward plan of action linked to the findings of MINEDAF V, the Lagos Plan of  Action, and the subsequent Priority Programme for Economic Recovery, action  deper.ds on support for regional frameworks and for technical co-operation among  developing countries.
5.23 Mr President, the material, social, cultural and ethical questions posed  by the dynamic relations between science and technology and development in  general are possibly the greatest challenge which Unesco must face. We feel,  however, that the proper approach is not to fund the opening up of new actions in  the third biennium of the second Medium-Term Plan. Rather, we should focus on the  consolidation of experience, and lay the foundations for co-ordinated action on  these issues in the third Medium-Term Plan, with a much higher priority for the  allocation of resources. In this, we find ourselves in full agreement with the  position taken by the Director-General.
5.24 In referring now to Major Programme X, which is of great importance to  African Member States, I would like to inform the General Conference that we  have, this year, established a MAB National Committee. This is seen as the first  of a series of national committees related to aspects of Major Programme X. They  are intended to provide us with mechanisms for the mobilization of the scientific  community and for the growth of relations with wider communities working in the  same areas.
5.25 Mr President, Major Programmes XII and XII1 are fundamental to the  dignity of mankind and have our full support. The Executive Board has rightly  stressed the need to give greater priority, and more emphasis to the battle  against attitudes and conditions which permit the continued existence of  apartheid. In promoting action against discrimination in all its forms, and  particularly its extreme form, apartheid, the Secretariat has drawn attention to  the need for a wide geographical location of research operations on these  questions. And we are happy to see that the Director-General has made provision  for this in his proposals in the coming biennium.
5.26 We are pleased to see a 4.3 per cent increase in resources for Major  Programme XIV - The status of women - in spite of financiql constraints. The  meeting in Nairobi in July 1985, which reviewed the outcome of the United Nations  Decade for Women, expressed some disappointment on the limited success achieved  during the Decade. We share that disappointment. The least we can do now is to  take a very close look at the Nairobi Forward-Looking Strategies for the  Advancement of Women to see how real support can be provided to these strategies  in the coming biennium, and in the third Medium-Term Plan.
5.27 To conclude, much of the activity in which we are engaged is designed to  bring about an improvement in the conditions of life in the future. Our  generation will be blamed by future generations if we fail to achieve,. and  maintain, forward movement. May I suggest, Mr President that in taking up this  fundamental task we would be well advised to focus, in all programmes, and with  greater vigour, on aspects which will bring benefit to women and youth. Women are  the providers of life, and are responsible for the larger part of its support.  Our youth must be equipped to deal with the challenges which stem from the  actions of our generation, and must be imbued with the spirit which will give  mankind hope for survival. Thank you.
