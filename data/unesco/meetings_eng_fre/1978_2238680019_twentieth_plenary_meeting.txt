Twentieth plenary meeting
6 November 1978 at 10.10 a.m. President: Mr. LEBLANC (Canada) later: Mr. TULL (Barbados) Vingtième séance plénière
1. Le PRESIDENT.- Mesdames, Messieurs, je déclare ouverte la vingtiéme séance pléniére de la vingtiéme session de la Conférence générale, je donne la parole a M. Hussein Ismael, secrétaire permanent du Ministére de l'éducation et chef de la délégation de l'Ethiopie.
2.1 Mr. ISMAEL (Ethiopia): Mr. President, Mr. Director-General, distinguished delegates, ladies and gentlemen: The Ethiopian delegation would like to join the preceding speakers, Mr. President, in congratulating you on your election to the presidency of the twentieth session of the General Conference. We have every confidence that under your wise leadership and guidance the deliberations of this Conference will have a successful outcome.
2.2 May we also take this opportunity to commend the Director-General and the Secretariat for their remarkable and comprehensive working documents, in particular the Draft Programme and Budget for
1979-1980 (20 C/5) and the Draft Adjustments to the Medium-Term Plan (20 C/4).
2.3 Ethiopia, Mr. President, has been a member of Unesco for almost a quarter of a century, but in a very real sense we feel that our membership has only recently become fully operational. The popular revolution achieved the overthrow of the corrupt feudal regime in September 1974, just before the eighteenth session of the General Conference, and our delegation to that Conference was able to view the objectives and aspirations of Unesco in a new light. Prior to this historic event, the population of Ethiopia had little contact with the processes of development which might rescue them from misery, ignorance and humiliation. Our revolution has brought radical changes; it has placed the resources of Ethiopia in the hands of its rightful owners; it has created popular organiza- tions within which people, in town and countryside, are mobilized for development: it has liberated the energies-and the creativity of a nation, and it has set objectives for progress in the Programme of the National Democratic Revolution, which echoes many of the objectives set by this international organization in its Medium-Term Plan.
20 2.4 It is in this context, Mr. President, that we feel the newness of our membership of Unesco. Very appropriately, our participation from 1974, as a Member State, in the formulation of a new programme of action for Unesco, coincided with the definition of a new programme of action for Ethiopia. In all sectors of development which fall within Unesco's field of competence in education, in science and technology, in cultural questions and in communications, very considerable advances have already been made in Ethiopia, and the stage has been set for even more rapid progress towards an improvement in the quality of life.
2.5 This progress has been achieved in the face of great difficulties. Throughout this period of change in which the Ethiopian people have been restructuring their society and their economy, they have also had to struggle against concerted and orchestrated attacks by internal and external enemies, all of whom have received the active support of interests outside Ethiopia who were anxious that the popular revolution should not succeed. These attacks included the continued violation of our borders, and culminated in the penetration of Ethiopia, to a depth of 700 kilometres, in military aggression by a neighbouring State.
2.6 Mr. President, when we come to the General Conference and give active support to Unesco's, major platforms for the promotion of peace and international understanding, we speak with very real experience of the consequences of the acts of men whose minds are still turned towards the promotion of war and aggression. Not only have we been obliged to apply the fruits of our labour and our scarce resources to the active defence of our motherland, but we now have to turn to the repair and reconstruction of the physical damage caused by this aggression. As a victim of brazen aggression, Ethiopia suffered colossal damage of essential infrastructural facilities worth millions of -dollars, as well as considerable loss of human life.
2.7 Mr. President, Ethiopia stands for peace, for international understanding, for the common welfare of mankind, and for the independence, integrity and fruitful development of the cultures and educational systems of Member States, as major factors in the promotion of mutual understanding between peoples. Ethiopia would like, now, to devote the whole of its energies and resources to the purposes of development.
2.8 Prior to the spontaneous outbreak of the revolution, education, whether formal or non-formal, was limited to a few urban centres. The gloomy picture in the field of education displayed by the feudal regime on the eve of the revolution was a 90 per cent illiteracy rate among the population while in the formal education sector the system catered for only 20 per cent of the school-age children.
2.9 One of the first tasks that the Provisional Military Administration Government had to embark upon was the proclamation of the abolition of feudalism by legislating the Agrarian Law which put "Land in the hands of the tiller". However, since the proclamation alone was not adequate in shatter- ing the feudal shackle that had enslaved the peasants of Ethiopia, the Government proclaimed the First National Campaign, in which 60,000 senior secondary and university students took part, to ensure the implementation of the Land Reform Act and at the same time to establish peasant associ- ations and to raise their level of literacy and political consciousness.
2.10 The effect of the campaign was significant in the formation and organization of popular associa- tions, both urban and rural, in the implementation of land use and its distribution. What is more, the campaign intensified the class struggle between the landless peasants and the handful of diehard feudal lords, who did not wish to surrender their latifundia and the peasants they had enslaved for years. Despite numerous obstacles and sabotage, the downtrodden masses of Ethiopia emerged victorious. At present we are actively engaged in preparing the launching of a comprehensive nation-wide campaign directed to the promotion of economic and cultural development of the broad masses. A massive literacy programme is envisaged as one of the components of the comprehensive economic and cultural campaign. The task force assigned to study the literacy component of the programme has recently concluded its studies and presented its proposal regarding the details of the programme, namely, the definition of its objective and priorities; the medium of instruction and instructional materials and the human and material resources required to attain this objective.
2.11 During the last four years the Ethiopian masses, though embroiled in a bitter struggle against internal and external enemies of their revolution, have nevertheless shown determination to strive steadfastly to surmount difficulties which seem overwhelming. They have learned the truism that the new and just order is superior to the unjust feudal, capitalist and decaying order. They rallied to the call of the motherland, magnanimously gave what they could spare, volunteered for service and fully participated in the development schemes of their localities. One can enumerate many instances of popular enthusiasm, but for the sake of brevity, suffice it to say that the revolution of Ethiopia has unleashed a tremendous human force eager to participate in development schemes. This revolu- tionary zeal is demonstrated by the doubling of the number of elementary schools and the number of children in them during the past four years, thanks to the willingness of the masses to share the burden of school construction and equipment.
2.12 The participation of peasant urban-dwellers in women's and youth associations contributed in the establishment and creation of different innovative infrastructures among which the Awraja Peda- gogical Centres and Community Skill Training Centres are important educational delivery points.
2.13 The APC, which are established in every subdistrict, serve as educational development or resource centres. Here, teaching materials are developed, new studies and inquiries are initiated, centrally designed curricula are related to local needs, and teachers are professionally stimulated through in-service training and workshops. It is our earnest hope that these centres will serve the
728    objectives of the Network of Educational Innovation for Development in Africa (NEIDA), which involves 20 projects organized for national, regional and international purposes.
2.14 Community Skill Training Centres, which are located at the district level, play an important role as multipurpose centres where varied community needs such as literacy, agriculture, health and family education are catered for. The programme is flexible, diversified and comprehensive to meet the requirements of each locality and the needs of each target group - children, youth and adults. The experience acquired in these centres is designed to prepare locally trained personnel, rural ani- mateurs for leadership and technical in-put for rural development.
2.15 In addition to these innovations, 11. radio stations to cover the needs of the entire country are being set up to tackle the problem of inaccessible remote regions with no infrastructures.
2.16 It is intended that this programme, in addition to its main function of achieving democratic access to education, especially for adults and women, through the use of local languages, should be utilized by extension agents and peasant associations in local development programmes at the grass root level.
2.17 Mr. President, we firmly believe that the new socio-political environment, the political will of the Government, and the active participation and determination of the masses are conducive to the enhancement of literacy and mass education. My delegation, therefore, appreciates the co-operation of Unesco and of other international bodies in these fields of endeavour.
2.18 Mr. President, in the sphere of higher education, Ethiopia has recently adopted a policy which will guarantee equity of access to higher education institutions for the hitherto disadvantaged rural school population. Every effort is being made to direct higher education activities to ensure their effective contribution to the development of the rural areas. Similarly, research activities in institu- tions of higher education are giving priority to rural development. In general, the policy is to strengthen existing research institutions and to open new ones as the need arises. These institutions will provide research and consultative support for the economic, scientific, technological and cultural needs and interests of the Ethiopian people on a wider basis. Almost all research projects are required to contribute to the knowledge base for teaching and service and will be focused on national problems and priority areas of development. In addition, in order to avoid duplication of functions and to enable the effective and economic utilization of facilities, research programmes are co-ordinated through the Ethiopian Commission for Higher Education.
2.19 Mr. President, in the field of science and technology, Unesco's contribution to the preparation of the forthcoming United Nations Conference on Science and Technology for Development (UNCSTD), has been considerable and will undoubtedly will help to ensure its success.
2.20 Unesco's role in promoting the efforts of developing countries in the area of science and technol- ogy could be further strengthened by giving appropriate consideration and emphasis to: (a) strength- ening scientific and technological institutions in developing countries; (b) strengthening research and development centres in various sectors of science and technology; (c) providing assistance to developing countries in the establishment of national infrastructures for effective transfer of appro- priate knowledge and information.
2.21 My delegation appreciates that Unesco's programme activities include, inter alia, the organization of numerous symposia, seminars and conferences. Nevertheless, we feel that a reduction in such activities is required, so that Unesco may redirect money and expertise to support field activities in the developing countries, particularly in the least developed among them.
2.22 Ethiopia is a country that possesses a diverse cultural wealth accumulated and preserved over a thousand years. A part of this wealth belongs to the world's cultural heritage, which Unesco, in co-operation with Member States, is doing its utmost to preserve for posterity.
2.23 Mr. President, everyone recognizes the role which the mass media play in modern societies. The use of the information media for hostile propaganda purposes has to be brought under control. Without adequate and effective governmental control, the media could be deployed for anti-social and anti-cultural activities, with tremendous consequences for society as a whole. That the information media have become a subject for intense discussion within Unesco is a matter of no surprise. Informa- tion is a monopoly of a very few countries, and because of the capabilities of these same countries in information technology, the media have been used to disseminate unfounded and baseless propaganda against Member States. The Ethiopian revolution has been a constant target of imperialist propaganda campaigns of vilification. Unless such media become subject to governmental supervision and direction, they could cause deterioration of relations between nations. Consequently, we endorse the draft Declaration on Fundamental Principles Governing the Contribution of the Mass Media to Strengthening Peace and International Understanding and to Combating War Propaganda, Racialism and Apartheid, as contained in document 20 C/20.
2.24 Mr. President, the realization of the new international economic order is an urgent imperative to arrest the constantly widening gap between the developing and the developed countries as far as their levels of income and wealth are concerned. The restructuring of the world economic system appears to be the only means to resolve the problems of inflation, currency fluctuation, trade and related matters.
2.25 There is no issue more important on the agenda of the world community than disarmament. The world cannot live in peace and harmony when increasing budgetary allocations are being made
729    by States for research, development, production and stock-piling of weapons and systems of mass destruction. The awareness of people the world over of the existence of the alarming arsenals of weapons of mass destruction and their threat to peace has been minimal. Unesco can therefore appropriately play a role in galvanizing world public opinion in favour of the halting the arms race.
2.26 The last vestiges of colonialism have not yet been removed from Southern Africa. The racist minority regime of Ian Smith has intensified its repressive measures in recent days. Namibia, whose admission to Unesco membership we welcome, is still under the illegal occupation of South Africa, which has perpetrated the abhorrent policy of apartheid. The three evils of colonialism, imperialism and apartheid have to be totally abolished if peace is to prevail in Southern Africa. The Ethiopian delegation fully supports the legitimate armed struggle being waged by the South West Africa People's Organization (SWAPO) and the Patriotic Front to liberate the peoples of Namibia and Zimbabwe from imperialism and apartheid.
2.27 The draft Declaration on Race and Racial Prejudice which is before the General Conference represents an initial first step towards the elimination of racism and racial prejudice. The Ethiopian delegation fully endorses the Draft Declaration, as contained in document 20 C718, and hopes that it will be unanimously adopted by the General Conference.
2.28 The situation in the Middle East remains a threat to world peace and security. A lasting solution to the problem can be found only through the recognition of the legitimate rights and aspira- tions of the Palestine people.
2.29 In conclusion, let me state, Mr. President, that the numerous activities and the tasks with which Unesco has been entrusted form an integral part of the global effort of the world community to build a prosperous and just world whose peoples are free from war, hunger and disease. And it is with a sense of satisfaction that I say Unesco has promoted and will continue to promote peace through the furthering of international understanding to make this planet a happy place to live in. Thank you, Mr. President.

6.13 Monsieur le Président, le contexte politique et idéologique que je viens d'esquisser à grands traits pour rester dans les limites du temps qui m'est imparti sous-tend tout un ensemble d'actions concrètes que notre Organisation entend entreprendre d'ici à 1982. Je voudrais maintenant aborder les actions prévues dans le domaine de l'éducation avant de passer aux domaines de la science, de la culture et de la communication.
6.14 Monsieur le Président, en République populaire du Congo, l'éducation fait partie des tâches que le Parti congolais du travail et le gouvernement se sont assignées en vue de la réalisation du bien-être du peuple. Nous voulons qu'elle soit à la portée de tout le monde, c'est-à-dire accessible à tous les jeunes d'âge scolaire, à tous les analphabètes et à tous les adultes, hommes et femmes, exerçant une profession. Un système éducatif inspiré par ces principes est exigeant et coûteux.
6.15 Mais malgré les difficultés inévitables, notre pays est allé de l'avant et a consenti beaucoup de sacrifices pour aboutir à des résultats assez remarquables puisque plus de 90 % de jeunes Congolais vont à l'école. Des progrès notables ont été également accomplis dans le domaine de l'alphabétisation et de l'éducation permanente. Pour la scolarisation de tous les jeunes Congolais, l'alphabétisation, la formation initiale et permanente en vue de l'apprentissage et de la maîtrise des métiers utiles au développement du pays, la République populaire du Congo dépense chaque année près de 40 % de son budget.
6.16 A la dix-neuvième session de notre Conférence, j'avais indiqué les fondements, les orientations et les objectifs de ce système éducatif que nous appelons l'Ecole du peuple. Aujourd'hui, je voudrais me contenter de noter d'autres faits saillants et de dégager les perspectives d'avenir qui cadrent avec les thèmes contenus dans le Projet de programme. En effet, après l'obtention de résultats quantitativement satisfaisants en matière d'accès à l'éducation, nous voulons maintenant consacrer des efforts plus importants à l'amélioration qualitative du système. C'est ainsi qu'un vaste programme de développement de l'école maternelle vient d'être mis sur pied pour préparer tous les jeunes Congolais à accéder avec d'égales chances de succès à l'éducation primaire. Pour cela, il nous faut résoudre le problème du déficit en enseignants, en locaux, en matériel pédagogique, les problèmes des programmes, des méthodes et des techniques d'éducation qui doivent correspondre aux préoccupations politiques, économiques et socioculturelles de nos masses. Il nous faut surtout réduire le fossé existant entre l'école et le monde du travail.
6.17 Ce souci nous oblige à tenir compte du système économique de notre pays, basé essentiellement sur l'agriculture, pour édifier la nouvelle école congolaise. L'école, tout le monde en convient, ne peut pas évoluer en marge du système économique national. Dans notre pays, elle doit s'intéresser essentiellement au monde rural et préparer les élèves à le mettre en valeur. Déjà des initiatives sont prises à l'Université Marien N'Gouabi, dont l'Institut de développement rural, situé à quelques kilo- mètres de Brazzaville, forme des ingénieurs et d'autres agents du monde rural. L'expérience faite dans cet établissement mérite d'être suivie et encouragée par l'Unesco, dans la mesure où elle ébauche une solution aux problèmes que pose le monde rural, base essentielle de l'économie dans la plupart des pays en développement. Au niveau des collèges, nous envisageons de créer un grand nombre d'écoles moyennes pour les métiers agricoles et industriels, dans lesquelles seront formés des personnels capables de réaliser les projets de développement rural retenus par le Parti et le gouvernement.
6.18 Mais quelles que soient son ampleur et sa pertinence, l'action de l'école ne peut constituer qu'une contribution à la solution des problèmes d'organisation et d'animation du monde rural.
6.19 Aussi pensons-nous que toutes les institutions nationales doivent s'attacher à résoudre ce problème et que les responsables de l'éducation doivent préparer les élèves, les enseignants et les parents d'élèves à la nouvelle situation qui est celle de la mobilisation de toutes les forces vives du pays en vue du travail productif à tous les niveaux. Le travail productif doit être considéré comme le support du nouveau système éducatif.
6.20 Le travail productif est un concept plein de promesses dont nous devons savoir appréhender les aspects éducatifs, sociologiques, économiques et philosophiques, bref toute la richesse, car c'est encore par son intermédiaire que nous pouvons, comme le veut l'Unesco, assurer la liaison entre le scolaire et l'extrascolaire. Si le travail productif est bien organisé à l'école, les responsables de
736    l'éducation que nous sommes pourront aborder avec plus d'efficacité les problèmes que pose l'inser- 20 tion des jeunes gens dans le circuit économique national. En tant qu'instrument au service de l'édu- cation, le travail productif doit être propice à l'épanouissement de toutes les facultés de l'homme et à la création des aptitudes qui le préparent à agir sur son milieu en vue de son bien-être.
6.21 A ce sujet, il nous apparaît nécessaire d'admettre que l'introduction du travail productif dans le système éducatif n'est possible que si nous réhabilitons nos cultures, nos langues, bref tout notre environnement philosophique, économique et culturel, car c'est sur cet environnement que nous devons désormais savoir compter pour former l'homme de demain afin qu'il soit capable d'apporter à l'humanité tout entière ce que son milieu a d'original, c'est-à-dire les richesses qui n'existent pas ailleurs et dont l'humanité a besoin pour accomplir des progrès sur le plan scientifique, culturel et économique.
6.22 Le concept de travail productif à l'école a des incidences nombreuses et profondes, Monsieur le Président, dans la mesure où la plupart des problèmes majeurs de l'éducation peuvent être abordés dans ce cadre. Tel est le cas de celui des langues locales. La place des langues locales, jusqu'ici marginale dans les systèmes éducatifs actuels - tournés essentiellement vers l'extérieur - doit être reconsidérée car on ne peut mieux pénétrer un milieu qu'en utilisant le médium linguistique qui permet de le comprendre. Il faut réhabiliter les langues locales et assurer leur promotion à l'école et autour d'elle.
6.23 Qu'on nous permette de saluer ici la position courageuse prise par l'Unesco sur cette question dans le Projet de programme. Les objectifs fixés pour l'horizon 2000 devront être réalisés. Nous le disons parce que nous sommes convaincus que l'utilisation de ces langues dans la vie officielle et dans toutes les activités de nos pays constituera un atout important pour l'amélioration du processus d'apprentissage à tous les niveaux, pour l'alphabétisation et enfin pour la démocratisation de la culture et de l'information.
6.24 La promotion des langues africaines, longtemps étouffées par le colonialisme et le néo-colonia- lisme, doit être abordée avec tout le sérieux qu'elle mérite et sous tous ses aspects. La lutte sur ce terrain a été longue et elle le sera encore si notre Organisation et les Etats membres ne mettent pas en oeuvre une stratégie débouchant sur des opérations ayant un impact réel sur les milieux concernés.
6.25 L'Unesco devra aider les chercheurs et les centres de recherche dont les travaux portent sur ces questions. A -Brazzaville, le département de linguistique de l'Université Marien N'Gouabi et le département des langues congolaises de l'Institut national de recherche et d'action pédagogiques, qui ont déjà réalisé des travaux fort appréciables dans ce domaine, seraient heureux de recevoir tout concours humain et matériel susceptible de les aider à promouvoir la recherche sur le lingala et le munukutuba, langues véhiculaires parlées dans notre pays et dans les pays voisins. Les établisse- ments de formation d'enseignants devront être mis à contribution en vue de l'élaboration et la pro- duction des manuels d'enseignement et d'alphabétisation. Mais pour que ce travail soit convenablement mené, il faut au préalable déterminer l'objet des études a faire. Il semble que les études portant sur les langues véhiculaires sont celles qu'il convient d'organiser en tout premier lieu car ces langues ont davantage d'impact sur la vie nationale et internationale. En effet, dans certains cas, elles débordent le cadre strictement national et facilitent les échanges entre plusieurs pays. Tel est le cas en Afrique centrale du lingala qui est parlé au Zaïre, chez nous, dans le sud du Cameroun et dans l'Empire centrafricain tout comme il l'est en Angola et dans les régions du Gabon voisines de la République populaire du Congo. Une telle langue devra faire l'objet d'une attention particulière de la part de l'Unesco dans le cadre du projet Horizon 2000. Je voudrais ici suggérer, Monsieur le Prési- dent, que dans ce cadre, il soit orgarisé un ou plusieurs séminaires régionaux en vue de la promo- tion de cette langue en Afrique centrale. Mon pays est tout disposé à apporter sa contribution à la réalisation de toute action qui irait dans ce sens.
6.26 Les problèmes du lingala font penser à ceux du swahili, du haoussa, du wolof et du bambara et à ceux d'autres langues victimes de préjugés et d'une domination néo-coloniale. Il faut donc une stratégie d'ensemble, avec des actions précises dans chaque région concernée qui porteront sur les aspects importants des structures de ces langues.
6.27 Monsieur le Président, si nous insistons sur le problème des langues nationales c'est parce qu'il est déterminant non seulement dans le monde scolaire, mais aussi pour l'élimination de l'analpha- bétisme qui est l'un des objectifs que nous envisageons d'atteindre dans un avenir proche avec le concours de toutes les institutions qui, comme l'Unesco, s'intéressent à cette question. La solution de ce problème conditionne le développement global de nos pays. Toutefois, il nous semble convenable de l'aborder, tant à l'école qu'à l'échelle de la société tout entière, dans le cadre du travail produc- tif ; en effet le travail productif bien compris et bien éxécuté par toute la nation peut, si une place appropriée est faite aux facteurs psychologiques, linguistiques, technologiques, scientifiques et culturels, constituer une base sûre pour le développement de nos pays respectifs. Tel est d'ailleurs l'objectif de l'Unesco.
6.28 Le travail productif dans le processus éducatif, tel est précisément le thème d'une conférence que Brazzaville, capitale de mon pays, abritera, sous la présidence d'honneur du chef de l'Etat congolais, du 25 au 30 novembre 1978. Ce thème occupe une position centrale dans notre réflexion sur l'édification d'un système éducatif cadrant étroitement avec les voeux de l'Unesco et les aspira- tions des masses laborieuses de notre pays. Nul n'ignore en effet que le travail productif nous plonge au coeur même des rapports entre individus et groupes sociaux à l'échelon national et inter- national. Il nous fait réfléchir à la science, à la technologie, à la culture et à l'information qui sont autant d'éléments concourant à sa mise en oeuvre. L'on peut d'ores et déjà espérer que la conférence
737    en question contribuera a la recherche des solutions aux problèmes que posent actuellement dans le monde et notamment dans les pays en développement les relations entre l'école et son environnement ou le rôle de l'école en tant que facteur de développement.
6.29 Grâce aux rapports humains harmonieux qui pourraient être créés dans la société grâce aux nouveaux comportements qui auront été façonnés par l'introduction du travail productif dans le processus éducatif, on devrait assister progressivement à l'élimination du traitement défavorable dont la femme et, notamment, la jeune fille sont victimes dans la plupart des Etats membres de notre Organisation. La femme pourra alors être considérée par nous tous comme un être cher, parce que la société humaine ne peut se construire sans elle ; elle pourra alors aller à l'école, se cultiver, s'in- former et enfin prendre une part active à la construction de son pays et aux rapports avec les autres nations.
6.30 Monsieur le Président, notre Organisation doit intensifier son action en vue de l'épanouissement total de la femme et veiller à ce qu'une éducation sexuelle soit donnée aux femmes et en particulier aux jeunes filles, pour qu'elles cessent d'être victimes, dans ce domaine, de situations préjudiciables à leur développement et à leur insertion harmonieuse dans la société.
6.31 Tous ces thèmes, que l'on trouve dans le Projet de programme de l'Unesco, intéressent mon pays et l'organisation dont je dirige le conseil exécutif, à savoir le Bureau africain des sciences de l'éducation (BASE), pour laquelle je sollicite, une fois de plus, le soutien de l'Unesco. En effet, dans le cadre de la décentralisation de ses activités en Afrique, l'Unesco peut confier l'exécution d'une partie de son programme au BASE.
6.32 La réalisation de tous ces projets appelle des recherches scientifiques fondamentales et appli- quées dans tous les domaines. Or, on constate, non sans amertume, que la recherche scientifique est bien loin d'être facilement accessible aux pays en développement. A cause du manque d'équipements et de fonds nécessaires aux divers travaux, bon nombre de ces pays restent dans l'impossibilité de connaître et de maîtriser leurs potentialités scientifiques et se trouvent de ce fait réduits à importer des technologies qui souvent aggravent leur sous-développement et leur dépendance vis-à-vis de l'extérieur. Dans ce domaine, notre Organisation devra veiller à ce que la promotion de la recherche scientifique soit effective dans chaque pays car il n'y a pas de développement sans recherche scien- tifique. Notre souhait est que l'Unesco dispose de moyens plus importants pour aider les pays les plus défavorisés à organiser des séminaires et des recherches à l'échelon régional et interrégional, en vue de permettre les échanges d'expériences.
6.33 Monsieur le Président, jusqu'ici, je n'ai pas parlé explicitement de la culture et de l'informa- tion. Notre conception de la culture veut qu'elle soit considérée avant tout comme un produit, une création de la société, et qu'à ce titre elle soit à la portée du peuple.
6.34 Quant à l'information, les technologies modernes ont pour effet de créer, à l'échelon interna- tional, une catégorie de pays condamnés à être sous-informés ou mal informés et, à l'intérieur de chaque pays, des zones où les informations internationales et même nationales resteront encore pen- dant longtemps inaccessibles aux masses laborieuses. Les intentions et le programme de l'Unesco en ce qui concerne l'établissement d'un nouvel ordre international de l'information plus juste doivent être soutenus.
6.35 Pour terminer, Monsieur le Président, je voudrais dire que l'Ecole du peuple - en tant que nouveau système éducatif de mon pays - se propose de contribuer dans le contexte politique, écono- mique et socioculturel congolais à la recherche des voies et moyens propres à aider la communauté internationale à former des hommes et des femmes qui la mettent à l'abri des idées rétrogrades, de l'injustice, de la violence, de l'apartheid, du racisme, du sionisme, bref à l'abri de tous les fléaux de notre époque.
6.36 Je sais que nous y parviendrons grâce à l'appui de l'Unesco et au concours des pays amis et des personnes et institutions qui s'intéressent, chez nous et dans le monde aux problèmes de la promotion de l'éducation et du développement. La nouvelle école congolaise, dont le fonctionnement a commencé de façon décisive voici un an, doit être un instrument au service du développement global et harmonieux de notre pays.
6.37 Je voudrais m'arrêter ici en souhaitant un plein succès aux travaux de la vingtième session de la Conférence générale de notre Organisation. Je vous remercie, Monsieur le Président.

10.1 Mr. JOSEPH (Trinidad and Tobago): Mr. President, Mr. Director-General, distinguished delegates, ladies and gentlemen: May I first join in congratulating Mr. LeBlanc on his election to the presidency of the General Conference. My delegation is confident that under his guidance our work will bear great fruit.
10.2 The delegation of Trinidad and Tobago also wishes to place on record its appreciation of the noble efforts of the Director-General in advancing the goals of Unesco and, more particularly, to congratulate him on his inspiring and comprehensive address to this assembly at the start of its deliberations. His eminent leadership of our Organization distinguishes him as one of the great internationalists of our time.
10.3 My first duty today, on behalf of the Government and people of the Republic of Trinidad and Tobago, is to welcome into the fold of our universal body its new members: the People's Republic of Angola, the Republic of Cape Verde, the Comoros, the Kingdom of Swaziland, Namibia and, in anticipa- tion of our function this afternoon, Dominica - one of our neighbouring sister States in the Caribbean.
10.4 Mr. President, the grave problems that confront mankind today are legion: inflation, unemploy- ment, political oppression, racism, poverty, illiteracy, ignorance and cultural stagnation. Many sectors of humanity, in developed and developing countries alike, and irrespective of political systems, feel enslaved because of the day-to-day conditions imposed on them by the existing world order. Nowhere, however, does the crisis plague humanity as acutely as in the Third World.
10.5 The central issue facing the United Nations system as a whole, and Unesco in particular, is to ensure greater appreciation of the fact that the solution to these problems is the responsibility of the international community in its entirety. There can be no hope for an alleviation of these problems unless there is a concerted commitment, on the part of all Member States, to the creation of a new international economic order.
10.6 The Government of my country sees this question as the major challenge facing the peoples of the world. For its own part, within its own territory and with the resources at its disposal, it has been carrying out programmes of economic and social reconstruction designed to repair the ravages of colonialism.
10.7 One of the chief strategies in this exercise lies in the education of the nation's children. Indeed, education and culture receive the largest slice of the public budget - over 20 per cent. With universal primary education achieved over 25 years ago, the goal of universal secondary education will be attained in the next four years. Increasing importance is being attached to special education. The secondary school curriculum emphasizes technical and vocational subjects, physical education and sport, the arts and moral training. Higher education is being reorganized with priority on science and technology. Women enjoy equal opportunity with men at all levels of the education system. Moreover, the Government is in the process of planning free school services for the nation's children as far as nutrition, medical and dental care, and bus transport are concerned.
10.8 A new accent is being placed on cultural development. Major projects at the planning or implementation stage include the construction of a new national library building, the erection of a new sports stadium, the provision of cultural art centres and - thanks to assistance from Unesco - the creation of a network of museums whose functions will include the preservation of our historical sites and buildings.
10.9 Mr. President, in order to succeed in establishing a new economic, social and cultural order at the national level, Trinidad and Tobago, in common with other developing countries, needs support from Unesco and its sister agencies in promoting the new international economic order. This is why my delegation supports the Director-General and Unesco in the approaches outlined in the Medium- Term Plan for the realization of this important objective.
10.10 We endorse Unesco's philosophical concept that the new order must encompass all aspects of man's total development, including economic, social and cultural development. In this connection, we wish to express our appreciation of the presentation by objectives of the Draft Programme and Budget for 1979-1980.
10.11 Permit me now, Mr. President, to comment briefly on one or two aspects of the Medium-Term Plan, and to indicate in passing how they relate to our developments at home. In so doing, I wish to emphasize that the delegation of Trinidad and Tobago believes that action by Unesco toward the establishment of the new international economic order must include the following five considerations, namely: a new concept of fundamental human rights to integrate and render indivisible civil and
744     political rights on the one hand, and economic, social and cultural rights on the other; international 20 education for disarmament and peace; a new philosophy of development which places man at the centre of the process, and which accommodates cultural diversity while recognizing a common humanity; a new international order for science and technology; and, finally, a new international order for information and communication.
10.12 As far as our first consideration - human rights - is concerned, I would point out that the Trinidad and Tobago Constitution guarantees civil and political rights to all, while the Government, as a matter of public policy, promotes the economic, social and cultural rights enumerated in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. The latter cannot, however, be guaranteed unless the international community agrees on new norms for economic, social and cultural relations among States. In demonstration of its stand on this issue, the House of Representatives of Trinidad and Tobago, three months ago, adopted a unanimous resolution in which the Government agreed to accede to both the United Nations Covenants on Human Rights - the Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, and the Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights.
10.13 May I refer to one aspect of human rights relating to women which is of immediate concern to us. Unesco should widen its programme of research into the effects of the increasing entry of women into productive sectors of employment on family structures, including the implications for the care and education of infants. Studies of this nature are needed in developed and developing coun- tries alike.
10.14 Still on the subject of human rights, the Trinidad and Tobago delegation takes this opportunity of voicing its full support for the measures in the Medium-Term Plan and in the biennial programme directed at combating the evil cancer of apartheid. The record of my country in this area at the international level is well known. The current programme, which has been in existence now for over one year and under which Africans from Zimbabwe and other territories in Southern Africa are undergoing training at educational institutions in Trinidad and Tobago, testifies to our solidarity with the oppressed peoples of Southern Africa. Trinidad and Tobago will support the adoption of the draft Declaration on Race and Racial Prejudice which is before this Conference.
10.15 With regard to the second consideration - disarmament - my delegation agrees with the policy of Unesco that education for disarmament must be an integral part of the establishment of the new international economic order. We therefore welcome the initiative taken by the Organization in propos- ing a World Congress on Education for Disarmament and Peace. Disarmament will undoubtedly release immense resources, including scientific and technological potential, for solving many of the urgent economic and social problems in all countries, developed as well as developing.
10.16 On the third consideration - man and development - we concur with Unesco's approach, in its programmes, according to which man must be placed at the centre of development. Two factors central to this policy need constant repetititon: firstly, cultural diversity must be the basis for international cultural development; and secondly, cultural diversity must be the platform for reinforc- ing a common humanity.
10.17 With respect to this policy, my delegation emphasizes that, in all Unesco's programmes, the Caribbean must be seen as a subregion of the Americas. In stressing this point in another context, the Prime Minister of Trinidad and Tobago, himself a distinguished historian and scholar, put it this way: "The Caribbean is normally defined, and has been so defined by us in particular, as an area of the islands and adjoining mainland countries in which the plantation economy first developed under the control of metropolitan powers from Europe with the bringing in of masses of alien labour, cheap labour, coerced labour from Africa, from India, from China and from other places. That is a distinctive unit - the Caribbean. It brings in Puerto Rico. It brings in Belize. It brings in Surinam. It brings in Cuba. It brings in Haiti. It brings in Martinique. It brings in Guadeloupe".
10.18 In this connection, Trinidad and Tobago welcomes the decision to establish a Regional Office for Unesco in Kingston, Jamaica, to serve the Caribbean
10.19 As far as the fourth consideration - science and technology - is concerned, the people of Trinidad and Tobago, like the peoples in the Third World, share the hope - which we trust is not in vain - that a new era in the application of science and technology for development will be ushered in with the United Nations Conference on Science and Technology for Development (UNCSTD), in 1979. It is necessary to reach international agreement for the transfer of technology on more equitable terms from developed to developing countries. We wish, however, to endorse fully the approach taken by Unesco, which places great emphasis on the creation of endogenous technology to suit the needs of developing countries. In this regard, my delegation would like to urge that priority attention be given to research projects relating to: laws impeding the transfer and development of technology; prospects for the exchange of technology and scientific knowledge among developing countries; and the effects on the natural and the social environment of technological changes in developing countries.
10.20 On the last of the considerations I mentioned earlier - information and communication - my delegation shares the view that a new order for information and communication is an essential component of the new international economic order. As part of such a new order, there must be easier dissemina- tion of scientific knowledge, closer cultural contact, and better human understanding among the peoples of the world. The Draft Declaration on the mass media raises a number of important issues related to this wider subject. For the present, however, I wish to comment on only three of them.
10.21 In the first place, because of the important role the mass media can play in strengthening
20 peace and international understanding, a serious obligation is incumbent on those responsible for the dissemination of information to ensure that the media are used for combating war propaganda, racialism and apartheid. Moreover, in our view, the mass media have a positive obligation to the entire international community to promote respect for fundamental human rights and freedoms and the advancement of peace.
10.22 Secondly, the new order for information and communication must seek to correct the quantita- tive and qualitative inequality in the flow of information to and from developing countries and between those countries. It is essential, therefore, for the mass media in developing countries to have adequate resources - both human and material - to enable them to gain strength and expand, and thus be better equipped to co-operate both among themselves and with the mass media in developed countries.
10.23 Thirdly, Trinidad and Tobago, as a sovereign democratic republic, guarantees fundamental human rights and freedoms including freedom of the press, under its Constitution. Under any democracy, whether national or international, rights and freedoms connote obligations and responsi- bilities. Trinidad and Tobago supports the provision in Article II of the draft Declaration that "the exercise of freedom of opinion, of expression and information, recognized as an integral part of human rights and fundamental freedoms ... confers special responsibilities and duties on the mass media".
10.24 In concluding, Mr. President, I wish to state that my delegation is fully aware of the immense disparities which exist between developed and developing countries in levels of attainment in education. In spite of these differences, however, a common feature recognized in respect of all educational systems is the limited role today of the traditional institution - the school. Because of the nature and complexity of today's world, the process of education is increasingly being cast within the moulds of wider schools of the community, the nation, the region and the universe.
10.25 In our view, this calls for new approaches to education on a global basis. In Trinidad and Tobago, these approaches include, first and foremost: a new orientation in educational planning and administration; greater use with wider scope of audio-visual aids; work/study programmes as an integral part of the curriculum; the development of effective services for career guidance and career education; an integrated approach toward teacher education; and a new emphasis on social studies to create a new awareness of man as a member of the human family.
10.26 My delegation notes with satisfaction that the Director-General has included in the Draft Programme and Budget for the forthcoming biennium a number of proposals for action in these important fields. We stress the need to give priority attention to such proposals.

14.29 Monsieur le Président, il est sans doute difficile de déterminer la place précise que l'Unesco doit occuper dans le monde d'aujourd'hui, car ses responsabilités sont multiples. Il lui revient en tout cas d'être l'un des centres intellectuels du monde, un lieu de rencontres, de dialogues et d'échanges de vues sur des problèmes fondamentaux de notre époque. Cela exige de l'Unesco une grande ouverture sur le monde, la création et le maintien de liens étroits non seulement avec les autorités gouvernementales des Etats membres mais aussi avec les milieux scientifiques et culturels, avec les universités et avec les artistes, avec les journalistes et les éducateurs. Ce n'est qu'ainsi qu'elle deviendra l'Unesco des peuples.
14.30 Nous savons que notre directeur général, M. M'Bow, se dévoue inlassablement à cet idéal. Nous lui en sommes profondément reconnaissants. Il est difficile de mesurer ce que l'Unesco et nos Etats membres doivent à cet homme, qui a su donner à notre Organisation un esprit nouveau et combien humain ; ses dons exceptionnels et sa puissance de conviction ont apporté à l'Unesco un précieux capital de sympathie. Inspirant la confiance, suscitant le dialogue, le Directeur général nous a rapprochés les uns des autres dans l'esprit de Nairobi, qui est l'esprit de la vraie coopéra- tion internationale. C'est sur cet hommage sincère à M. M'Bow, que le gouvernement suisse et la Commission nationale suisse pour l'Unesco ont eu le privilège d'accueillir récemment, que je tenais à terminer mon intervention. Merci, Monsieur le Président.

16.1 Mr. MAMBA (Swaziland): Mr. President, distinguished delegates, ladies and gentlemen: Allow me on behalf of the Swaziland delegation to congratulate Mr. LeBlanc on his election to the presidency of the twentieth session of the General Conference. We offer him our full support and co-operation in all his and his colleagues’ endeavours.
16.2 On the occasion of the admission of the Kingdom of Swaziland to full membership of Unesco, permit me, Mr. President, to express on behalf of its Government and people our profound appreci- ation of this act of friendship, which to us is no empty gesture. Unesco's dedication to the principles of human dignity, equality and justice is well known to us. And these same principles, we are glad to say, are enshrined in the traditions and custom of the Swazi nation and bind us irrevocably to this international community. Furthermore, those of us who live in Southern Africa know how difficult their implementation can be, and how jealously they should be guarded, but we draw inspiration from the determination of this world body to pursue them.
16.3 Permit me also to say that in our own way, we have, since recovery of our independence some ten years ago, striven to live up to these principles, and this in spite of our limited size and resources and notwithstanding the pressures of growth and change. To this end, we have made every effort to move away from the elitist system of education that we inherited from colonial times, while access to education and other agents of change has increased at an ever-accelerating pace.
16.4 Our primary school enrolments, for example, have risen from about 62,000 at the time of independence in 1968 to over- 100,000 at the end of the first decade of independence. We are deter- mined to maintain and accelerate this rate of access to schooling until all children of primary school age find a place in our schools by 1985. Our secondary school enrolments have shown similar trends of increase. From 31 secondary schools in 1968, with an enrolment of just over 6,000, we are now able to open the doors of 70 secondary schools to over 19,000 pupils, most of them in rural areas. Furthermore, we were able to establish university campuses in our country within the first three years of accession to independence, and the total capacity of the University in Swaziland now amounts to about 800 students. All this tells the story of our efforts to expand opportunities within the formal sector of education.
16.5 Like many other developing countries, however, we have sizeable populations of unschooled adults and young people, whom we are endeavouring to reach through a variety of programmes of non-formal education run by many departments of Government, para-State bodies, industry and voluntary agents, as well as through the self-help efforts of communities themselves. Our distance teaching-learning facilities include the use of radio. We are particularly conscious of the needs of the rural poor, and have increasingly focused attention on new strategies, incorporating the least developed zones of our country as "accelerating development areas" eligible for extra attention and resources. An Inter-Ministerial Development Committee has been set up to co-ordinate and spearhead efforts in these special areas, while rural education centres will provide additional training for young people leaving school. In this way, we hope to achieve an integrated approach to development. It is also our belief that priority in rural development must be accorded to human beings themselves by increasing their potential for participating in and directing change, and at the same time giving them the means to gain a fair share of the fruits of development and independence.
16.6 Mr. President, distinguished delegates, ladies and gentlemen: We mention these basic princi- ples of our development efforts because we wish you to know that we have benefited from our past association with this international community. Being among the youngest members of the Organization, we naturally look forward to many years of fruitful membership of Unesco. We look forward, more- over, to describing our own experience and to exercising our rights and freedoms within Unesco as well as to meeting our obligations under the Constitution.
16.7 Taking comfort from the sympathetic hearing which you, Mr. President and distinguished delegates, have already given to the Swaziland delegation, we are encouraged to mention at this stage some of our immediate problems and aspirations in the field of education and cultural development.
16.8 We are determined to ensure the continued and ever faster expansion of our education system. To this end, we are working on a number of projects for which we would be glad of further assistance from the international community. These include, firstly, increasing access to schooling at primary, secondary and university levels. For this purpose, the production of science and technical teachers is of particular importance; we are anxious to enable our University to offer full science degree courses, and also to strengthen courses at our college of technology. Secondly, we are reaching out to non-school populations through our functional literacy programmes (SEBENTA), correspondence programmes (SIEC), rural education centres, youth programmes, and the University department of extra-mural studies. And thirdly, we are working on the use of radio and television for educational purposes.
16.9 We also seek continued improvement in the quality of our education. To this end, we have devised a number of projects which also require further assistance. These include: curriculum development units at secondary and primary school levels; in-service teacher education programmes at college and university; teacher education for personnel in the non-formal education sector based on our university; and management education for our educational administrators at all levels.
16.10 Moreover, as our system of education expands, we wish to see a lowering of the unit costs of
752      our education. We have therefore established a special schools building unit to experiment with 20 low-cost construction. Preliminary results are encouraging and we wish to see an expansion and an extension of research and experimentation to cover all aspects that affect the cost of education.
16.11 Lastly, and above all, we wish to increase the capacity of our system to innovate and to monitor the progress of innovation efforts. To this end, we wish to sponsor the establishment of a centre for spearheading educational innovation and development and to provide mechanism for monitoring and evaluating the progress of innovation.
16.12 Mr. President, we are confident that the opportunity accorded to us by virtue of admission to full membership of Unesco will enable us to receive the ready assistance of the Organization and its Member States. We therefore take our seat as full members in gratitude and hope. Thank you, Mr. President.
