27. Statement by the Swiss observer  concerning Switzerland’s accession to  the Organization.  The PRESIDENT : I call upon the observer for  Switzerland, Professor Piaget, to make a state-  ment.  Professor Jean PIAGET (Switzerland) (Trans-  lation) Mr. President, ladies and gentlemen,  the Swiss delegation has great pleasure in infor-  ming you that it has just received a telegram from  M. Petitpierre of the Federal Council stating that  the Federal Parliament of Switzerland has ap-  proved the adherence of Switzerland to Unesco.  We have often been asked the reasons for our  slowness in joining Unesco. But the notion of  speed is purely relative, and in this matter the  machinery has moved at the Swiss pace : slowly  and surely. The apparent delay was due solely  to the complexities of parliamentary procedure;  our Federal Council voted unanimously for our  joining Unesco. That adherence will be warmly  welcomed in Switzerland both by intellectual  circles and by the general public, who have felt  from the beginning that there was a relationship  between the ideal of Unesco and the ideal of Swit-  zerland—to ensure co-operation between various  cultures despite the difficulties of language, creed  and political ideology. Here in Lebanon, our host  country this year, which is as familiar as we are  with the problem of diversity in civilizations, there  is no need to stress this ideal.  Since the preliminary work and the meetings  of the Preparatory Commission in London, our  country has followed with the greatest interest the  development of Unesco. It has sent observers and
154  has established ever closer technical contacts with  you, through various organizations associated with  Unesco : the International Council of Scientific  Unions, the International Bureau of Education,  the International Theatre Institute, etc. It offi-  cially asked for admission to the Paris Conference  and was greatly touched at the favourable reception  given to it in Mexico City. We now intend, ladies  and gentlemen, to co-operate with you as continu-  ously and effectively as possible. Our scientific  and literary circles expect much from you and  will be very struck by the spirit of constructive  criticism which has found expression during this  Conference. Our educationists, too, who are  proud of their long tradition, are well aware that  they have everything to gain from continuous inter-  national co-operation, and that in education, more  than in any other field, each benefits from the  experience of others.  The PRESIDENT : On behalf of the General  Conference, I have the greatest pleasure in wel-  coming Switzerland to the Organization. Swit-  zerland, I am told, becomes the forty-fifth State  to ratify the Constitution of Unesco. Switzerland,  as we all know, has long adhered to the principles  which the Constitution of Unesco embodies. We  look forward to the very early deposit of ratifica-  tions, which I am told will take place in a day  or two, and we again extend our warmest wel-  come to Switzerland.
28. Report of the Joint Committee of  Fifteen.  The PRESIDENT : The next item on the agenda  is the discussion of the Report of the Joint         Committee of Fifteen. I call upon Mr. Davies of  Australia to present the Report on behalf of the  Rapporteur of that Committee.  Mr. Gardner DAVIES (Australia) : Mr. Presi-  dent, the Report of the Joint Committee of Fifteen  is set out in document 3C/94, distributed yesterday  in English and French. Three general questions  of vital importance to Unesco were assigned to  the Committee.  The first was that of Unesco’'s function as a  world centre for the exchange of information.  The part of the Committee's Report relating to  this question has already been submitted to the  plenary Conference, when the question of the  world centre was discussed.  The second general question before the Com-  mittee of Fifteen was that of Unesco’s policy and  programme with regard to publications. The  Committee devoted a great deal of time to discus-  sing with the Secretariat problems of the financing  and distribution of Unesco’s publications, and its  conclusions are embodied in a series of recom-  mendations which are before you.  The other question assigned to the Committee  was the vastly important one of public infor-  mation. The Committee was unanimous in its  feeling that adequate public information services  are essential to the very life of the Organization.  It has therefore made in this respect, too, a series  of recommendations aiming in particular at widen-  ing the appeal and circulation of Unesco’s Courier.  I have the honour, Mr. President, to present to  the Conference the Report of the Committee of  Fifteen. I ask the Chairman of the Committee  of Fifteen to comment on this Report.  Mrs. Louise WRIGHT (United States of Ame-  rica) (Chairman of the Joint Committee of Fif-  teen) : Mr. President, I should like first of all,  on behalf of the United States delegation, to thank  the members of the Committee of Fifteen for the  very thoughtful consideration they have given to  the questions which were entrusted to them. The  Committee was set up on the proposal of the  United States delegation because of our conviction  that, if the objective of Unesco, namely peace  through understanding, is to be achieved, it must  be, first of all, by letting the peoples of the world  know what Unesco is and, secondly, by letting  them know so much that they will want to carry  its work to success.  The present clientele of Unesco, in terms of  those who are vitally interested and can implement  the programme, is confined first of all to those  of us who are fortunate enough to be at the Con-  ference—numerically, of course, infinitesimal  groups. Then there are the members of the
155  National Commissions, a larger group but still a  limited one. We must therefore seek other ways  of interesting the peoples of the world. It seems  to us, the United States delegation—and I am glad  to see that the Committee of Fifteen agrees—that  the publication of the programme of Unesco 1s one  of the most important ways in which this can be  done. Many people judge Unesco exclusively by  its publications. As the deliberations of the Com-  mittee brought out, that list of publications 1s, to  date, brief—insignificant, some would say; and  even those publications which are already in print  are not distributed throughout the world. The  Committee therefore made several recommen-  dations.  It was aware that at no time had the General  Conference of Unesco considered the publications  programme as a whole. We had a list compiled  for our Committee of the publications proposed  for the coming year. It amounts to about fifty  titles, and entails a contemplated expenditure, for  printing only, of about $270,000. But there 1s  no correlation of this programme as a whole with  the priority set by the Conference and the desire  of the Conference to put forward certain special  projects. We therefore recommend that at the  next General Conference the delegates be provided  with a complete record of such publications as  have already been issued, together with an account  of their distribution, and that the publications pro-  posed for the subsequent year be also set before  the Conference, so that there will be an oppor-  tunity for delegates to decide whether this is the  way in which they want the people of the world  to learn about Unesco.  We were very much impressed by the fact that  the distribution of Unesco’s publications 1s meagre  and in some cases non-existent. One explanation  for this is that so little money is available, and  the editions which have been published are so  small, that there are not enough copies for distri-  bution. For instance, the Director-General's Re-  port, which seems to us to be a desirable vehicle  for acquainting the peoples of the world with  Unesco, was printed in such a small edition that  at the end of this Conference there will not be  copies available to distribute throughout the  world. We therefore recommend, as one device  for increasing the size of editions, the establish-  ment of a publications revolving fund, in which  the revenue from the sale of publications would  be deposited.  We also recommend that the normal channels  of distribution be supplemented by National Com-  missions, that they assist the Unesco Secretariat  in devising ways and means in which to have the  publications distributed in their respective coun-  tries. We thought that, if these suggestions were  carried out, the Publications Programme of Unesco           would become clearer and more persuasive to the  peoples of the world.  In talking about public information, as the Rap-  porteur has already stated, we felt that it was  essential that this part of Unesco’s activities should  be carried out with the greatest skill. Some of  us have to go home to newspapers which say that  the taxpayers are wasting their money on Unesco.  We would like to have sufficient material in the  press and radio, in periodicals and various other  media, to explain to people what Unesco is. We  are well aware that it is all too easy to make fun  of Unesco, as our press often does; and Unesco 1s  a difficult venture to explain satisfactorily. There-  fore we think we need all the skill available in  the Public Information Bureau. We were sur-  prised, on the Committee, to find that the staff  is so small, but we were told that there are other  sections of the Unesco Secretariat which could be  used to help carry out this programme, and we  have forwarded proposals to that effect.  We are agreed that the Courier is, at the mo-  ment, the best possible vehicle for public infor-  mation purposes, and we are glad to note that the  recommendation that there be a larger editorial  appropriation for the Courier has already been  adopted by the Conference. Other recommenda-  tions you will see in the Report; some of them  have been considered and approved, so I doubt  if it is necessary to go through them in detail.  [ would, however, be very glad to answer questions  about any of the specific resolutions.  May I say, finally, that the United States dele-  gation is in accord with the findings of the Com-  mittee. I take pleasure in moving that the Report  be adopted and forwarded to the Director-General  with the request that its recommendations be  carried out as rapidly as possible. Joseph ABOUKHATER (Lebanon) (Trans-  lation) : Mr. President, ladies and gentlemen, as  regards this question of language, I want to say  at once that the Arab delegates reject the draît  amendment put forward by the Danish delegation.  I will inform you of the reasons. The Arab draft  resolution was as follows :  The delegations of the Arab States propose that  Arabic be considered a working language as regards  the translation of important documents, publications  and books issued by Unesco. The Arab States gua-  rantee to take upon themselves the translation, at  their own expense, of those documents, publications  and books which they consider suitable and in the  interest of Unesco to disseminate among Arab-speaking  peoples, provided Unesco bears the costs of printing  and distribution.  Before going on, 1 leave it to you to say whether  this text is either exaggerated or too modest. We  do not claim to make Arabic a full working lan-  guage in Unesco or elsewhere. We fully under-  stand the practical difficulties that such a proposal  would raise. Once again, I appeal to the whole  Conference to recognize that we have never held  up the work by speaking in Arabic, in spite of  our undeniable right to do so. Yet, against our  modest proposal, and particularly the undertaking        e e   Mau  R e i s   i  Ee   AS  t n   E m   E E   en   E R   a  i  a E   A   dE   ET   E E E     of the Arab States to contribute a large part of  the expenses, we note that the Danish amendment  proposes $20,000 a year for translation, printing  and distribution in “languages other than the  working languages of Unesco”. This, gentlemen, is  unacceptable to us. Our proposal, we repeat, 1s  modest and extremely clear. We have offered,  and we offer again, to contribute in the largest  possible measure, even exceeding $20,000 or  $30,000, to the translation of documents into  Arabic, if the Organization will bear the rest of  the expenses; but in no circumstances can the  Arab delegations accept the Danish amendment.
