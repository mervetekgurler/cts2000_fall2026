3C/PRO/7, Annexes IV and V) (continuation).  The CHAIRMAN proposed reverting to consider-  ation of Rules 51 to 56 of the draft Rules of Pro-  cedure of the General Conference (document
3C/PRO/1), together with the proposals for the  adoption of Spanish as a working language. The  documents relating to that question were : a draft  resolution submitted by the Director-General (doc-  ument 3C/PRO/5, page 13), a draft resolution sub-  mitted by the United States delegation (document
3C/PRO/12), a draft resolution by eight Latin  American countries (document 3C/PRO/7, An-  nex V), a draft resolution by the Lebanese delega-  tion (document 3C/PRO/14), and a new proposal  submitted in typescript by the delegations of Latin  America. after consultation with the United States  delegation.  M. ABOUKHATER (Lebanon) feared that there  was some confusion about the use of Arabic (or  any other language) as a working language. He  reminded the meeting that the question had been  settled by the Mexico City Conference on 3 De-  cember 1947 and that Arabic had been accepted as  one of Unesco’s official languages (document
2C/132, Vol. I, page 203). Under the terms of  the resolution unanimously adopted by the Mexico  Conference, a request had only to be put to the  Director-General for Arabic to be accepted as an  official language of Unesco, and on 23 February
1948 Dr. El Diwany (Egypt) had made such a  request. Arabic was therefore indisputably one  of the official languages of Unesco.  The question under discussion was the use of  Arabic as a working language, if only in the qua-  lified or attenuated form proposed in the Lebanese  delegation’s amendment of the day before. Arabic  had been adopted at Beirut as a working language,  but it was to be noted that no delegate of the  Arabic-speaking countries had spoken in Arabic;  that was, of course, in order to speed up the work  of the Conference. If the Lebanese delegation  pressed for the acceptance of the proposal con-  tained in document 3C/PRO/14, it was not in a  chauvinistic spirit, but in order the better to  disseminate Unesco’s ideals. It should be re-  membered that Arabic was spoken by 70,000,000  people and that Unesco had five Member  States whose official language was Arabic. Until  the world spoke a single language, the language  of the heart and of reason, it was in the interests  of Unesco and of the delegations that everyone  should speak his own language.  Dr. del RIO y CANEDO (Mexico) wished to  clarify the position as it then stood. There were  not two draft resolutions from the Latin Amer-  ican delegations, but only one, which had been  submitted in typescript and drafted after consul-  tation with the head of the United States dele-  gation. That new text, which replaced the  original proposal, took account of Unesco's  budgetary resources and also of the desire to  ensure success for the Organization's ideas and  ideals.  The CHAIRMAN read the typed copy of the text  mentioned by the delegate of Mexico.  Rule 51 : Working languages.  (a) English, French and Spanish may be used in  oral deliberations of the General Conference. All  documents and records, as well as the Journal of the  Conference, shall be issued in French and English. (b) The Director-General is authorized to publish  in Spanish. within the limitations of the budget and  in consultation with the specialized Commissions, the  documents which he may deem most important or  necessary for the most effective diffusion of the  objectives and ideals of Unesco among Spanish-speak-  ing peoples. Mr. ALLEN (United States of America) agreed  with the substance of the draft submitted by the  Latin American delegations. The American dele-  gation was most anxious that the twelve Spanish-  speaking delegations attending the General Confer-  ence should be able to take a fuller part in the  discussions by using their own language; it  wished to be sure, however, that Spanish was to  be used only at meetings where simultaneous  interpretation was employed, as the discussions  would be unduly prolonged if more than two  languages were used with consecutive interpre-  tation.  M. SEYDOUX (France) recalled that, at Mexico  City, the question of the adoption of Spanish as  a working language had been very fully consi-  dered, in an atmosphere where feeling often ran  high. At the current Session, it had been proved,  at the previous days meeting, that it was pos-  sible to resume the discussion in a calmer and less  ruffled atmosphere. Negotiations had been going  on and the result was embodied in the draft sub-  mitted jointly by the Latin American and United  States delegations, which was in truth a Latin  American proposal.  So far, no speaker had really refuted the argu-  ments advanced by the Secretariat against the  adoption of Spanish as a working language,  except, perhaps, as far as a few financial points  were concerned. Any delegation which might  wish to maintain the status quo could thus  simply repeat the Secretariat’s very strong argu-  ments. France would not adopt that attitude,  however; France wished to consider the question  from the point of view of the advocates of Spanish,  and to examine the cultural (rather than the  budgetary) arguments put forward in support of  the Latin American contention. As all question  of prestige had been left out of the argument (the  speech by the Colombian delegate was a striking  proof of that fact), the discussion was continuing  on Unesco’s own ground, i.e., in the world-wide  community of all men of goodwill. There was  thus no question of drawing comparisons between  the Spanish and other civilizations; no language  could boast a monopoly of literary masterpieces  or valuable contributions to civilization, and a  debate on that subject would be fruitless. An-  other important argument had been put forward,  namely, that 200,000,000 people spoke Spanish.  What, then, was the position of Arabic, Chinese,  Russian or Hindustani? Nations were represented  in Unesco only by their delegates, who were  highly cultured men, most of whom could  express themselves fluently in one of the two  working languages and sometimes in both; in  fact, the speeches of the advocates whom the  Spanish-speaking world had instructed to put  forward the case for Spanish as a working lan-  guage, suggested that the speakers had been  almost too well chosen. The Syrian delegate had  expressed the wish that delegations should be able  to speak in their own languages; but were there  not grounds for fear that, if that were so, Unesco  might really become a Tower of Babel?  The French delegation had come to the con-  clusion that the Director-General had stated the  question very well at the previous day s meeting :  the point at issue was whether Unesco's work  would or would not be facilitated by adopting a  new language for purposes of “exposition”. The  French delegation was inclined to support the  draft submitted jointly by the Latin American  and United States delegations. However, it would  like to see it in writing, and to know, firstly, what  would happen to the other proposals (particularly  the proposal for the adoption of Arabic as a  working language for some purposes) and, sec-  ondly, the financial implications of the reso-  lution (and especially what contributions would  be made by the Member States concerned towards  the additional cost entailed by it). In connexion  with the last matter, the Committee would be  well advised to ask the Budget Sub-Commission  to prepare an estimate; when the Committee had  all the necessary data, it would be in a position  to reach a decision. The delegate of France hoped  that that decision would then be unanimous.  Fuad Bey HAMZI (Saudi Arabia) remarked that  the question whether Arabic was an official or a  working language had apparently been left aside  for the time being; he reserved the right to  return to it on an appropriate occasion.  In reply to the French delegate, M. FLORES  (Venezuela) first emphasized that Spanish-  speaking delegations attending the General Confer-  ence brought the world their cultural and scienti-  fic wealth and, in return, took back other assets  which the rest of the world could offer them.  All those delegations were asking was that dele-  gates at the Conference should be allowed to use  Spanish, which was their common bond. If  Unesco was indeed endeavouring to bring the  nations together, it was essential that delegates  to the General Conference should not be only  professional diplomats, but that specialists on  education, science and culture, who could gen-  erally express themselves only in their native lan-  guage, should be included. As far as Europeans
463               were concerned, French was almost a second  language, but for Latin Americans, Spanish was  the only tongue.  With regard to the decision to be given in  connexion with Arabic, M. Flores reminded the  meeting that the only question under consi-  deration at the moment was that of Spanish.  Arabic was to be an official language of Unesco  and it had been treated as a working language  during the Conference at Beirut, in accordance  with the tradition that the language of the  couniry in which the General Conference was  held should become a working language tem-  porarily. For the time being, the question to be  considered was the adoption of Spanish as a  working language, and the delegation of Venezuela  would be glad if the Director-General would  explain his objections to that adoption.  The CHAIRMAN said that it was not out of place  to discuss Arabic when the Committee was  dealing with Rule 51 of the Rules of Pro-  cedure, concerning working languages. With  regard to the French delegate’s request that the  matter should be referred to the Budget Sub-  Commission, the Chairman explained that the  Committee had to report to the General Confer-  ence, and that its report would automatically be  transmitted via the Budget Sub-Commission.  Dr. SAGLAM (Turkey) was convinced that, if  Spanish, and possibly also Arabic, were to be  adopted as working languages, the precedent  would be sure to encourage China, India, and  possibly Russia and Germany to ask for their  national languages to be treated in the same way.  It was unnecessary to prove that the resulting cost  to Unesco would be enormous and that the Gen-  eral Conference, a very Tower of Babel, would  have no alternative to sitting continuously.  It was advisable to clarify the significance of  the term “working language” : a working lan-  guage was used for conferences and for a body  of publications, most of which were not intended  for the general public. Three years’ experience  had proved that the two existing working lan-  guages were quite enough for the meetings of  the General Conference and the permanent organs  of Unesco. The dissemination of Unesco’s ideas  in the various countries was a task for the  Member States and their National Commissions  far more than for the Secretariat, which had  other things to do. Looking at the question from  the practical and utilitarian standpoint, 1t was  desirable that there should be only one working  language; that implied that no other working  language should be added to the two already  employed in Unesco.  Mr. THOMAS (United Kingdom) proposed that  the Committee should first consider the question  of Spanish. On the whole, the British delegation  agreed with the proposal submitted by the dele-  gations of Latin America and the United States,  but it considered that paragraph (b) would have  repercussions on the budget, and, while it would  accept the Chairman’s ruling, the delegation of  the United Kingdom would like the Budget Sub-  Commission to submit its conclusions to a small  drafting committee appointed by the Procedure  Committee.  The CHAIRMAN thought the arrangement  feasible. He also suggested separating the ques-  tion of Spanish and Arabic, unless the delegate of  Lebanon objected. Lastly, the Chairman recalled  that, in addition to the Latin American proposal,  one had been put forward by the Syrian dele-  gation.  Dr. SALIBA (Syria) withdrew his proposal in  favour of the Latin American proposal. However,  he urged that Arabic should be given the same  consideration as Spanish.  The DIRECTOR-GENERAL reminded the  meeting that the adoption of Spanish as a working  language would have the budgetary implications  mentioned in document 3C/PRO/5, pages 9 to 12,  according to each of the three hypotheses consi-  dered. If Arabic were added as a working lan-  guage for the translation of the main documents,  the additional expenditure would amount to about  $43,000 a year. It should finally be noted that  the adoption of another working language auto-  matically necessitated three times as many trans-  lators (instead of an English-French and French-  English service, there would have to be  English-French, French-English, English-Spanish,  French-Spanish, Spanish-French and Spanish-  English services); there would be a progressive  increase with the adoption of every new working  language.  Professor CARNEIRO (Brazil) stated that the  problem of working languages was not the  special concern of any one delegation or group of  delegations represented at the General Conference,  but of the Organization as a whole. The dele-  gates to the General Conference bore a message  from their peoples and the language in which  they expressed themselves was not so important as  the message which that assembly should bring  to all the peoples of the world on the completion  of its work. If the Conference adopted new  working languages, there would always be  countries whose language would not be a working  language and whose delegates would have to

Brazil (which,  incidentally, had not been consulted by the  delegations of Latin America) represented a good  half of Latin America, yet it would never ask for  Portuguese to become a working language of  Unesco, for Brazil wished to observe strictly the  duties imposed by international work. The Bra-  zilian delegation had voted for Mexico City as the  seat of the Second Session of the General Confer-  ence; it had voted for a Director-General who  came from Latin America; and it had voted for  Beirut as the seat of the Third Session of the  Conference; that implied that it had wished to  pay tribute to Spanish and, equally, to Arabic  culture, to which Spanish civilization owed so  much. In all friendliness, Professor Carneiro  therefore asked his Latin American and Arabic  friends to abide by the status quo; everyone  would benefit from their sacrifice, since, unless  a collective sacrifice were made in favour of work  in common, there was no way of avoiding com-  plete failure, due to the multiplicity of languages  in a new Babel. The delegate of Brazil would vote  for the siatus quo,  Dr. del RIO y CANEDO (Mexico) wished to  make it clear that the Latin American countries  had all due respect for the great civilizations of  the world. whether Chinese, Indian, Arabic or  Russian. He added that it was not the first time  that the question of the adoption of Spanish as  a working language had been raised at the Gen-  eral Conference and that, if its adoption were  rejected at the current Conference, it would not  be the last time. Unesco was not reserved for  diplomats; what the General Conference needed  was leaders in education, science and culture, and,  to secure them, it was essential that they should  be able to express themselves easily and therefore  use their own language. In reply to Professor Carneiro, the delegate of  Mexico assured him that Brazil was never over-  looked in any matter concerning Latin America,  and he reminded him of his informal conversation  with him the day before.  Returning to the Latin American proposal, the  delegate of Mexico showed that it was not a  compromise solution, but took into account the  realistic views which should be adopted by the  General Conference. (1) Arabic and Spanish; (2) the question of the  official and the working language. Mr. ALLEN (United States of America) wished  to make it clear that, by accepting the proposal  of the delegate of Mexico, the delegation of the  United States of America recognized that many  delegations were unable to take a full part in the  discussions of the Conference, as they could not  speak English or French fluently. However, the  American delegation emphasized that it could not  consider that proposal as equivalent to the adop-  tion of Spanish as a full working language.  Moreover, the text proposed was very clear in that  respect : English and French remained the only  full working languages of Unesco.  Count Stefano JACINI (Italy) felt that the  question of principle and prestige was solved by  having Spanish and Arabic recognized as official  languages. Italian was also included among the  official languages. It was agreed that the lan-  guage of the country where the General Confer-  ence was being held should become temporarily a  working language of Unesco. The question was  accordingly brought down to the practical and  budgetary plane and, from that point of view, the  delegate of Italy fully agreed with the opinion  expressed by the delegate of Brazil.
