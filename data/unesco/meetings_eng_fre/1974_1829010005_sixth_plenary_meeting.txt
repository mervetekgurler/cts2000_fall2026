29,4. Madam President, Bangladesh - à new nation on the political map of the world - has a rich  and resplendent cultural legacy. The innumerable rivers and rivulets meandering across the  alluvial plains and meadows have endowed this land of 75:million people with an idyllic beauty. .
111   Tha people have clung fast to their cultural and ideological mooring since time immemorial, yet  they received with open arms whatever they found to be best in the cultural waves that swept the  reglen during two thousand years and more.
29.5 We have archaeological evidence of a flourishing Buddhist éulture in’the seventh century and  also other evidence of a Hindu culture. During the Muslim rule sincere attempts were made to  bring about a fusion of all the cultural nuances which echoed the pulsation of the soil, Bengaltraded  with Aftab, Persians, Greeks, Portuguese, Danes, Dutch,” A¥mentans, French, British and Span-  fards. We gratefully acknow-  ledge the help and assistance which Bangladesh has received from the international community and  also from this great Organization, and we are still receiving help from many friendly nations inthe  economic and social rebuilding of the country. Unesco will, Madam President, be of great assist-  ance in this task of reconstruction.
29, 7 Bangladesh is interested in the establishment of an interdisciplinary applied research body  devoted to the permanent study of the Interactions between the land, the rivers, the sea and the  human activities of the Bengal'delta, as a basis for development planning and protection against  natural hazards. 29.9 Madam President, no educational programme can be complete unless it pays attention to the  problems of thé young. Unemployed youth, like the floating log beaten by waves,  portends great dangers to society and reduces it at times to an arena of harmful convulsicns.
29.10 Madam: President, the Director- General's report (document 18 c/32) on the desirability of  adopting one or more protocols to the agreement on the import of educational, scientific and cul- .  tural materials, will attempt to modify to some extent the Beirut Agreement of 1948 and the  Florence Agreement of 1950. The adoption of these additional protocols will certainly facilitate  the dissemination of ideas across frontiers between the developed and the developing countries.  The-developing nations at the moment are the victims of world-wide inflation, -and their financial .  resources have everywhere been mobilized to ensure the minimum economic survival of the peo-  ple. The recommendations for the liberalization of tariff restrictions were made at a time when  the world was not beset by the present economic crisis. - The protocols under reference basically  aim; firstly, ‘at liburelization of import duties on other Internal taxes, and secondly at the alloca-  tion of adequate foreign exchange for the import of cultural, educational and sclentific materials  for libraries and other public institutions engaged In educational and cultural activity. While it  may not be very difficult to cut down import duties on such materials, I am sceptical about the  ability of the developing countries to allocate foreign exchange liberally for importing educational,  cultural and sclentific materials, in view of thelr depleted foreign exchange resources. In sucha  situation the countries having export potential for these materials can greatly help the countries at  the receiving end by subtantially reducing the cost of those materials, or by accepting a nominal  price from the déveloping nations. By this generous gesture, the developed nations will not only
112   help the developing ones to tide over the present economio imbalance, but will also help to over-  come the communication gap in the field of advanced technology between the developed and the  underdeveloped. Access to knowledge should be as little costly as possible, in view of the un-  favourable balance of payment position of most of the developing nations,
