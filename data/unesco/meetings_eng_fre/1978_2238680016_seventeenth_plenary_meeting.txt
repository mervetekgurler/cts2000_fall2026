Seventeenth plenary meeting
3 November 1978 at 10,10 a, m, President: Mr. LEBLANC (Canada) Dix-septième séance plénière
1. Le PRESIDENT.- Mesdames, Messieurs, je déclare ouverte la dix-neuviéme séance plénière. J'invite M. Gaëtano Adinolfi, secrétaire général adjoint du Conseil de l'Europe, à prendre la parole.
2.1 M. ADINOLFI (Conseil de l'Europe).- Monsieur le Président, Excellences, Monsieur le Directeur général, Mesdames, Mesdemoiselles, Messieurs, c'est avec vive satisfaction que je tiens, avant tout, à rappeler les liens de collaboration noués en 1952 entre les secrétariats de l'Unesco et du Conseil de l'Europe, liens qui se sont depuis lors resserrés au bénéfice, je crois, des deux organisations. Ces relations se sont concrétisées par des rencontres au niveau politique et par des réunions périodiques intersecrétariats, ainsi que par la participation d'observateurs aux réunions les plus importantes organisées par nos deux institutions. Les questions d'intérêt commun sont ainsi examinées à la faveur de ces contacts réguliers qui per- mettent une meilleure connaissance de nos programmes respectifs.
2.2 Une telle coopération paraît d'autant plus opportune que la Convention européenne des droits de l'homme, fondement essentiel de l'institution de Strasbourg que je représente, englobe les droits civils et politiques consacrés par la Déclaration universelle des droits de l'homme qui a été adoptée par les Nations Unies et qui doit guider l'action de l'Unesco. Les deux organisations s'inspirent ainsi de principes similaires, bien que leur application ait été différente. Le système de garantie judiciaire internationale des droits de l'homme établi par la Convention européenne constitue en effet un exemple unique de mise en oeuvre effective desdits principes au niveau régional. Le Conseil de l'Europe, dans un cadre plus restreint que celui de l'Unesco, apporte ainsi une contribution exemplaire et unique à la communauté internationale en matière de droits de l'homme.
2.3 Récemment, deux nouveaux Etats européens ont adhéré au Conseil de l'Europe et à la Con- vention européenne des droits de l'homme. Peut-on en déduire que la conception politique et juri- dique des libertés fondamentales qui est propre à nos Etats membres exerce un pouvoir d'attraction toujours grandissant ? Seul l'avenir apportera une réponse à cette question ; mais pour l'instant il n'est nas vain d'espérer que les divergences entre les hommes puissent s'estomper et que tout ce qui peut faciliter leur compréhension réciproque sorte renforcé de la présente session de la Conférence générale.
2.4 Par-delà les droits civils et politiques, le Conseil de l'Europe se propose aussi d'étudier les méthodes et les moyens permettant d'assurer à tous l'exercice effectif des droits sociaux et culturels. A cet égard, M. Kahn-Ackermann, secrétaire général du Conseil de l'Europe, retenu cette semaine en Grèce, m'a demandé de vous redire toute l'importance qu'il attache à une collaboration de nos deux organisations en ce domaine.
2.5 En réunissant tous les deux ans une Conférence des ministres européens de l'éducation, dont la dernière en date s'est tenue à Strasbourg en 1977, ainsi que des conférences des ministres euro- péens des affaires culturelles dont la deuxième a eu lieu à Athènes le mois passé, le Conseil de l'Europe n'a nullement adopté la voie du repli sur soi. Nombreux sont les exemples, dans les tra- vaux de ces deux conférences, d'orientation vers le monde extérieur, c'est-à-dire vers le monde de l'Unesco : problèmes de l'éducation et de la culture aux niveaux préscolaire, primaire, secondaire, technique, supérieur (ou encore en ce qui concerne les enfants des travailleurs migrants), problèmes du développement culturel, dans le cadre général du système d'éducation permanente. Notre Orga- nisation attache le plus grand prix aux travaux de ces deux conférences, dont il assure le Secrétariat et met en oeuvre les résolutions qui sont de sa compétence.
2.6 Il est particulièrement important de souligner que nos relations avec l'Unesco se placent non seulement au niveau gouvernemental, mais aussi sur le plan parlementaire. C'est très certainement sous ce dernier aspect que notre collaboration touche le plus directement le public et peut avoir le plus grand rayonnement. Des membres du Secrétariat de 1'Unesco sont, naturellement, invités a des réunions de la Commission parlementaire de la culture et de l'éducation, en fonction bien entendu des questions figurant à l'ordre du jour.
2.7 C'est ainsi, par exemple, que donnant suite à la résolution 636 de l'Assemblée du Conseil de l'Europe relative à la sauvegarde du patrimoine architectural d'Istanbul, l'Unesco a envoyé deux architectes en mission dans cette ville.
2.8 Le Président de notre Assemblée et ses collègues parlementaires attachent une grande impor- tance au fait que des représentants hautement qualifiés de l'Unesco assistent aux débats de notre assemblée. Cette participation permet, en effet, des échanges de vues d'autant plus bénéfiques qu'ils permettent des appréciations critiques, et donc parfois divergentes, de certaines réalités politiques.
2.9 La coopération existe aussi - et je devrais peut-être dire d'abord - entre nos deux secrétariats En cette époque de difficultés budgétaires et de concentration des efforts, un examen comparatif, méthodique et approfondi des programmes des deux organisations s'est avéré indispensable.
2.10 Il est dans les intentions du Secrétaire général du Conseil de l'Europe de poursuivre ses efforts dans ce sens et de rechercher partout où cela est possible, les moyens d'une coopération, voire d'une coordination, avec l'Unesco, en particulier dans le cadre de la mise en oeuvre de nos deux plans à moyen terme qui couvrent sensiblement la même période. Cet effort parallèle de plani- fication est déjà en soi une invitation à une coopération plus poussée. Pour sa part, le Conseil de l'Europe continuera à convier l'Unesco aux réunions susceptibles de présenter un intérêt pour l'exé- cution de son programme. Dans le même esprit, le Secrétaire général s'efforcera d'accepter les invi- tations qu'il recevra à se faire représenter à des réunions de l'Unesco.
2.11 C'est avec le plus vif intérêt que le Conseil de l'Europe a pris note de l'impressionnant pro- gramme de l'Unesco pour les années à venir, dans le domaine des droits de l'homme. L'objectif du Conseil de l'Europe, je tiens à le souligner, est avant tout la défense et le progrès des droits de l'homme et des libertés fondamentales, conçus comme la base et le moteur d'un régime parlementaire démocratique. Dans cette perspective, le Conseil de l'Europe est prêt à apporter toute sa contribu- tion à l'Unesco chaque fois qu'il s'agira de développer la connaissance et l'enseignement d'une telle conception des droits de l'homme. L'action du Conseil de l'Europe dans ce domaine tend à faire bénéficier l'individu d'une protection internationale par la voie d'une requête auprès d'organismes internationaux. C'est pourquoi le Conseil de l'Europe suit avec un intérêt vigilant les efforts de l'Unesco visant à l'adoption d'une nouvelle déclaration sur la race et les préjugés raciaux.
2.12 Dans le domaine de la protection du patrimoine culturel, je voudrais citer la participation de l'Unesco aux activités de la Fondation Pro Venetia Viva, créée à l'initiative du Conseil de l'Europe, tant par la présence d'un représentant de son Directeur général à ses travaux que par l'attribution de bourses pour le Centre européen de formation d'artisans pour la protection du patrimoine architectural.
2.13 Vous me permettrez en terminant d'évoquer les possibilités de coopération dans le domaine du sport tant au niveau du Secrétariat qu'entre les organes récemment créés ou en voie de création : d'une part, le Comité intergouvernemental de l'Unesco pour l'éducation physique et le sport et le Fonds de l'Unesco pour le développement de l'éducation physique et du sport, d'autre part, le Co- mité de développement du sport du Conseil de l'Europe.
2.14 Suivant en cela sa mission statutaire qui est de situer la promotion de la coopération entre ses Etats membres sous le signe du respect des droits de l'homme et des libertés fondamentales, le Conseil de l'Europe poursuit ses expériences sur les interactions entre la culture et les médias. Il a également créé sous l'autorité du Comité des ministres un Comité d'experts gouvernementaux chargé d'examiner l'ensemble des problèmes actuels concernant les moyens de communication de masse et d'élaborer des lignes directrices qui pourront guider la politique des Etats membres à l'égard des médias. Ce vaste exercice de prospection, d'étude et de recherche d'un consensus sur les grands défis du monde contemporain et de l'avenir dans le domaine de la communication sociale s'inspire du.
640    concept traditionnel mais toujours bien vivant de la liberté de l'information, de l'autonomie et de l'indépendance des médias à l'égard de l'Etat. Il s'inspire aussi de la nécessité de garantir sans réserve la pluralité des sources d'information et partant la pluralité des opinions et des idées. Ce concept et sa mise en oeuvre constituent l'une des cellules vitales de la démocratie pluraliste au ser- vice tant de la défense de l'individu contre toute domination que de son plein épanouissement dans la société.
2.15 En vous remerciant, Monsieur le Président, de m'avoir offert la possibilité d'esquisser ce tour d'horizon sur la coopération entre nos deux organisations, je tiens à vous exprimer nos voeux les plus chaleureux pour le succès de cette vingtième session de la Conférence générale afin que l'uni- versalisme de la liberté et du droit puisse devenir le plus précieux dénominateur commun de l'Unesco et du Conseil de l'Europe.
3. Le PRESIDENT .- Je remercie le Secrétaire général adjoint du Conseil de l'Europe et je donne maintenant la parole à S. Exc. M. Luis Niemann Nuñez, ministre de l'éducation et chef de la délégation du Chili.
(4.1) Mr. NIEMANN NUÑEZ (Chile)(Translation from the Spanish): Mr. President; this twentieth session of the General Conference of Unesco brings the Organization together once again at its Headquarters in Paris. The choice of France for the Headquarters of the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, gave due recognition to that country's historical mission in the world of culture. For France has indeed played a decisive historical role in developing the universal culture which unites all of humanity around a set of ideals and values, which are precisely those ideals and values which Unesco has made its own. Today France is continuing, under the distinguished presidency of Mr. Valéry Giscard d'Estaing, to fulfil the cultural mission it assumed centuries ago. On behalf of the Government and the people of Chile, closely linked by their institutions and by their forms of expression to the heritage and the cultural mission of France, I wish to pay tribute to the country which is the seat of Unesco.
(4.2) We also warmly welcome the election to the presidency of this Conference of a man who is excep- tionally well-versed in the structure and functions of Unesco, having served on its Executive Board, and who is also a prominent figure in the world of education, science, culture and communication: the eminent Vice-Rector of the University of Quebec, Professor Napoléon LeBlanc to whom we offer our congratulations. Mr. LeBlanc's predecessor in the high office of President of the General Conference was my distinguished colleague Mr. Taaitta Toweett, who presided so brilliantly over the deliberations of the nineteenth session. That session, held in Nairobi, was honoured by the presence of the distinguished President and great national leader of Kenya, Mr. Jomo Kenyatta, whose subsequent death has been such a tragic loss to his country, to the African continent and to humanity as a whole.
(4.3) In a forum of so high a spiritual level as the General Conference of Unesco, I believe it appropriate, on behalf of my Government, to express our sorrow at the death of two pontiffs of the Catholic Church: His Holiness Pope Paul VI - whose encyclical Populorum progressio will never be forgotten by the developing countries - and His Holiness Pope John Paul I who in a pontificate of tragically brief duration implanted an indelible image of peace and humility in the hearts of all men of goodwill. The assumption of the pontificate by his Holiness Pope John Paul II is an important and fresh sign of the universal mission of the Catholic Pe, a fresh sign of mankind's hopes that its deep and ancient traditions will point the way to new epartures.
(4.4) Realizing the need to facilitate the course of an inevitably complex discussion, I shall confine myself to some comments on the principal topics proposed to us.
(4.5) 1. New international economic order. The first item of a general policy nature which we have to study is Unesco's contribution to the establishment of a new international order. Our purpose is to give two-thirds of humanity the opportunity to enjoy an equitable share in the benefits of the technological revolution. Other United Nations agencies with different purposes and fields of competence are also working towards this objective. I should like to express my appreciation of the specific contribution made by Unesco - especially at the level of reflection - in its Medium-Term Plan and in documents 103 EX/4 and 105 EX/5 as well as in works such as Moving Towards Change and Thinking Ahead. These publications bear witness to the high intellectual standard and outstanding ability of the Secretariat and the Director-General.
(4.6) There are, however, some key questions - such as that of what are known as "basic needs" - which have been the subject of in-depth studies but which still call for further consideration and efforts on our part with a view to achieving a more precise definition of the concepts involved. The conceptual effort which is being carried out - and which is essential to any long-term action - must be undertaken parallel to and concurrently with the efforts made in other fields with a view to the gradual but effective establishment of a new international economic order. Meanwhile, it must be remembered, the industrial countries have been steadily increasing their share in world trade while that of the developing countries, particularly those of Latin America, has diminished.
(4.7) The conditions governing the transfer of technology to the developing countries and those governing foreign investments need to be thoroughly readjusted and adapted to the degree of interdependence which has been reached i. international economic relations. The gradual recognition of universal needs and aspira- tions, of the threat that non-renewable natural resources may be exhausted and of abuses in the exploitation of renewable resources, is creating a new bargaining position between the North and the South, between the developed world and the developing world. Up to the present everything seemed to depend on the possession of the capital and technology required to transform nature and raw materials effectively and rapidly. Today the need to control the use of natural resources - with which the developing countries are often well-supplied and which the developed countries need from them - results in an imperative need for more equitable relations between nations. And the reasons for this are not only moral and humanitarian; there are also pressing economic arguments for the establishment of equitable relations between nations to replace the aid programmes of the rich countries to the poor countries. The former colonies are today independent and sovereign States and they deal with the former colonizers on equal terms. The latter have need of the former and they also have at their disposal the capital and technology required for their develop- ment. Let us hope that all will realize how fragile are the advantages derived from purely historical combi- nations of circumstances, and that all will be ready faithfully and resolutely to establish an economic order based on justice.
(4.8) This presupposes not only a new economic order or a new social order based solely on economic relations. It presupposes the dynamic presence of a cultural order which would set the scene for social and economic development by bringing spiritual values such as justice and freedom to the fore. We hope that Unesco will proceed with its task of reflection and study of these extremely important issues and that it will be able to make a positive contribution to the work of next year's UNCTAD Conference in Manila. In this connection we would stress the immense importance of the programme on man and the biosphere which is managed by Unesco with the co-operation of many States and institutions. The MAB programme constitutes an example of dynamism, universality, relevance and unselfish co-operation which does honour to Unesco and confirms the wisdom of the Director-General in selecting the eminent Professor Di Castri to co-ordinate it. I believe it opportune to announce at this point that the Government of Chile - following the recent Conference held in Buenos Aires - has established a programme for technical co-operation with African countries for which a fund of $500,000 has been constituted.
(4.9) 2. Unesco's contribution to peace and its task with respect to the promotion of human rights and the elimination of colonialism and racism. "Since wars begin in the minds of men, it is in the minds of men that the defences of peace must be constructed." It is fitting that we should always bear this truth in mind in our debates. Conferences such as ours should always work towards building up these defences in our minds. They should help us to see ourselves as we really are. They should assist in dissipating false images which often tend to obscure our vision and lead to faulty communication.
(4.10) Unesco's mission is better fulfilled in contemplation than in decision-making. Its contribution is more valuable when it is "teaching how to live" or "teaching how to teach" than when it is taking decisions determined by the shifting pattern of majorities. All the efforts made in other United Nations forums or in the framework of international relations will be made in vain and will fall short of the work if we do not succeed in instilling in the minds and hearts of the peoples of the world an unshakeable conviction of the need for and the benefits of peace. It gives me great pleasure to pay tribute to Unesco's achievements in this task of directing the thinking and developing the conscience of the world, and I feel sure the General Conference will appreciate the Director-General's excellent work and approve the programmes being carried out under his wise leadership.
(4.11) Respect for human rights is the number one condition for peace. We believe that the principal task of Unesco is to contribute to a true and sincere consideration of what human rights are, how they contribute to the establishment of the rule of law, and the extent to which the historical development, characteristic features and cultural forms of each nation make them possible and give them their individual stamp. This is a task quite separate from that of the various forums and tribunals established and equipped for the express purpose of investigating and judging violations of human rights in whatever shape or form these violations may occur and however skilful or intimidating may be the way in which they are masked. No organization is better placed than Unesco to open minds to a deep understanding of the importance of human rights in the construction of patterns of human coexistence. The problem of enforcing world-wide observance of human rights is that of bringing the domestic jurisdiction of States into line with the interna- tional legal framework which the universal character of these rights implies. The organization of the interna- tional community is founded on the existence of free and sovereign States and the very Constitution of Unesco is designed with a view to preserving the independence, integrity and fruitful diversity of the cultures and educational systems of the Member States of this Organization, the Organization being prohibited from intervening in matters which are essentially within their domestic jurisdiction. For these reasons, the assignation, however tactically expedient, of judicial or jurisdictional functions to Unesco organs designed for a very different purpose would threaten to affect the character of those organs adversely and should be carefully avoided.
(4.12) The new procedure employed at the meetings of the Committee on Conventions and Recommendations revealed serious shortcomings. For example, it is by no means clear what criterion is to be used to differen- tiate between "cases" and "questions". And apart from the fact that this distinction may go against the essential and indivisible character which human rights must have by nature, introducing, as it does, notions of relativity and quantity, Unesco will be obliged to enter-into qualifications which necessarily call for procedures specific to a tribunal, thus contradicting its own Constitution and resolutions such as that adopted by the nineteenth session of the General Conference which stated that Unesèo was not an interna- tional judicial body. For this reason we believe that the Executive Board was right in its decision not to call on this Conference to approve a procedure which should still remain of a tentative and provisional nature.
(4.13) 3. The need to promote a greater concentration of the programme. The Draft Programme and Budget for the 1979-1980 biennium will most certainly be a central theme in the deliberations of this Conference. As always, the excellent introduction by the Director-General, as well as the main body of the document itself, reflect the complexity, extent and variety of the tasks in which the Organization is engaged. As this is not the right moment for in-depth analysis, we should simply like to say that the study carried out by the Swedish National Commission for Unesco seems to us to offer some excellent suggestions with regard to achieving greater concentration in the work of the Organization by pruning excess commitments which inevitably affect programme quality, resource levels and the proper co-ordination of the various activities.
(4.14) 4. International Fund for the Promotion of Culture. The Government of Chile has observed with special interest the development of the International Fund for the Promotion of Culture under the auspices of Unesco. Although only recently established, the Fund is already functioning vigorously under the distinguished leadership of the former President of the Inter-American Development Bank, Mr. Felipe Herrera, and with the whole-hearted co-operation of a number of highly distinguished figures from the world of culture. I am happy to be able to announce my Government's decision to participate in the Fund and to make an initial contribution to it. Although for the time being modest and limited in scope, this contribution is intended to signify Chilean sympathy and support for one of the most interesting initiatives to come out of Unesco's fruitful concern with these problems.
(4.15) 5. Regional bodies. As we did at the last Conference, held in Nairobi, we should like to express our strong support for the Regional Offices of Unesco. Their task is not only one of information and documen- tation, but also fundamentally of relating and co-ordinating the various regional programmes. We have given them our fullest collaboration and have certainly received in return a contribution to the educational activities currently being developed in Chile which I am happy to have this opportunity of acknowledging publicly.
(4.16) 6. Conference on Science and Technology. The Government of Chile is paying particular attention to the preparatory work for the United Nations Conference on Science and Technology for Development which is to be held in 1979. Among the results of this have been the preparation of a national monograph on the topic, the holding of a subregional preparatory meeting and participation in the regional preparatory meeting held recently in Panama. It would appear from our observations to date - which coincide with those of many Latin American and Caribbean governments - that there is a disturbing imbalance in the attention which is being given to political, economic and social problems as compared with that which is being given to scientific and technological policy problems as such. In this respect we were particularly pleased to note the decision adopted by the Executive Board during its 104th session, wherein the Director- General is invited to develop further Unesco's activities in the preparations for the Conference, notably by ensuring that the Organization co-operates in the identification of the social and cultural objectives and in the preparation of the programme of action. The Government of Chile, in the context of the Latin American and Caribbean region, will co-operate in endeavouring to ensure that Unesco occupies its rightful place in an event which by its very nature falls within the Organization's field of competence.
(4.17) TI should like to conclude these words spoken in the language of my country, by thanking the Director-General for his endeavours to promote the use of Spanish in Unesco's work and for the considerable progress that has been achieved in that regard. All that needs to be said on the subject has already been said most admirably by my distinguished colleague, the Minister of Education of Spain, and I need do no more than associate myself with his remarks. Thank you.
5. Le PRESIDENT.- Je remercie le chef de la délégation du Chili et j'invite maintenant S. Exc. M. Mohammed Ali Khan, ministre de l'éducation, chef de la délégation du Pakistan, à prendre la parole.
6.1 Mr. Mohammed Ali KHAN (Pakistan): Mr. President, Mr. Director-General, distinguished heads of delegations, ladies and gentlemen: We feel immensely happy to be attending this twentieth session of the General Conference of Unesco. Please allow me, Mr. President, to offer you our warmest congratulations on your election to the presidency of the Conference. My delegation has complete faith in your vision and understanding in steering the deliberations of this Conference to a rewarding conclusion. May I also congratulate the Vice-Presidents on their election. Lastly, I am indeed grateful to the Director-General and his staff for the excellent arrangements made for the Conference.
6.2 I should also like to take this opportunity of welcoming Namibia as a new member of Unesco. This admission is in line with decisions taken by the General Assembly and other bodies of the United Nations system, and I am sure that Namibia will make an important contribution to the work of the Organization.
6.3 Mr. President, Pakistan has been the cradle of one of the oldest and richest cultures in the world. Moenjodaro and Harrapa were flourishing cities with highly developed facilities five thousand years ago. The Buddhist period gave birth once again to the splendour of Gandhara art and culture, which is renowned for its beauty and precision, following which my country witnessed a momentous period of Islamic culture and civilization. Proud of its rich heritage and its traditions, Pakistan is at the same time conscious of its place in this rapidly changing modern society, and has consequently made considerable progress in giving a new dimension to its developmental effort. It has also played an effective role in the comity of nations, and continues to make its humble contribution to inter- national peace and understanding.
6.4 Pakistan is one of the older members of this Organization, and has been an ardent admirer of its achievements. We have always felt that Unesco constitutes an important and effective inter- national forum for voicing feelings and seeking international understanding and co-operation. We feel encouraged, therefore, to make a number of free and frank observations, and to put forward some proposals and suggestions for consideration.
6.5 Mr. President, you will agree with me that many of the developing countries are today still beset with intractable problems of mass poverty, food shortage, illiteracy, resource depletion, in- equality and unemployment. They suffer from numerous inequities in terms of trade, commerce and industry, and from many disabilities in acquiring the scientific and technological capacity for self- reliant and self-sustained development and in ensuring a secure future for the coming generations. Their miseries are further aggravated by continuing economic domination, the pressure of external powers and socio-cultural subservience.
6.6 This imbalance and inequity has created a situation in which the rich are becoming richer and the poor are becoming poorer. Today, 20 per cent of the affluent world enjoys 70 per cent of the total income of the world whereas three-fourths of mankind has the paltry share of 17 per cent. This is not due to any inevitable design, but without doubt to the existence of an oppressive and inequitous economic order in the world today. The Bandung Conference, the four United Nations Conferences on Trade and Development (UNCTAD), the two Special Sessions of the United Nations General Assembly and the North-South Dialogue have all proved that the wealthy nations have become wealthy not only because of their internal effort, but largely because of the continuous transfer of valuable resources from the developing world. This haemorrhage is still continuing at an alarming rate.
6.7 On the other hand, the developing countries have tremendous economic potential for making their own world a happy and peaceful one. They are richly endowed with raw materials, minerals and manpower. They produce more than 60 per cent of the world's oil. They account for 80 per cent of the world's exports of raw materials. They possess 70 per cent of the world's manpower, a massive commodity power, a sizeable potential for strategic market power and above all a collective and united strength.
6.8 Mr. President, most developing countries, which today comprise the majority of the world's nations, attained independence from the colonial rule in recent years. They are today sovereign nations, and members of this international Organization. Nevertheless, their independence and sovereignty, as well as their capacity to develop their economies and to meet the needs of their peoples, remain subject to outside forces. Although their peoples have indeed received economic aid and assistance from the developed countries during the past two decades, for many of them the economic struggle is a struggle for survival; and all of them find that they have no share in making the economic decisions which affect the welfare of their peoples. Development means economic eman- cipation, social justice and a better life for all within every country. The struggle for equality of opportunity for all peoples and nations, rich or poor, occupies a central place in international diplomacy.
6.9 Mr. President, the outlook for the world economy remains bleak. Whereas the developed nations face problems of inflation and unemployment, the developing countries have to contend with a never-ending deterioration in their terms of trade, increasing external deficits and stagnant or declining growth rates. The gulf between the developed and developing countries is widening and may become unbridgeable. The gravity of the situation is universally recognized.
6.10 The Sixth Special Session of the United Nations General Assembly adopted a Declaration and a Programme of Action on the establishment of a new international economic order. The Seventh Special Session drew up an agenda for negotiations between the developed and developing countries and established an order of priorities. These documents stressed the need for radical and fundamental changes which alone can bring about a more just and more efficient economic system. The Paris Conference, the most intensive negotiation held so far between developing and industrialized countries, produced very meagre results. Since the conclusion of that Conference, the North-South Dialogue has become bogged down in semantic differences. The world, Mr. President, is in danger of regressing to an era of economic isolationism and protectionism. This is surely the road to disaster. If disaster is to be averted and if the present crisis is to be overcome, a massive effort must be mounted to promote international growth by making optimum use of the available resources, manpower and technology of the developed and the developing countries alike. Yet, as speaker after speaker before me has recognized, little progress has been made towards finding agreed solutions for these issues, and indeed it would seem that their global and interdependent nature has not received recognition.
6.11 Mr. President, Unesco is not an appropriate forum for the detailed discussion of economic issues. I have referred to them in passing because, as everybody recognizes, these issues are central to the prevailing world crisis, and because the subjects with which this Organization deals are closely related to the process of economic and social development. In general terms, there are two clearly identified fields in which Unesco can play a positive and essential role. In the first place, it can do so by promoting education, science and culture in the developing countries and contributing to the requisite and massive effort for economic and social transformation on a global scale., The fact that the world stands today at a cross-roads between such a transformation and retrogression to the policies of isolationism and economic warfare, imposes on this Organization a second important responsibility, which is: to help in the creation of a better understanding of the universal nature of the goals which mankind must pursue if we are to emerge from our present difficulties. To be more specific, it is necessary that at this Conference we should find ways of breaking the paralysis of political will which impedes progress towards resolving the deadlock in the
646    negotiations, between the developed and the developing worlds. Unesco, which brings together educationalists and scientists, thinkers and writers, is eminently, indeed uniquely fitted to perform the task of creating an awareness of the true independence of all countries.
6.12 Mr. President, permit me to emphasize once more that the common problem faced by the develop- ing countries today is the pressing need for comprehensive socio-economic transformation. This requires the moulding of a new man and an emergence of a new value system, which can only be made possible through a change-oriented and culture-stimulating educational system. The achievement of socio-cultural liberation and the emergence of distinctive and progressive indigenous cultures will largely depend on the nature of education and communication systems provided for the people and on their effective deployment as instruments of economic or socio-cultural change and development. The interaction of individuals or groups of people with education, and the acceptance of education, depends on a number of factors, including environment, cultural heritage and past experiences, future possibilities, expectations and aspirations. Consequently, in the process of assimilation and acceptance, education itself undergoes a change and in turn transforms the entire personality of the human being.
6.13 This twentieth session of the General Conference will no doubt take stock of the philosophy spelt out in the Medium-Term Plan and its consolidation in the document now before us for ratification. Unesco's objectives: and its contribution to peace, international understanding, the eradication of apartheid, development, the emancipation of women and the new economic order are known to all. The developing countries invest high hopes and expectations in this Organization, and look forward to it for help and assistance in bringing them closer to each other. Developing countries have identity of purpose, commonality of views, agreement in the order of priorities and similarity in their socio-cultural situations. A synthesis of their divergent resource potentials could lead to the trans- formation of the developing world.
6.14 Unesco has a key role to play in achieving such a transformation, and thus contribute to lasting peace. It can play this role for the betterment of the developing countries by evolving a blue-print for the eighties, based on the following broad guidelines: the rejuvenation of the cultural heritage and its confrontation with the challanges of the present and the future; the reinvigoration of individual and collective creativity; the revitalization of indigenous structures, institutions and traditions for self-realization; and the decommercialization and depolitization of technical co-operation.
6.15 In making these suggestions, I should like to commend the many initiatives already taken by Unesco in this direction. One outstanding example is the Asian Programme of Educational Innovation for Development (APEID). We fully support the philosophy, the method of work, and the modes of operation of this programme, and urge that it be expanded and its resources strengthened. New programmes in other areas of importance to Member States might be developed on a similar pattern.
6.16 A hitherto neglected but most fruitful area which can offer increasing opportunities for co-operation and collaboration between the developing countries is that of science and technology. Scientific knowledge and technology are key inputs for development. Yet, for one reason or another, a variety of constraints are imposed by the advanced nations on the transfer of technology, and when such transfer does take place, it is often on costly terms and under onerous conditions. It would thus appear that science and technolology, which are universal in their very nature, are to be used as instruments for political and economic domination. At the same time, it has now become abundantly clear that without capacbility in science and technology, the developing countries are doomed to dependent status.
6.17 Mr. President, this is not to deny the need to adapt particular technologies for use in the developing countries. . Much of the technology developed in the industrialized countries is suited to their own conditions and markets and was developed to meet their own particular needs and problems. The concept of "appropriate technology" should not, however, be interpreted to mean that only out-dated or simple technologies are to be made available to the developing countries. It is not always appreciated that developing countries may need the latest and most efficient techniques in order to provide their peoples with a reasonable standard of living and to make up the leeway that separates them from the advanced countries.
6.18 The long-term solution is, of course, for the developing countries to build up their own basic research facilities and to develop their own technology. For many reasons, imported technology cannot provide a permament solution. In the first place, it impedes a country's capacity for generat- ing growth and cripples its self-confidence; secondly, imported techniques and processes are expen- sive; thirdly, purchased technology requires adjustment and modification; fourthly, standards of teaching and research institutions of higher education are seriously impaired; fifthly, the exploitation of indigenous resources receives a set-back; and finally, long-term solutions for national development cannot be left to borrowed technology.
6.19 Since the development of technologies suited to each country's resources and socio-economic systems is a prerequisite to development; I propose that regional co-operation for the sharing of experience be strengthened around the nucleus of existing regional mechanisms. We in Pakistan have some experience in this field and would like to share it with other developing countries, perhaps within the framework of the Pakistan Institute of Appropriate Technology.
6.20 Mr. President, development is basically endogenous in nature. It grows from within, and must be rooted in the social, cultural and economic soil of society. It has to be self-sustaining and self-evaluating, multidimensional and all-embracing. In drawing up their development strategies, the
17 developing countries cannot take ready-made models from developed countries and transplant them without adaptation into their own environment. This does not mean, of course, that we are advocat- ing social and cultural isolation. On the contrary, as I said a moment ago, education, science and culture are by their very nature universal and do not admit of frontiers. Every nation which develops and strengthens its own social, economic and cultural identity in accordance with its traditions, goals and aspirations enriches the fabric of civilization in its entirety.
6.21 A nation's pre-eminence in education and science is determined by the excellence of its institu- tions of higher learning and research. Consequently, it is necessary for the developing countries to aim at building a strong and productive system of higher education and research. Mr. President, the pressing need of the developing countries for high quality manpower and expertise to steer their developmental effort and to man their developmental projects makes it urgent that their higher educa- tion systems be large enough to meet future requirement and at the same time strong enough to give them a feeling of self-respect and self-reliance.
6.22 During recent years, the aid givers have unfortunately shown little interest in the educational and scientific effort of the developing countries. They are narrowing down their contributions to the lower levels of school education. This being so, it has become all the more important for the developing countries to concentrate on higher education in order to catalyse their developmental effort.
6.23 Mr. President, the intellectual centre of gravity in the developing countries is, generally speaking, located in their universities and institutions of higher learning. It is urgent that these institutions be strengthened through the provision of better facilities, so that a conducive environment may be created for the generation and promotion of new ideas, and for innovative approaches to support for endogenous and indigenous development.
6.24 The different developing countries possess talent and expertise which can make an important contribution to their economic development. As a matter of fact, there is a continuous exodus of talent from East to West, largely because of lack of incentives and facilities at home. This indicates that research facilities for productive and creative work in our own universities must be enhanced and strengthened, so that our talent is attracted and committed to national development.
6.25 During the Conference of Ministers of Education and Ministers Responsible for Economic Planning in Asia and Oceania held in Sri Lanka, it was resolved that Unesco should set up a study group for this purpose. It was also suggested at that Conference that regional commissions on higher education and research and associations of universities might be established to help to place higher education in the developing countries on a stronger footing. I would appeal to the Executive Board and the Director-General to give greater attention to this urgent and pressing need. Unesco must exert itself to launch an innovative programme in higher education and research. Our Organization has already done great service in the universalization of education and adult literacy. A further initiative in higher education and research would indeed constitute a unique step forward. Thank you, Mr. President.

8.10 In his introductory remarks on the mass media declaration, the Director-General called for a constructive dialogue that could lead to a consensus. 8.11 Contemporary examples of this basic point are not difficult to find. They have also moved to prohibit the circulation of information about the extent and the effects of racism in that region. We have recently witnessed similar attempts by governments in other regions to suppress the circulation of documents that draw attention to the violation of human rights. It seems clear from these illustrations that it is freedom of information, and not its control by the State, that is best calculated to achieve the elimination of racism and to promote the attainment of economic and political rights.

8.15 This movement towards constructive and principled and unifying action is in the continuing spirit of Nairobi. So also is what I have to say today on the subject of practical co-operation. Two years ago, when I was privileged to address the General Conference in Nairobi, I acknowledged the existence of dependencies, disparities, and imbalances in and among national communication capabilities. On that occasion I proposed that measures might be taken by the United States and other developed countries, together with their private sectors and the multilateral institutions, to help other States strengthen their information and communication systems in accordance with their needs. Today I want to describe what has been and is being done on our part, and then move beyond that to propose a system for improved co-operation among all the nations that can, I believe, move us purposefully and measurably toward the realization of our common goals.
8.16 Let me begin by recalling the scope and dimension of those goals. As I said in 1976, "the central issue is ... to achieve growth with equity and to pay special attention to the poorest of the poor within nations and among nations". Internal and international disparities often go hand in hand. Of the four hundred million telephones in the world, for example, only forty million, a bare ten per cent are to be found in all of Africa, Asia, and Latin America combined. What does this imply for the scope of participation in the life of those societies, or for two-way information flows within them?
8.17 A presently pending Unesco report to the General Conference devotes similar attention to the unevenness of communications development within societies, and also highlights the existence of gross quantitative disparities among the nations of the world. It reveals that thirty developing countries still have no television service at all, nor the technical skills to develop one. In about forty develop- ing countries, fewer than five per cent of the people ever see a newspaper, and in more than sixty countries, where radio broadcasting may be the instrument chosen for nation-building, more than half the population has no radio set. To this must be added a pervasive shortage of skilled tech- nicians and teachers to build up and extend communication capacities.
8.18 It should be apparent from this brief description that the challenge of communications develop- ment is not one that can be met by simple or random infusions of assistance, or by the immediate adoption of any formula for a new world order. If we are to have any serious impact, we must proceed in a far more systematic, long-range, and concerted fashion than we have previously pursued. And we must attract co-operation from every quarter I mentioned two years ago - the more prosperous nations, the private sector in those nations, the multilateral institutions, and the disadvantaged countries themselves.
8.19 Why should we collectively take on this burden? First, because information is increasingly recognized as a basic resource - intangible and inexhaustible, but otherwise akin to energy and raw materials - that is to say, essential to full participation in the modern world. Second, because in the face of this recognition it would be unthinkable for us to allow our nations and our peoples to drift by neglect into two separate and distinct camps, the "information rich" versus the "information poor". Third, there are some common goals on which we do agree and around which we can construct an action agenda that draws us together and that emphasizes the value of our common institutions, like Unesco. Those goals include the steady reduction of disparities and dependencies and imbalances in communication capacities, and the progressive fostering of many-sided dialogues rather than monologues in internal as well as international communication structures.
8.20 What can be done, then, to get things started? Two years ago I suggested a collegial effort. The responses we have been hearing at this Conference thus far are heartening. More will no doubt be heard, and a great deal more is required if we are to move appreciably towards the attainment of our goal. Let me begin my own contribution by recounting what the United States Government has been doing in this field since Nairobi.

In my own country, an effort is now under way for the first time to design a comprehensive and readily accessible clearing-house of all communication policy research undertaken in the various relevant disciplines; when completed, this should be suitable for interconnection with national research centres in other countries through the Unesco- affiliated network known as COMNET.

The basic result should be to take important information, much of which is already available in urban centres of developing nations, and distribute it to remote sections where people have little or no access to knowledge that can improve their way of life.

A major part of the American contribution will be the provision of technical assistance, equipment, and training to promote fully informed use of satellite capacity in the developing nations.
8.32 We expect to learn much from this new project. But it is much more than a technological demonstration. The project will be aimed at building permanent communication technological skills in these countries. At its conclusion, all aspects of management and control will be turned over to the recipient nations. We believe that this can mark an innovative, productive approach to urgent problems of rural development and communications, and we are pleased that this project will be moving forward in the months ahead.
8.33 These are the major new initiatives that the United States is taking to help develop a better balance of communications capability throughout the world. But as I have stressed repeatedly, we need more. We need in particular to gather the strength and purpose that can come from the inter- change of insights, experiences, and plans, whether bilateral or multilateral or from the public or private sectors, and from the systematized presentation of development objectives.
8.34 A large part of communications development is now accomplished through bilateral co-operation. It is in this sector that collaborative consultation could serve to detect gaps and overlaps, and to strengthen the presently fragmented process. The bilateral character of such activities need not be changed, but ways should be found to focus them on priority needs in a co-operative way with identifiable goals and measurements of progress. Our study has suggested to us that the international community may have already discovered at least a partial precedent for what is required, in the organization and work of the Consultative Group on International Agricultural Research.
8.35 The applicability of this precedent to our purposes is not perfect. The agricultural research centres had been in existence for several years before their funding was co-ordinated, so that the sponsoring institutions took over a fully proven concept. We have nothing like that at present in the field of communications assistance. But is the analogy, none the less, perhaps worth pursuing? My Government believes that it may be.
8.36 The present Consultative Group is jointly sponsored by the Food and Agriculture Organization, the World Bank, and the United Nations Development Programme. We could substitute Unesco for FAO as a sponsor. Like the existing ‘Group, we could establish an integrated and effective member- ship consisting of both developed and developing countries, the regional banks, concerned multilateral agencies, and non-profit foundations. Other appropriate international organizations could certainly be invited to participate. Out of the meetings and studies of a Communications Consultative Group there should emerge a shared sense of development priorities and of the effectiveness of existing and proposed remedies. More than that, we would, with the help of the sponsoring institutions including Unesco, engender co-operation on a scale that simply is not possible under arrangements existing at present. My Government would invite our fellow members to consider this possibility with us.

I hope we can discover and display the seriousness of purpose that alone will attract the sponsorship of serious international bodies. Therefore, I invite the Director-General to convene a planning meeting within the next six months, at which government delegations can seek to reach agreement on a specific proposal that can be presented on behalf of developing and developed countries alike to the institutions whose co- ordinating sponsorship we would seek. My Government is prepared to play a full part in these deliberations.
8.38 Mr. President, my concluding hope is that we will come to agreement on the communication issues and on all the others we confront, so that together we can move towards making Unesco a more effective instrument for meeting historic challenges. For it is through such strengthening of

The minds of men and women are stirred by purposeful participation in programmes of effective action, not by mere rhetoric or political posturing. This is Unesco's mission: to provide the means for enhancing practical co-operation in education, the sciences, culture, and communication. Let us get on with that job. Thank you, Mr. President.

We are thus endeavouring to determine what is the best for our school leavers, and in the interests of the country's economic development.
10.6 Mr. President, since we believe that all Kenyans should enjoy the right of access to education, it is the Government's intention to make the first seven years of schooling free of charge. This will make primary education available to all children of school age, whether they come from poor or from rich families. It will raise the national standard of education and enable the primary school leaver to acquire sufficient basic education for more specialized training. In order that all children of school age may be enrolled in primary schools, the Government plans to increase the number of boarding primary schools in areas where the nomadic people of Kenya live, so as to ensure that their children remain in the primary school system.
10.7 One of the major problems facing our country, Mr. President, and one which is shared by many other countries, is unemploymnt among those who have received formal education. This problem is even greater for young people who terminate their education at the end of primary school. We have therefore considered it necessary to review the curriculum, so that it may be more instrumental in instilling the attitudes and skills which would enable young school leavers to engage in gainful employment and thus contribute towards national social, cultural and economic development. There are still major areas of national development which are seriously hampered by lack of people with the appropriate attitudes and skills. In particular, we realize that there is need to facilitate the growth of the informal sector of the economy, and in particular to create opportunities for gainful self- employment in agriculture or other activities in rural areas. This means that we must take deliberate steps to readjust the educational system so that these considerations may be reflected in the curriculum. We intend to introduce the teaching of adaptive skills in upper primary school classes, because we feel that this will provide the graduates of these classes with a useful initiation to technology and the world of work. We also believe that this will provide young children with the opportunity of discovering their own talents and enable them to pursue further training in their specialities after school, and that this enlargement of educational horizons will also offer an opportunity for work-study programmes and thus bring schoolchildren closer to the realities of their environment.
10.8 After this initiation to technology and the world of work, the Kenya Government plans to offer more opportunities for further training, through the establishment of more specialized village poly- technics designed to serve as centres for the training of more specialized craftsmen. Such village polytechnics will initially concern themselves with the teaching of skills which can make use of the raw materials available in the immediate environment. They will also have the capacity to provide a broad knowledge of basic skills applicable to occupations within a given field. This will give the trainees an opportunity to make effective choices and to select satisfying careers. We feel that this will not only reduce the rate of migration from rural to urban areas, but will also equip young people with the tools that are necessary to enable them to contribute to the long-term aim of improving
653   the quality of life in rural areas. It will also create more opportunities for self-improvement and competency for production, marketing and the management of enterprises in these areas. We realize that this will be an expensive undertaking, but we believe that when the expected goals are achieved we will have moved a step closer to involving our youth in the national development. We know that we shall have to make sacrifices as a nation, but we believe that in the long run there will be enormous benefits to the whole population of Kenya.
10.9 Teacher education is a most essential component of change in the field of education. Planning for the development, expansion and reorientation of education must take into account the quality and the in-service training of the teachers. The training of new teachers must be oriented towards the achievement of new goals, and must be based on a thorough evaluation of both short-term and long- term needs. In other words, policies with regard to the training of teachers and educational personnel in general must support both the general objectives adopted for the educational process and national social and economic requirements. In this context, we feel that Unesco should make an even greater contribution to the training of educational personnel which has a special relationship with growth and change in education, because unless education can change with the requirements of the society and time it cannot in itself serve as an instrument of change. That is why, Mr. President, we fully support Objective 5.5 (Promotion of the training of educational personnel), and feel that further activities in this area will greatly strengthen national education systems. We suggest that Unesco explore possibilities of offering more fellowships here, so that sufficient numbers of personnel may be trained to steer national educational systems in the right and desirable direction.
10.10 Mr. President, special education continues to occupy a very prominent place in the democrat- ization of our education system. We indeed realize that we have a responsibility to provide education for the handicapped, and to integrate them as far as it is possible into the normal pattern of education and training. Kenya's National Committee on Education Objectives and Policies, which I mentioned earlier, has made several recommendations which should enable us to cater for the handicapped in a more systematic manner. Because of the diversity of handicaps, training of teachers is a complicated issue, but we feel that schools for handicapped children, if the latter are to be trained and allowed to develop their skills to the best of their abilities, require teachers whose competences match those of teachers in any other institution. This is essentially because the development of such skills and attitudes will permit the total integration of the handicapped into society. My delegation therefore believes that Unesco should explore possibilities of increasing Unesco training programmes for the teachers of the handicapped.
10.11 Like many other developing countries, Kenya attaches great importance to non-formal education. This is because we are aware that in order to achieve overall national development, we must think more deeply and seriously about the development of our rural areas. The full contribution of such areas to national development is of utmost importance, because this will mean self-improvement in both economic and social terms, as well as general awareness of the environment. People must know how to preserve their own environment and how to improve upon agricultural methods so that natural resources may be exploited for the improved health and advancement of the community. The Kenya Government thus intends to intensify its activities in the non-formal education sector, to continue to diversify such education and to make it even more production-oriented, since it believes that this will make the rural population even more self-reliant. We also believe non-formal education will eventually lead to the creation of job opportunities for people in rural areas, either on a self-employed basis or through employment in small co-operatives, and that this will reduce the rate of migration into urban areas. In our view, the improvement of opportunities in rural areas will redress the balance in development between rural and urban areas, and will enable the urban population to make a greater contribution to the social, economic and cultural development of the nation.
10.12 Mr. President, science and technology should aim at establishing a just and more equitable order. It should aim at establishing a more fraternal world, based on equity and greater fairness. It should promote international peace and enhance the cultural, social and economic development of man. It should serve to develop a better world and it should not be used for destruction. This role is carefully outlined in Chapter 2 of document 20 C/5, and my delegation wishes to commend the Director-General and the Secretariat on the careful manner in which this chapter has been prepared. We believe that the nations of the world are willing to co-operate to ensure that technological knowledge is made available to those who need it. My delegation feels that Unesco should be in the vanguard of world thinking in the field of science and technology, and should concern itself particularly with the advancement of science and technology in managing and utilizing the resources at our disposal, some of which are not renewable. It should endeavour to provide national governments with training facilities for the most-needed personnel, particularly in the field of research techniques. The develop- ment and management of natural resources is very important, particularly in a country like ours, where threequarters of the population depend on the land for their livelihood.
10.13 In July 1977, the Kenya Government implemented the Science and Technology Act, establishing a National Council for Science and Technology which is the body responsible for advising it on all matters relating to scientific and technological activities in the country. This Council is responsible for the co-ordination of research, and for advising the Government on the formulation of suitable policies on various aspects of science and technology for advancement and progress. For it to be of maximum service to the nation, this young body needs the necessary expertise to develop its documen- tation systems. Information, however carefully stored, cannot be useful unless it can be made easily available to the user and at the right time. The development of usable national information systems is a very important aspect of scientific and technological advancement. The developing countries have conducted a considerable amount of research in different fields, but the method of storage of the knowledge acquired may not facilitate access to such knowledge. This leads to a wastage of
654      financial resqurces which could otherwise be used for further research. Unesco's assistance and expertise in this field would be welcomed by many developing countries.
10.14 Mr. President, it is the policy of my Government to offer a science education based pre- dominantly on the needs of terminal students. We feel that such students should acquire a certain core of scientific knowledge that will be of use to them in their practical life after school, bearing in mind that most of them leave school after the primary cycle and at a very early age. This knowledge will also enable them to understand the environment around them and make them better able to exploit the natural resources at their disposal. The aim here is to facilitate their endeavours to improve themselves and subsequently play a role in the improvement of society. This will have a definite and strong bearing on the role our university is expected to play in the provision of science teachers. Our major problem, at present, which many other countries perhaps share, is to provide qualified science teachers for our secondary schools. We expect our university to produce teachers of quality, who will in turn contribute to the improvement of science education in our country.
10.15 The Kenya Institute of Education has been entrusted with responsibility for the development of courses in environmental education. This is an area which requires immediate attention if we are to create the kind of awareness which leads to a more conscious preservation of our environment. We believe that every citizen of the future should be inculcated with such awareness from the moment at which he begins school. We believe that if the pastoralists understand the need to preserve the environment, then they will recognize the danger of overgrazing their lands, and consequently join in the efforts to prevent encroachment by the desert. Engineers and planners should also have some knowledge of conservation, so that they can relate their work to the environment. Although at present we are focusing our attention on certain areas where the ecological balance has in some way been disturbed, we consider the development of a total education concept for the maintenance of a balanced environment to be very important. In this connection, we feel that Unesco could assist needy governments in the development of suitable courses and teaching aids, particularly for primary and secondary schools. We note with satisfaction that Objective 7.7 (Education and information relating to the environment), in document 20 C/5, clearly outlines the line of action in this area, and my delegation fully supports the resolution proposed in paragraph 2569. We also support the work plan under that heading, and are glad to note that National Committees of the MAB Programme will be fully involved in some of the planned activities. The programmes proposed in the chapter on natural resources also meet with our support in principle.
10.16 Mr. President, the need to belong to a culture is universal and every culture has its own specificity. This specificity is a reflection of its identity. Each society has the need and desire to be aware of its identity, because it is this identity which forms the basis of development and the promotion of unity. It would be difficult to carry out developmental programmes without taking into account the cultural background of people. We have seen some good projects rejected because they conflict in some way or another with the cultural norms and beliefs of the society concerned. We hold that participation in culture in a country like Kenya becomes an emancipatory act, and that it represents creation, innovation and free self-expression. We believe, moreover, that irrespective of social and political organization, cultural identity must be asserted and cultural values must be promoted. My Government holds that it has a responsibility and a duty to promote the cultural values of the people, so that they may live in dignity and satisfaction.
10.17 Indeed, Kenya's Sessional Paper No. 10 on African Socialism and its Application to Develop- ment, which spells out the Government policy, strongly emphasizes that the total cultural values of the people constitute the foundations of principles which can be adopted to fit in with a dynamic modern economic system. In the words of the late President of Kenya, Mzee Jomo Kenyatta: "songs and dances are the roots of the national culture, and culture is the foundation of any nation. The value of a people lies in their culture".
10.18 While it is important to intensify the study of specific areas of societies, it is equally important to encourage studies and activities designed to encourage the mutual appreciation of cultures. To understand and to appreciate other people requires an understanding of their cultures, while this reciprocal understanding and appreciation of other cultures will doubtless in turn foster international understanding. We are glad to note that reasonable attention will be given to the philosophical aspects of intercultural studies, and that regional studies dealing specifically with the intermingling of cultures will be encouraged. At the same time, we would like to urge that these studies be not only limited to the Caribbean and North and South America, as proposed, but should extend to Europe, whence much of the distortion of the African image has emanated. We feel that this could lead to better understanding and could go a long way in correcting some of this distortion.
10.19 Kenya has been lucky in that its people have inherited a reasonable diversity of cultural traditions, many of which have been handed down from generation to generation within the numerous groups that comprise the nation. Others have come to us through our contacts with other peoples of the world, which date back to pre-historic times. Thus, though change may be desirable in all fields of human endeavour, there still remains that indispensable requirement of retaining valuable wisdom, satisfying aesthetic expression, and fulfilling patterns of human relations among persons which cannot be discarded in favour of alien cultural thinking. My Government is thus fully aware that the unity, advancement and cohesion of our nation must lie within a progressive national cultural policy which takes into consideration the dignity of each cultural entity.
10.20 Mr. President, it is the intention of my Government to allow the voluntary agencies which desire to participate in cultural activities to do so, in co-operation with the government institutions which are charged with responsibility for these activities. Indeed, the Government intends to offer
655   assistance to voluntary groups or individuals wishing to engage in activities aimed at promoting culture. To this end, it plans to establish a National Council of Arts and Culture, which will be responsible for co-ordinating cultural activities throughout the country. This Council, which is expected to have branches all over the Republic, with the aim of reaching all the people, will be expected to promote public interest in cultural arts and to ensure that cultural affairs are accorded sufficient prominence in the national plans for social and economic development. It will work closely with all the other bodies. in the country, and particularly with the Ministry of Education, to ensure that young people participate fully in the nation's cultural activities.
10.21 Research in oral traditions is still a major preoccupation of my Government. This research has assumed even greater significance during the past few years, since our discovery of the wealth of knowledge that can be documented and stored for posterity. Kenya is at present vigorously engaged in strengthening the institutions which are involved in this valuable work. We note with great satisfaction that it is planned to establish a regional centre for oral traditions in Zanzibar. This centre will no doubt be an intellectual stimulant, and also act as a catalyst for the establishment and reinforcement of national research centres for oral traditions. Kenya intends to participate fully in the activities of this centre.
10.22 Mr. President, my delegation appreciates the proposal contained in paragraphs 4072 et seq of document 20 C/5 on the promotion of national languages. Kenya has declared Kiswahili as its national language, and we are now engaged in research to refine Kiswahili so that it can effectively be used in schools and other academic institutions. We fully support the proposed Unesco project "Horizon 2000", the aim of which is to enable some African languages to be developed and used as languages of instruction in schools and as means of transmitting knowledge before the end of the century. This is a task which must be carried out with vigour. The result will no doubt be satisfying and will bring pride and dignity to Africa.
10.23 The encouragement of artistic and intellectual creativity should lie at the very heart of cultural activities. Artists of all kinds have the duty to promote cultural and social development through their diverse engagements. Although the value of the latter may not be easily recognizable in our highly materialistic societies, they must be seen as an integral part of any society, and as a very important component in its total development process. Even our traditional societies, artists were harmoniously integrated, and their work was highly respected. In seeking to stimulate artistic and intellectual creativity in Kenya, we intend not only to look at the work done in places of intellectual aspiration such as schools, colleges and universities, but also to examine the various forms of artistic creativity that may be in progress in such institutions as village polytechnics, women's organizations, and youth organizations. Special attention must, however, be paid to the schools, where efforts should be made to initiate young people in artistic and creative activities. It is here that the young child should have the opportunity of self-discovery, and of self-expression in various ways, with art as the medium of communication. This initiation into the world of art should be followed by encourage- ment in the continued development of these abilities, either through formal education or through activities in the context of lifelong education. To this end, the Kenya Government has always en- couraged the teaching of arts in schools and the development of suitable art courses at all levels. The plan to increase the number of village polytechnics and to equip them to offer a wide diversity of training programmes will no doubt go a long way in offering the right atmosphere for a continuation of artistic activities by those who are outside the formal school system. The overall national plan for artistic activities has been specified in the report of the National Committee on Education Objectives and Policies, to which I referred earlier.
10.24 Information, Mr. President, is a very important tool for all-round development. In all fields of human endeavour we find that information plays a very important role in communicating the available and needed knowledge to the user. We find that even the small-scale farmer needs information on his various activities, in order to improve his methods of farming and subsequently improve himself. Man is known to have the ability to invent new processes and open up new paths in all fields of knowledge leading to development and growth. These new processes need to be put at the disposal of the whole human race for peaceful uses and for creation of better living conditions, now and in the future. This is where the role of information becomes crucial. Information therefore, cannot be viewed as a separate sector but should be seen as a branch of the economy which is completely integrated into the development process and which contributes to the fulfilment of assigned objectives. Information is also a very important tool for the transfer of technology and can assist a great deal in creating the right atmosphere for the advancement and fulfilment of the developmental programmes of the nations of the world.
10.25 Mr. President, Kenya has, in many forums, expressed her feelings about free flow of informa- tion. We believe that information can be of great service in creating the proper atmosphere for international understanding and peace. Correct and unprejudiced information about other people, about their cultures and values, will create greater understanding and respect among all the peoples of the world. At the national level, and particularly in countries like mine where we have many ethnic groups, information serves the purpose of creating better understanding among the various groups and thus creating the right atmosphere for national peace. It serves the purpose of trans- mitting cultural information and in this it contributes to the desired harmonious living.
10.26 At present, many developing countries like Kenya need information on matters which are crucial to their development. Le PRESIDENT.- En votre nom, je remercie chaleureusement le chef de la délégation du Kenya, qui, est-il besoin de vous le rappeler, était le président de la dix-neuviéme session de la Conférence générale. 12.1 Mr. NIANIAS (Greece): Mr. President, on behalf of the Greek delegation, I should like to congratulate you on your election as President of our General Conference and to wish you great success.
12.2 I am going to talk very generally and leave my colleagues in the Greek delegation to present, if necessary, a picture of what is happening in our country in regard to matters with which Unesco is concerned.
12.3 I believe that despite all the efforts made over a numberof years there are still crucial problems to solve and fundamental questions to answer that are relevant to the very purposes for which Unesco was founded.
12.4 "Development" is the name by which we know one of those deities set up by our present-day society to show our concern for the economic growth of the world. In the nineteenth century it was "progress" that worked its charms, like another Circe, on the society of the time, and "adventure" on yet earlier generations beginning with the establishment of the Hanseatic League. History is full of such shifts in terminology, but by whatever name we have referred to it, we have all along been talking about economics.
12.5 There is no facet of public life which is not directly or indirectly under the influence of "development". In our day and age, civilization is under the almighty sway of programming. We all know that in these circumstances cultural affairs budgets are in constant crisis until they are incor- porated into the long-term or short-term development programmes of governments.
12.6 Unesco has long experience of this matter. And yet so-called "development" is a process of our existence that derives from and is determined by other more profound functions of life. It is in fact a secondary manifestation. However serious "development" may be in its aims, it is only a veneer cloaking with calm and optimism those deep-seated and turbulent problems and hazards which, if not dealt with, may lead swiftly to dire consequences that defy control.
12.7 We all know that development rests on a solid foundation of scientific and technological processes. A grave problem arises here, a problem that has to do with the course of scientific research and technology and the conditions under which they are conducted. For it is a fact that both fundamental and applied research are carried out within a framework that gives rise to grave doubts and questioning regarding their impact on the fate of the world, riddled as it is with crisis, conflict and anxiety. The regime of fundamental and applied research comprises three elements: programming of the objectives and priorities of research, allocation and control of its resources and, lastly, the professionals
658       (the human potential) engaged in research. These elements - priorities, financing and staffing - are of course the same in the later phase of exploitation of scientific and technological development, that is, the practical application of knowledge and its economic utilization.
12.8 I would mention here our shattering experience during the last half century which has seen the collapse of age-old assumptions and the revelation that science is not so much a god as a potentially benevolent servant. The collapse began when we first noticed that all forms of violence and all dangers to mankind were originating in science laboratories. The simplistic optimism of the nineteenth century and the conceit of our arrogant twentieth century with regard to the supremacy of science, summed up in the slogan "science takes the lead", were swept away by the threat to mankind of extinction by the so-called gifts of science.
12.9 I do not need to point out that man's future, the conduct of war and peace, the health and nourishment of man, the very continuation of life in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries depend entirely on scientific activity. Having said that, scientists must ask themselves who determines the programme of research, who determines the priorities, and the practical, social and ethical consider- ations on which they are to rest. Further, they must ask whether the provider of resources can be identified with the taker of decisions and, indeed, who can be such a provider of resources. Of course, by this I mean the source of finance for a research project of vast import to the fate of mankind.
12.10 Depending on the answers to these questions, scientists, those countless workers engaged in research in the major countries of the world, acquire a special significance. Without them science is inconceivable. On what assumptions then, and with what knowledge of objectives do scientists apply themselves? How aware are they of the social and economic implications of their research? Do they indeed know what their research is all about and, whether they do or do not, what persuades them to undertake their work in the first place? The same questions may be asked of those engaged in the exploitation of knowledge, which once it is wrested from nature becomes a technology and acquires a commercial value. If we accept that there are possessors of knowledge, whether governments or universities, political parties or industrialists, the question arises on what criteria do they determine priorities for the exploitation of their possession. Or by what criteria is research financed? Or on what principle is research into weaponry, whether atomic or conventional, given priority over research into cancer, heart disease and arthritis? There are, in short, three questions to be asked: who determines, by what right, and on what principles does he so determine?
12.11 I only know that these questions are of more vital importance to the fate of humanity than the general run of political questions that citizens ask when judging their governments. It is eminently clear that these questions concern both nations and leaders of nations. If you want an answer, it is clear that so far as rights and social objectives are concerned, it lies beyond the realm of science and should come from other areas of human thought and discipline.
12.12 In themselves, technology and its products are neither ethically good nor ethically evil. They acquire a positive or negative moral value according to the end to which man applies them. Thus our aims and ideas, our ethical criteria, our values and convictions all have their place in determining the use to which we put double-faced science, that one inescapable factor in our lives.
12.13 One would have expected the solution of such problems, or some kind of direction, to be provided by the disciplines dealing with human behaviour and values: by sociology, philosophy, ethics and psychology, and so on. Unfortunately, there is no agreement on this crucial question; nor is there an objectively accepted interpretation or a system of principles for guiding the greatest enterprise and adventure in human history, the growth and direction of present-day and future science. Something should be done about this for the sake of the six billion people who will be alive in the world at the end of the century. The development of science should be controlled by legal action to allow human individuality to be cultivated in justice and freedom.
12.14 For the time being, it would seem that we lack a correct understanding, not of the problems of government, but of the most critical, practical and moral problems presented by the everyday realities of our lives. If, confronted by such an absence of generally accepted values and principles, one has to hold to anything it should surely be to the inviolable principle of human individuality. And yet this itself appears to many a dogmatic principle on which throughout history doubt has been cast time and again. This is where education has its place, for it is one of the most formative factors in our society. Everything wise and profound that has been said about education reinforces the arguments of those who believe in its power, particularly in the absence of an established interpret- ation of the purpose of life and human happiness.
12.15 However, as long as education was strictly confined within the bounds of natural science, it tended to be a training in the knowledge and application of natural laws, materials and processes, and produced a one-sided, introspective and at times dangerous human being. For this reason, in using the term education one must include all the cultural upbringing that occurs outside the school- room: it is this upbringing that leads a man to become aware of his fate and motivates him to partici- pate in and enjoy community life and cultural benefits, to express himself and to be at one with his ellow man.
12.16 In this way, culture has become the basis of common experience, which in turn can ultimately express constructive views on the proper course and correct attitude to science, which I believe to be the crucial predicament of the century. I do not ignore for a moment the great political problems involved in the issue. But I thought it pertinent to raise these issues here, to give a picture of
17 the dangerous gap between today's science and its threat to culture and to emphasize the fact that the problems arising from these very real situations should be attacked properly and methodically. see Unesco as the only Organization fitted to undertake this action. Thank you, Mr. President.
13. Le PRESIDENT. - Je remercie le chef de la délégation de la Grèce. La séance est levée.
