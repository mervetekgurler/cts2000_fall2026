Draft resolution presented by Iraq [document  bC/bl].  [13] Dr. JAMALI (Iraq) regretted that this prob-  lem had not received the priority due to it in the  discussions. The position of those refugees had to  be settled before a new world could be built up.  The importance of Unesco’s work in Germany and  Japan had been discussed at length, but such  work was, to say the least of it, equally important  in Iraq and Palestine. He therefore hoped that it  would meet with the same reception from the  Conference. He described the pitiable situation  of millions of refugees driven from their homes  as a result, not of war, but of decisions voted by  the United Nations, the champions of peace and  democracy. Without wishing to dwell on the
436  political aspect of the matter, Dr. Jamali felt that  the Commission should realize that this was a  humanitarian problem. The very ideals of Unesco  would be in jeopardy unless speedy relief were  given in that part of the world. The Iraqi delega-  tion considered that the problem should be given  priority over many other resolutions voted, the  implementation of which could wait until the  following year without loss. Dr. Jamali con-  cluded by hoping that the Commission would  devote to this question the attention it deserved.  [14] Sir John MAUD (Chairman, Executive  Board) observed that the Board had considered  the matter carefully. It fully appreciated its im-  portance, but unhappily, with the absurdly small  resources at its disposal, the Organization could  not satisfy such extensive needs, either in the  Near East or elsewhere. The amount for direct  assistance provided for 1951 out of the Emergency  relief Fund was only $188,000, and the principle  had already been adopted that the most effective  relief that Unesco could give to victims of disasters  was to make enquiries about needs, and then  launch appeals to governments and non-  governmental organizations to finance the work.  The Iraqi draft resolution called for a grant of  funds over and above the amounts already con-  tained in the budget for direct assistance and,  despite its sympathy with the refugees, the Ex-  ecutive Board had been unable to support the  project. The Commission was of course free to  adopt the resolution and provide a budget, but  the Chairman of the Board proposed that it should  adopt the same procedure as in the case of the  Mexican and Venezuelan scheme, leaving it to the  Director-General and the Board to assign part of  the direct assistance credits to that project.  [15] Professor PHOTIADES (Greece) recalled  that reconstruction problems had appeared in the  Organization’s programme since its earliest days  —indeed ever since the preparatory period. They  had been converted into problems of relief assist-  ance, and he had no objection to the change,  provided such assistance was made effective.  Millions had already been spent on purposes not  always in conformity with the Organization's  true aims. Professor Photiades called to mind the  appeal made by Unesco on behalf of Greek chil-  dren in the Near East, and urged that that appeal  should be renewed in still more striking and  eloquent terms. It was not through its ordinary  budget that Unesco could furnish relief, but by  using its moral influence. It must accomplish  its most elementary duty towards those destitute  children by rising to the demands of its educa-  tional mission.  [16] Dr. SIDDIQUI (Pakistan) associated him-     m c                               self with what Dr. Jamali had said. The prob-  lem of the Arab child refugees from Palestine was  a grave one and demanded an urgent solution for  the sake of world peace. A comparison had been  drawn with the proposal made by the Mexican  and Venezuelan delegates on behalf of the historic  monuments of Cuzco, but monuments could not  be compared with children, whose situation was  far more tragic and called for urgent relief. The  $188,000 provided for emergency relief re-  prepresented only a very small fraction of the  total Unesco Budget, and he urged that other  activities should be dropped in favour of the im-  mediate action which was indispensable in this  matter.  [17] Dr. ZOOK (United States of America) had  very great sympathy with the Palestine Arab  refugees, whose sad plight had come to his notice  at the Beirut Conference. He was proud to be  able to state that Unesco had already taken direct  action to improve the position. The adoption of  the amendments to resolutions 7.11, 7.31 and 7.32  made action by Unesco possible in several of those  countries on the basis of voluntary assistance. The  funds which could be collected in that way were  sure to be far in excess of any relief that Unesco  could give directly. He therefore recommended  that, instead of asking for an increase in the  amount set aside for direct assistance, the Com-  mission should pay closer attention to the collec-  tion of money from non-governmental organiza-  tions.  [18] Mgr. MAROUN (Lebanon) expressed his  gratitude for the help given each year by Unesco  to the Palestine refugees and to Greek children.  Experts had been sent to the spot, and an appeal  addressed to Member States. The Executive  Board had studied the problem sympathetically  and effective aid had been given to the refugees,  but such aid was not in proportion to their im-  mense needs. Although he could not expect  Unesco to find a complete solution to this dis-  tressing problem, Mgr. Maroun advocated the  renewal of the Director-General’s appeal to Mem-  ber States. Moreover, he considered that the time  had come to give more concrete proof of sym-  pathy by making an appeal to the United Nations,  with a view to the return of those refugee popula-  tions to their respective countries.  The countries which had opened their doors  to the refugees could not continue to help them,  and he mentioned the case of the Catholic Recep-  tion Committee, in Lebanon, which alone, in the  course of two years, had spent 1,200,000 Lebanese  pounds for the instruction of refugee children.  Mgr. Maroun fully supported Dr. Jamali’s state-  ments, and thanked those who had taken such a  human interest in that distressing problem.
