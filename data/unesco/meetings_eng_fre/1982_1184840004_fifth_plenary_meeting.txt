1.1 The PRESIDENT:  Ladies and gentlemen, I wish you all a very good morning, and I declare open this fifth  plenary meeting. I should first like to draw your attention to an item of business concerning the  arrears of contributions of one Member State. Treatment of this question is outstanding from our  first meeting on 23 November 1982. At that time, I mentioned that a letter had been received  from the Minister of Education of the Republic of Sierra Leone, invoking the provisions of  Article IV C, paragraph 8(c), of the Constitution. This letter is now before you as an annex to  document 4 XC/8/Add.2.
1.2 You will recall that in Article IV C, paragraph 8(b), of the Constitution, the right to vote  is made conditional upon the payment by Member States of their financial contributions. The text  in question reads as follows; 'A Member State shall have no vote in the General Conference if  the total amount of contributions due from it exceeds the total amount of contributions payable  by it for the current year and the immediately preceding calendar year'.
1.3 On the other hand, as we saw on 23 November, paragraph 8(c) of Article IV C of the  Constitution provides that: 'The General Conference may nevertheless permit such a Member  State to vote, if it is satisfied that failure to pay is due to conditions beyond the control of the  Member Nation’.
1.4 Is it your wish that Sierra Leone be permitted to vote at this session of the General  Conference? I see no objections.
1.5 It is so decided.
1.6 Now, ladies and gentlemen, as I announced to you yesterday morning, H.E. Mr Constantine  Karamanlis, President of the Hellenic Republic, will address the General Conference at
12.15 p.m. today. The meeting will be suspended 45 minutes earlier, at 11.30 a.m., and will  resume at 12.15. All delegates are requested to return to their seats by 12 noon at the latest,  so that the ceremonial entry of President Karamanlis may proceed smoothly. Thank you. 2. The PRESIDENT:  We shall now resume the general debate, and I give the floor to H.E. Mr Alfredo Tarre  Murzi, Ambassador, Permanent Delegate of Venezuela to Unesco and Head of the Delegation of  Venezuela,
(3.1) Mr TARRE MURZI (Venezuela) (Translation from the Arabic):  Mr President, Mr Director-General, distinguished delegates, allow me to express the warm  congratulations of the delegation of Venezuela to you, Mr President, on your re-election to the Presidency  of the General Conference, in which you will once again demonstrate--as you did at the last General  Conference in Belgrade--your outstanding ability to guide our discussions intelligently, seriously and  responsibly.
(3.2) This General Conference is meeting at the end of a difficult and sombre year, which has seen the  problems facing mankind grow deeper and more acute, seriously complicating not only the tasks of states  but also the role of the organizations of the United Nations system.
(3.3) Perhaps in the present international situation it is Unesco's duty to suggest goals to Member States  and offer the peoples of the world some positive hopes in accordance with the spirit of our Organization's  Constitution, which stands for the moral and intellectual progress of the international community. As Unesco  is a body concerned with culture, education, science, technology and communication, it therefore bears the  immense responsibility for guiding and illuminating mankind at a dramatic moment in its history.
(3.4) We therefore regard this fourth extraordinary session of the General Conference not simply as a  milestone in the programme of meetings organized by Unesco to examine the Medium-Term Plan but as a  valuable and unique opportunity to reaffirm Unesco's historic and dialectical mission in the context of the  crisis which is today affecting the nations of North and South, East and West.
(3.5) If we wish to be honest and realistic when discussing and adopting the Medium-Term Plan, we cannot  pass over the true reasons for the crisis and must make a sincere attempt to unravel the causes and  consequences of the serious problems that confront us, while at the same time constantly seeking  convergence and consensus among the different elements of the international community. This is where  Unesco's essential role lies: fostering peace, coexistence and détente in the realm of the spirit, which  should be the ultimate aim of any educational, scientific, technological and cultural programme.
(3.6) At the same time, the planning process must be ruthless in eliminating unnecessary rhetoric and  bureaucracy so that the Member States of Unesco may have full confidence in a Medium-Term Plan that is  designed to solve the most urgent problems facing peoples and governments in virtually every part of the  world. Planning must therefore be global, coherent and pragmatic, and based on a scale of priorities,  starting with the elimination of all the ills which affect the developing countries of the Third World.
(3.7) We, the countries of the Third World, believe that top priority should be given to endogenous  development, which should always have a social and cultural significance and dimension. At the same time  we think that Unesco's action should be inspired by the principles of the new international economic order  and of international social justice, so as to bring about more balanced and equitable forms of exchange with  the aim of climinating colonialism, which continues to be the main source of backwardness and poverty  among many peoples of the world.
(3.8) It is true, as the Medium-Term Plan asserts, that the point of departure for the planning process  should be the analysis of world problems. But Unesco should not accept institutional subterfuge or excuses  designed to avoid a genuine debate which would reveal the true source of the problems concerned. We must  recognize that violence, interventionism and war continue to challenge the moral authority of the United  Nations as a factor of coexistence and harmony, as we have seen this year in the tragic conflict in the  South Atlantic, in the violent hostilities in the Middle East and in the constant bloodshed in Central  America. If we do not have confidence in the United Nations and its Specialized Agencies, and if its norms  and resolutions are flouted, the whole system of international organizations is thrown into disrepute and  undermined, to the detriment of peace, justice and culture. Man then resorts to brute force and relapses  into barbarism.
(3.9) Alongside the political crisis there is a worldwide financial and economic crisis; but we cannot and  must not accept the argument that this crisis should cause Unesco to limit the scope of its Medium-Term  Plan. It is the developing countries that are most affected by the deterioration of the international economic  situation. We know that the industrialized nations are going through a critical period of deep depression,  but the crisis hits the Third World countries even harder.
(3.10) The slump in the price of raw materials, the decline in international trade, low gross national  product growth rates, the revival of protectionism, monetary and currency instability, sharp and  unexpected balance of payments fluctuations, high levels of indebtedness, the high cost of debt servicing,  the slowing down of new investment and the increase in financial speculation are creating a most serious  situation for all the developing countries at the same time as the major powers are increasing military  expenditure and accelerating the arms race. This is the crux of the crisis, which cannot be resolved by  limiting social and cultural expenditure or denying Unesco the resources which it requires in order to  implement its programme.
(3.11) It is for this reason that we in the developing countries--who are suffering a financial crisis with  terrible social and political repercussions~-are not prepared to accept cuts in the Medium-Term Plan which  would compromise the integrity and comprehensiveness of Unesco's programme.
(3.12) We consider that, once they have been duly examined, the draft resolutions relating to the  Medium-Term Plan (1984-1439) submitted by the Director-General with a view to facilitating the work of this  Conference should be approved, together with Unesco's programme of future-oriented studies of world  problems, so that we may be in a position to predict future trends and act promptly to avoid aggravating  the difficulties which are so evident in the world today.
(3.13) The Director-General und his colleagues are to be congratulated not only on their preparation of  these draft resolutions, but also on the careful and responsible way in which they have guided Unesco's  preparation, since the Nairobi General Conference, of the series of instruments which make up the  Medium-Term Plan. Naturally, this Conference has sovereign powers to make amendments and changes to  improve the programme from the geographical, technical and practical standpoints. But I think thai no-one  will have any difficulty in approving without reservation the five tasks or objectives which the  Director-General has proposed so that Unesco may contribute within its fields of ccompetence towards the  solution of the problems which face mankind today.
(3.14) We must also congratulate the members of the Executive Board for their outstanding appraisal of the  Draft Medium-Term Plan at the 115th session of the Board and for the pertinence of their observations and  recommendations to the General Conference--designed to ensure a more comprehensive and coherent  programme--which have laid the basis for a broad consensus among the participating delegations at the  fourth extraordinary session. lt is to be hoped that the additional proposals made by certain memebrs of  the Executive Board will also help to improve and extend the thirteen major programmes drawn up within  the framework of the five tasks identified as the basis for Unesco's future activities. At this crucial and  dangerous moment in history, it would be highly regrettable if proposals were made at this Conference to  limit or curb Unesco's distinguished role in activities in the international sphere.
(3.15) Mr President, my delegation attaches great importance to the question of human rights and the  rights of peoples. As has been pointed out by the Group of 77, of which I have the honour to be the  President, respect for human rights and for the right of peoples to self-determination represent irreversible  gains by the international community. These rights are closely linked: the violation of the rights of peoples  gravely affects the exercise of human rights; similarly, the systematic violation of human rights denies the  aforementioned right of peoples to self-determination. We believe that under no circumstances should the  right of peoples or that of self-determination by every state be used to justify the violation of individual  freedoms or human rights. If this principle is not established clearly, any national, regional or multilateral  action to further the advancement of the international community and to promote peace among the nations  would be fruitless or incomplete.
(3.16) The Government of Venezuela approves of Major Programme II, 'Education for all', Major  Programme IV, 'The formulation and application of education policies! and Major Programme V, ‘Education,  training and society'. We believe these three programmes to be of fundamental and priority importance in  the immense task of eradicating illiteracy in the world and eliminating inequalities of access and opportunity  in the field of education. We would also give priority to the universalization of education, which should be  Unesco's main objective throughout the world.
(3.17) Perhaps it would be relevant to quote the experience of my own country, which attaches great  importance to basic education as the foundation of any development programme. We have managed to lower  the illiteracy rate considerably using a basic school curriculum that includes the language, art education,  vocational training, training in thinking, community action, national identity studies, health education,  physical education, recreation, the natural sciences and mathematics. The curriculum is studied over a  nine-year course, which is divided into three parts and always takes account of the a pupil's level of  intellectual and emotional maturity and motivation. Pilot projects in basic education have been extended to  rural areas of the country with the establishment of thousands of country schools operating under the  authority of the Department of Rural School Centres for Integrated Development. The adult literacy  programme has also been intensified through the provision of additional vocational training and guidance  courses. This is the true meaning of the democratization of education--a close concern with human potential  as the key to civic participation and accelerated social and economic development, Through the Office of the  Regional Co-ordinator for Latin America and the Caribbean and the Regional Centre for Higher Education in  Latin America and the Caribbean Region, both based in Venezuela, Unesco is a driving force in the  struggle to develop education in Latin America. We have here a clear illustration of how a Member State of  Unesco can serve the Organization by means of its models, experiments, services and resources. Venezuela  thus has experience to offer which could be useful for international co-operation in the field of basic  education.
(3.18) Major Programmes VI and IX on the sciences and their application to development and on the  relationship between science, technology and society merit our special attention, because it is only by  strengthening national research potential and improving infrastructures and technical services that we can  transform our dependent economies and overcome our critical backwardness in the production of consumer  goods. The training of key personnel and skilled manpower is essential for the Third World, and it is  Unesco's task to assist us in the rational exploitation of our basic products through technologies which  serve to further tke progress of each nation, rather than the interests of the transnational corporations.
(3.19) In that connection, we consider that Major Programme VIII, Principles, methods and strategies of  action for development', may well be an additional programme of great significance. So far the objectives of  the International Development Strategy for the Third United Nations Development Decade have not been  attained and Unesco's Medium-Term Plan therefore provides an excellent opportunity to finalize development  planning, to expose foreign pressures and obstacles that stand in the way of autonomous development and  to mobilize human and financial resources for the development of the nations of Africa, Asia and Latin  America.
(3.20) In this respect the fourth extraordinary session of the General Conference should take note of the  gravity of the situation which has arisen as a result of the pressures being exerted in connection with the  global debt of the developing countries, which has given rise to actions motivated by other than financial
5   considerations and which places an unbearable burden on the foreign exchange situation of the Third World  countries. In Latin America alone the debt will amount to 280 thousand million dollars this year; and the  amortization of that debt represents the equivalent of 56 per cent of Latin American exports, a situation  which could have grave political and social consequences.
(3.21) For that reason the Third World countries must make strenuous efforts to strengthen their  economies by increasing industrial and agricultural productivity, by improving the skilled manpower ratio  and by making rational use of financial resources. To this end, Unesco could do much to lessen the social  impact of the foreign debt crisis of the developing countries, which is giving rise to a most serious and  vexatious form of neo-colonialist intervention.
(3.22) Mr President, I shall conclude with a brief reference to the programmes on culture and  communication, We should be entirely satisfied if the fourth extraordinary General Conference were to  integrate the fundamental principles embodied in the Mexico City Declaration with the additional proposals  contained in Major Programme XI on 'Culture and the future'. The World Conference on Cultural Policies  held in Mexico City, provided a valuable insight into the preservation and dissemination of fundamental  values such as cultural sovereignty, cultural identity, the safeguarding of the cultural heritage and freedom  of creation and expression through the development of art and literature, which not only foster peaceful  coexistence between peoples but also enhance the moral and intellectual stature of the developing countries.
(3.23) Alongside all this, there is a need to place communication in the service of man, as laid down in  Major Programme III of the Medium-Term Plan. Unesco ‘should pursue its work and programmes aimed at  promoting the free exchange of ideas and knowledge, more ready public access to information, a more  balanced two-way flow of information and freedom of expression. We must therefore support to the full the  work of the International Programme for the Development of Communication (IPDC) by defending Unesco  ‘whenever voices are raised in criticism of this programme which has been satisfactorily included in the  Medium-Term Plan.
(3.24) In this turbulent and complex world, full of problems and tensions, Unesco must continue to be a  source of ideas and guidance, an instrument of peace, a forum for the advancement of mankind and the  centre of the broadest possible international movement for the promotion of the spiritual well being of the  peoples of the world. We must continue to pursue this aim with unflagging energy and singleness of  purpose. This is our destiny if we truly seek to ensure a better life for future generations. Thank you,  Mr Chairman.
4. The PRESIDENT:  I thank the Head of the Delegation of Venezuela. The next speaker on my list is the  representative of the United Nations Environment Programme, Mr Golubev. He has the floor.

UNEP always welcomes any  chance of establishing closer mutual understanding with Unesco, considering the development of links to be  an extension of its catalytic and co-ordinating role in environmental issues. UNEP's activities are in  implementation of United Nations General Assembly resolution 2997 (XXVII) of 15 December 1972 establishing  the Environment Programme, which stated that UNEP should 'provide general policy guidance for the  direction and co-ordination of environment programmes within the United Nations system'. In the same  resolution, the General Assembly invited the organizations of the United Nations system to ‘adopt the  measures that may be required to undertake concerted and co-ordinated programmes with regard to  environmental problems...’
(5.2) Since this Conference comes in the wake of the commemoration in Nairobi in May-June 1982 of the  tenth anniversary of the United Nations Conference cn the Human Environment (1972), I have the chance to  tell you about some of our achievements. The special session of the Governing Council and the tenth session  which followed, gave governments an opportunity to review the main accomplishments, as well as the  failures, regarding the environment during the ten years which have clapsed since the Stockholm  Conference. They were also able to make recommendations concerning future prospects, action and  international co-operation regarding the environment and the main environmental trends with which UNEP  will be concerned in the next ten years. I should like to emphasize that the growing awareness of  environmental issues has been achieved not only through the efforts of UNEP and other international  organizations but also through the actions of Member States and non-governmental organizations. At the  commemorative session, the conclusion was reached that despite past achievements, the ecological situation  in the early 1980s was gloomy in many respects. While certain threats to the environment identified in 1972  could now be seen as less serious, other problems remained, some had worsened, and new ones had arisen.
(5.3) Now, as in the past, environmental protection and enhancement must be organized on a preventive  basis, which means that all parties concerned must engage in interdisciplinary planning and programming,  and also take account of ccological considerations in all major activities with an actual or potential impact on  the environment. Special emphasis has been placed on the crucial importance of exchanging information  through the wide circulation of the results of our work. Overcoming these problems is therefore our task  for the future, and in this respect Unesco could make a substantial contribution.

The Memorandum of Understanding b>tween Unesco and UNEP, which defines  areas and methods of co-operation, is based on this premise and was designed to provide a framework of  co-operation between the two organizations. The Memorandum of Understanding assists the implementation of  the resolutions and decisions of the General Assembly and of the governing bodies of Unesco and UNEP,  providing a framework for continuing co-operation based on that mutual understanding.

Le Plan à moyen terme que nous allons adopter va constituer, pendant six années, le  cadre conceptuel de tous les programmes d'activité que nous aurons à définir et qui pourront  ainsi être heureusement concentrés autour de quelques axes principaux, harmonisés par une  vision d'ensemble.
11.4 Il est vrai que ce Plan pour la période 1984-1989 ne sera pas le tout premier du genre mais  il est tout aussi clair que la méthode qui a présidé à son élaboration, très largement novatrice,  ainsi que l'effort considérable accompli pour assurer sa cohérence interne n'ont guère de précé-  dent et montrent en tout cas que le processus de planification à l'Unesco est maintenant parvenu  à maturité. Le succès de la méthode d'élaboration réside essentiellement dans l'ampleur et la  qualité de la consultation réalisée auprès des Etats membres, de leurs commissions nationales et  des autres instances, parmi lesquelles les organisations non gouvernementales, qui ont largement  répondu à cet appel. Chacun de nous sait bien comme il peut être délicat d'animer une concerta-  tion démocratique qui de surcroît aboutit à une telle abondance de suggestions et d'idées. L'effort
136   de cohérence n'en est que plus difficile, mais il a porté ses fruits et cela apparait non sculement  à travers la synthèse des réponses, mais encore dans la structure, je devrais même dire l'archi-  tecture, du document qui nous est présenté.

11.6 La lecture attentive de la "problématique mondiale" m'amène maintenant à formuler trois  observations. La première est que, de l'avis de tous, notre monde est confronté à un ensemble de  "défis", les uns face à des dangers évidents, les autres en présence d'évolutions qui pourraient  constituer des progrès mais dont la maîtrise semble nous échapper et qui risquent ainsi de com-  porter de redoutables contreparties.
11.7 Parmi les dangers évidents, on ne peut que mentionner en premier lieu les menaces qui  pèsent sur la paix dans l'ensemble de la planète, les atteintes répétées aux droits de l'homme et  aux droits des peuples, dont l'apartheid constitue à n'en pas douter l'un des cus les plus  dramatiques.

La France, dont je n'ai pas besoin de rappeler une fois de plus le sou-  tien qu'elle apporte à l'action spécifique de l'Unesco dans tous ses domaines de compétence  - culturelle, scientifique et touchant aux problèmes d'éducation - ne croit pas que la dispension  des efforts de l'Unesco serve les intérêts et l'efficacité de l'Organisation. Elle sera donc attentive  à tout ce qui pourra être fait en coordination avec le Centre du désarmement des Nations Unies,  comme l'a spécifiquement demandé l'Assemblée générale des Nations Unies lors de la deuxième ses-  sion extraordinaire consacrée au désarmement, pour ce qui est des tâches que l'Unesco peut, dans  les domaines où sa compétence est reconnue, remplir mieux que quiconque, et je pense notamment  à l'élaboration d'une pédagogie sur ces questions comme sur celles des droits de l'homme et de la  non-discrimination raciale.

Réponses qui ne doivent  pas être isolées, sectorisées, mais qui doivent sous-tendre et même fonder les autres analyses.

13.7 It is a well recognized fact of our times that the issues of peace and prosperity are inter-  related. 20.1 Mr Constantile KARAMANLIS, President of the Hellenic Republic  (Speech delivered in Greek, English text supplied):  Mr President, Mr Director-General, Your Excellencies, ladies and gentlemen, I particularly  welcome the opportunity offered to me today to take part in this session of your General  Conference, and to address from this podium such a distinguished gathering of eminent  representatives of all nations. 1 owe this opportunity to Mr M'Bow, the Director-General of  Unesco, who was kind enough to invite me to visit Unesco today. I should like to thank him  warmly for this invitation and for the kind words he has spoken about my country and myself.  My warmest thanks also got to Mr Margan, President of the General Conference, and a member of  the government of a friendly neighbouring country, for his hearty welcome to me.
20.2 I do not think it necessary for me, ladies and gentlemen, as a representative of Greece, to  proclaim my country's faith in the principles of the United Nations nor to stress the great  importance we Greeks attach to Unesco's work. We are a small country with a centuries-old  history behind it which has contributed a great deal to the cultural heritage of mankind and to  the promotion of man by establishing those high principles which Unesco is striving to uphold  and which are shared by nearly all great civilizations, whatever form they may have acquired.
20.3 Man's tendency to seek cultural values and to build his life on them is precisely what sets  him apart from all other animate and inanimate beings in this world. As Malraux said; 'La culture  nous apparaît d'abord comme la connaissance de ce qui a fait de l'homne autre chose qu'un  accident de l'univers'.
20.4 In the infinite course of prehistoric time, man slowly drew away from all other beings,  from all other organisms, and set upon his own course. He stopped being simply part of nature  and became history. At the same time he stopped being a simple genius or species and became an  individual. Instead of only observing the ouiside world, he looked at himself. He became  conscious of himself. And at the same time he began emancipating himself from his surroundings  and acquired the sense of his own freedom both in thought and in action. Placing his continually  developing consciousness in juxtaposition to objects, he began to judge which of them had value  for him and which did not. He thus formed a world of values and, by setting these in order of  importance, acquired certain primary values which were for him immovable and which are, I  believe, eternal.
20.5 This centuries-old struggle of mankind to approach in thought and in action the highest  values--a struggle which each people carried out in its own way--is the very civilization of  mankind for whose preservation and promotion i all its forms Unesco is working.
20.6 Encompassing all these forms, Unesco not only shows that it stands above racial,  geographic and ideological discrimination but also reveals--and this is most important of all--that  behind all differences there are some points of encounter for all men upon which the mutual  understanding of peoples can be founded and, through this, world peace and justice.
20.7 This is pre:isely the great mission of your Organization. With the dissemination of  knowledge, which is the prerequisite of self-definition, of technology, which is the basis of  material liberation and of art, which confirms the identity of men, the ideal of the community of  mankind is forged and the value of man is recognized. The dialogue among peoples and  civilizations, the linking of the past with the present, of tradition with progress, of the  individual with the community, promotes mutual respect and understanding, cultivates a  consciousness of international unity and upholds peace. And here I should like to digress for a  moment, ladies and gentlemen, and tell you that my written statement says 'democracy' instead of  'peace'. When I just said 'peace', it was indeed peace that I had in mind. The question of peace  is the most important question of our age.
20.8 At the threshold of the twenty-first century, this role of Unesco's is decisive because  Unesco's aims tend to be identified with liberty, but mainly because they tend to reconcile  politics with ethics and to form a new world ethos.
20.9 It is a well-known fact that the effect of civilization on human life is decisive. In material  terms, civilization, or the struggle of man against nature, has lessened his labours and improved  his living conditions. In cultural terms, civilization, or the struggle of man with himself, has led  to a common choice of values and institutions which constitute the ideal of human society. To  these values, upon which the European civilization was founded, the Greek spirit gave a  universal dimension.
20.10 Ladies and gentlemen, I have not come here to praise my country's history. This has  already been done by many in the forefront of culture and, among them, Valery, who said:  "Toute race, toute terre qui a ete successivement romanisée, christianisée et soumise, quant à  l'esprit, à la discipline des Grecs, est absolument européenne'.
20.11 It is Greek philosophy which restored the dialogue between man and nature and between  man and himself and revealed the correlation between the self and the non-self. It aimed at the  value of man as man, it created the universal system of meanings and concepts and put forward  reason as a bridge for communication, far from dogmas and myths.
20.12 Moreover, the great political achievement of the Greeks is that they gave us the political  ideal, the spirit of freedom and democracy. Within the city, the citizen was born with absolute  equality of rights and freedom of speech. A balanced relationship was formed between the  individual and society and a dialogue between the citizen and the state was organized. From civil  life, civilization was born.
20.13 Finally, Greek classical art does not portray any specific figure, any historical or  geographic form. It depicts the ideal, the impersonal man as an idea, as beauty itself. Historical  knowledge is not required to understand its portrayals. Greek art has a universal meaning.
20.14 Ancient Greek culture moved in the same direction and it has become topical today for  this very reason, The aim of Greek culture was to teach 'living according to nature, that is with  virtue', and through virtue to achieve happiness. But virtue is tested and perfected within the  city. There, its task is 'greater and more complete’, as Aristotle put it. The highest ideal was  the shaping of 'a good citizen', in other words the creation of 'a good and virtuous man'.
20.15 As for the significance of Greek civilization to modern culture, I shall refer to the  brilliant address of the Director-General of Unesco, Mr M'Bow, to the Academy of Athens, where  he presented, in all its depth, the significance of Greek civilization and its importance to modern  man. Referring to Greek culture and its importance to the modern world, Mr M'Bow recalled the  link that still existed today 'between the educational ideal that established itself in Athens  during the golden age of Greece, and that which it is today our calling to promote on a  worldwide scale' and the need to 'refer back to a culture that [acknowledged] the central part  played by man, free, creative'... man. Mr M'Bow also told us that 'the educational ideal that  ancient Greece has bequeathed to us' had now been raised by Unesco to a universal ideal.
20.16 I have already referred to European civilization, which is based on Greek civilization. If T  were asked to say in one word what stands at the core of European civilization, I would say that  it is the individual as a free, logical being; the individual whe was endowed with reason by  Greek thought and with an immortal soul by Christianity; the individual who, through reason, is  constantly in search of and advancing towards new conquests.
20.17 The European, in the course of the centuries, has managed to combine Greco-Roman  thought with the message of Christianity, and to give to his civilization a specificity which  distinguishes it from other great civilizations: civilizations about which I am not in a position to  speak, although I have the deepest respect for their achievements.
20.18 Ladies and gentlemen, it is common knowledge that mankind is today traversing a deep  and dangerous crisis: a political, economic and, particularly, cultural crisis. It is going through  a difficult period of transition which is marked by universal and intense anxiety: an anxiety  which manifests itself in various forms, ranging from anguish to violence. Because man, in our  time, wishes to move away from one way of life and is seeking a new one, He mistrusts existing  institutions and seeks new ones, He is looking for new values without, however, being able to  define either their form or their content.
20.19 In other words, he is going through a cultural crisis which is the source of the  prevailing confusion in the world today and of the problems we are facing. A crisis which  threatens to shake the most fundamental values in our lives, such as freedom, justice and peace.
20.20 At no time in history has man known as much as he does today, thanks to the tremendous  progress of the applied sciences. But not only has his cultural progress failed to keep pace with  this development, not only has it fallen behind; it has been opporessed by it. We know that  education is not always equivalent to culture. Knowledge becomes sterile when it is not filtered  through the intellectual process which defines its human mission. Factual knowledge alone does  not promote human culture. Beyond knowledge, moral and spiritual guidance is also necessary.  In other words, we need that more profound education which designates man's attitude to the  highest values of life, not only those of the intellect but those of ethos as well.
20.21 These great truths are apparently ignored in our materialistic age. That is why humanity  appears lost and all at sea. One indeed has the feeling that it has lost its sense of direction and  is sailing like a ship without a compass.
20.22 The confusion and, I would say, the moral and spiritual anarchy that prevail in the world  today are assuming such a form and such dimensions that one has the impression that we are  seeking our own destruction. Not only do we not take the necessary steps to cure the causes of  our present problems; through our own selfish behaviour, we are making these problems more  acute.
20.23 While I could mention many examples which confirm these truths, I will confine myself to  those that are most characteristic.
20.24 We discuss daily the danger of a nuclear war. And although we know in advance what its  consequences would be, we face this danger almost with apathy, as if our spirit and our soul  were paralysed.
20.25 We talk about disarmament every day, and yet we con,.nuously and dangerously add more  weapons to our armouries. In 1980, more than 500 billion dollars were spent on arms. These  sums are spent for purposes of war when about three-quarters of the world's population is  hungry or underfed, and when no people free from noisome influences wants war. It is natural  for them not to want war, because--as Herodotus says--in wartime, parents bury their children:  in peacetime, children bury their parents.
20.26 We talk daily about the inequality between North and South which, combined with the  population problem, has assumed explosive proportions during the last few years. Every year,  the poor become poorer, more numerous and also more aggressive, while we resort to wishful  phrases, even though we know that this inequality, which is continually growing, will upset the  balance of the world and ultimately threaten peace. Sooner or later, the poor will join together  against the rich, and the differences between North and South will become more acute as a  result.
20.27 At the same time, under the pressure of these differences, the rivalry between East and  West will be lessened. And it can be lessened, because both super-powers know that a nuclear  war between them is inconceivable because they also know that nobody can conquer the world,  and especially because they know that even if the world is conquered, it cannot be governed by  either of them.
20.28 1 will add another example, ladies and gentlemen, which, in a way, covers those I have  given already. It is a well-known fact that the permanent and greatest problems with which  humanity has concerned itself in vain throughout the centuries are peace and human misery. The  simultaneous solution of both these problems is not merely possible: it would be easy, if reason  and good faith were to prevail. If military expenditures were progressively reduced, on the one  hand, the dangers which threaten peace would be limited and, on the other hand, those  resources would be created which are essential for curing the ills which beset humanity--hunger  and disease in particular. In spite of this, the paranoia which prevails in our time is such that  this simple and logical solution cannot be attained.
20.29 Speaking before the General Conference of Unesco, I am aware that I am addressing a cul-  tured gathering which is seeking a cure for the crisis we are going through today with the most  effective medicine, that is to say the promotion of culture and the projection, through education,  of those values which will extricate us from our present impasse. This effort must be supported  by all those who are responsible for the fate of the world. In other words, by its political and  spiritual leadership.
20.30 In order to succeed in this effort, however, we must seek not only ar effective procedure  but also safe and tried models, and you will forgive me, ladies and gentlemen, if I refer once  again to Greek civilization in maintaining that we can draw from it lessons whose wisdom has  been confirmed by the ages.
20.31 Against the passion of extremism, let us raise the eternal principle of moderation through  a dialogue between free-thinking people. Let us accept disagreements with toleration and let us  moderate them, in the knowledge that disagreements, too, enrich the human spirit. Let us res-  pect each man as if he were the expression of the whole of mankind. Let us build between strict  logic and unreasoning sentiment a bridge which can lead to harmony between opposites and to  Aristotelian measure. Let us become citizens of our city, as Thucydides and Pericles wished us  to be, and citizens of all the world, as Diogenes and Zeno wanted.
20.32 And, through all this, let us endeavour to give a clearer form to the individuality of man  and a deeper meaning to his spiritual and social freedom. Because these are the most permanent  and most profound meanings of true civilization.
20.33 Ladies and gentlemen, Unesco is a sort of 'bank' where all the peoples of the world  deposit their cultural capital in order to create with it the cultural 'goods' that are necessary for  the future course of humanity. For her part, Greece wishes to serve this aim with all that she  has that is good and beautiful. She comes with her natural environment, which was the great  teacher and determining factor of her civilization. She comes with her classical centuries, the  wealth of which she shares today with the whole world. She comes with her Hellenistic Age,  which brought Greek civilization to Asia, and with Byzantium, which opened the way for  Christianity, Especially, she comes with her profound faith in those eternal values which,  revitalized, can reshape modern life.
20.34 She comes, ladies and gentlemen, in this spirit to this temple of the spirit, in order to  take part in the noblest contest of our time, bringing with her a three thousand year-old history  together with her modern, live presence and contribution. She comes, finally, to serve the civili-  zation of man and to contribute to the great tagk of Unesco. A difficult task which, however,  can become easy if we all believe in jt. Because, after all, faith is identical with will. Was it not  Sophocles who said: 'That which is sought is attainable, while that which is neglected, escapes’.
