[1] The CHAIRMAN welcomed Senator William  Benton, a member of the United States delegation  and a pioneer of Unesco, which he had served  from its earliest days, both abroad and in his own  country. Mr. Benton had attended the Prepara-  tory Conference in London and had headed his  country’s delegation to the Conferences in Paris
(1946) and Mexico (1947). As Secretary of State  for Cultural Affairs, Mr. Benton had strongly en-  couraged all work connected with Unesco and had  personally contributed to the development of the  United States National Commission.  The Chairman called on Mr. Benton to present  a draft resolution [document 5C/59] framed by  the United States delegation and requesting the  adoption by Unesco of exceptional measures to  cope with an exceptional world situation.  The Chairman was sorry he could not open a  discussion on the draft that day, but nevertheless  thought it worth the Commission’s while to hear  Mr. Benton's statement forthwith.  [2] Mr. BENTON (United States of America)  regretted that it had been impossible for him to  join earlier that particularly important Session of  the General Conference. In recent weeks the  United States Senate had approved the extention  of ERP (European Recovery Programme), and  had discussed President Truman’s Point Four  proposals, in which Unesco would have an impor-  tant share. Part of a Senate resolution he had  been sponsoring called upon all Member States to  assist an acceleration of Unesco’s work so as to  make it a significant and perhaps decisive con-  tribution to peace. He urged a return to funda-  mentals and pointed out that in Unesco, educa-  tion, science and culture were not ends in them-  selves but means of making a direct contribution  to peace. This contribution, however, could  only be made by strict concentration and integra-  tion of the Programme. He quoted from Article I,  paragraph 1 of Unesco’s Constitution, and from  Articles 19, 26 and 27 of the Universal Declaration  of Human Rights, and emphasized that those in  Unesco who denied these fundamental principles  should not be permitted to retard the Organiza-  tion on the way to its great objective.  The political struggle raging in the world was  an intellectual, moral and educational struggle;  Unesco could not remain indifferent to the pres-  sures by which the minds of men were assailed  from all sides. Mr. Benton hoped the Conference  would agree with the Director-Generals argu-  ments for Unesco’s right to an active role in world  politics in order to ensure the implementation of  Article 26, i.e., “to promote understanding, toler-  ance and friendship among all nations”. The  occasionally expressed theory that Unesco must  soften its policies so that it could be a bridge be-  tween East and West was a chimera. The present  world situation called for measures over and  above the normal Programme and Budget; they  should represent a constructive and positive  affirmation of freedom for the sake of freedom  and an effort to bolster the fundamental freedoms  wherever they were threatened. Unesco must find  a way to tap all the resources of the free world,  and learn to use every means of mass communica-  tion to tell the glorious story of freedom. [6] Mr. SEDAR SENGHOR (France) remarked  that his task had been facilitated by the state-  ment of the United States delegate, as the draft  amendment he had communicated to the Secre-  tariat was along the same lines as the American  proposal. It read as follows:  AMENDMENT TO PARAGRAPH 1 OF THE APPROPRIATION  RESOLUTION FOR 1951.  For the financial year 1951, the amount of $8,200,000  is hereby appropriated for the purpose indicated in  the Appropriation Table included in this resolution,  with a view to financing the entire Programme  approved by the Fifth Session Conference.  Should the income of the Organization for the finan-  cial year 1951 prove inadequate, the Director-General  shall endeavour to reduce administrative expenditure  and shall further be authorized to draw upon the  Working Capital Fund as a temporay means of off-  setting a possible budgetary deficit for the financial  year 1951.  Mr. Sedar Senghor attached no special value to  his draft: the aim in view was all that mattered.  The deficit resulting from arrears in the payment  of contributions by certain Member States was no  new problem. In the past, that deficit had been  met through the Working Capital Fund and the  recovery of arrears of contributions. As at
28 February 1950, those arrears amounted to  $1,609,776, as against a budget surplus of  $1,105,391, thus making an actual deficit of  $504,385. The Working Capital Fund with a  reserve of $3,000,000 consisted of advances and  actually belonged to Member States.  Mr. Sedar Senghor observed that there was a  twofold problem to solve; there were two kinds  of arrears, some of which had to be regarded as  irrecoverable, while there was every reason to  expect that the others would eventually be paid.  The French delegation was intending in due  course to propose that the Administrative Com-  mission should amend Financial Regulations 25  and 26, and to advocate the establishment of a  special reserve fund built up from budget sur-  pluses. For the time being, a solution had to be  found for the “normal” arrears, which could be  met from the Working Capital Fund.  On behalf of the French delegation, Mr. Sedar  Senghor advocated an actual budget of $8,200,000  and emphasized that the Director-General should  have that full sum at his disposal for the im-  plementation of his programme. He assumed  that the arrears could be covered by effecting  savings in personnel costs and by drawing on the  Working Capital Fund.  The subscriptions from new Members and the  firm promises of payment by certain Member  States were an encouraging factor which would  go some way towards restoring the situation to  normal.  [7] Sir Sarvepalli RADHAKRISHNAN (India)  warmly praised Mr. Benton's statement and  supported the United States proposal. He stressed  that the so-called advanced countries needed self-  education as much as the others required re-  education.  [8] Professor CARNEIRO (Brazil) seconded the  American proposal to the effect that the Organ-  ization should have a real budget of $8,200,000  in 1951, the Director-General being authorized to  draw upon any further funds that might be  available from the financial year 1950.  [9] Shafik GHORBAL Bey (Egypt) wished to  pay homage to the Director-General’s leadership  and supported the United States motion.  [10] Professor VERNIERS (Belgium) also agreed  with the United States proposal that the budget  ceiling should be fixed at $8,200,000.  [11] Dr. JAMALI (Iraq) was glad to note the  recognition that Unesco’s action must also be  political if it was to prevent war and preserve  peace. He emphasized that the Programme must  essentially consist of (1) reconstruction through  the Technical Assistance Programme, (2) the  reduction of existing tensions, and (3) spreading  United Nations ideals and aims. He stated that  the Iraqi delegation associated itself with every-  thing the United States delegate had said.  [12] Professor PHOTIADES (Greece) emphasized  that, despite its poverty, Greece was prepared to  contribute by every means in its power to the  budget and the fulfilment of Unesco’s ideals, in  accordance with its policy since the earliest days  of the Organization. He therefore supported the  proposals of the United States and French del-  egations.  [13] Mr. SEVERI (Italy) agreed with the  United States proposal and shared the views  expressed by the delegate of Iraq. He suggested  that the General Conference should authorize the  Executive Board and the Director-General, after  the close of the Conference and in accordance with  its instructions, to establish an order of preced-  ence relegating the less important proposals to the                           background, should the budget be found in-  adequate for the full implementation of the pro-  gramme approved.  [14] Professor PIAGET (Switzerland) supported  the United States proposal and stressed that,  though there was a practically unanimous desire  to compress the programme, the various delega-  tions held very different views on the means of  attaining that end: he considered that in its first  few years Unesco would have to resign itself to a  compromise programme, as the compression  advocated and desired by all was bound to be a  gradual process based on experience.  [15] Mr. BERNAL JIMENEZ (Colombia) also  spoke in favour of the United States proposal.  Referring to the last remark of the Swiss delegate,  Mr. Jiménez felt safe in asserting that Unesco’s  main aims had already been defined by the  Conference, both in plenary meeting during the  discussion of the Director-General’s report and in  that Commission. He felt that the matter pre-  sented no serious difficulties, since it was gen-  erally agreed that the programme should be com-  pressed.  [16] Dr. WALKER (Australia) was in full  agreement with the United States delegate’s  views concerning the necessity to concentrate on  objectives of major international importance; buf  he was unable to follow his financial proposals,  because it would be confusing for the Conference  to be invited to vote for a ceiling on the assump-  tion that the Administrative Commission would  take certain decisions. He pointed out that:
(1) The new Member States’ contributions  should be applied to reduce contributions two  years later, if there were a real surplus on the  current year—an unlikely event;
(2) The proposal that the 1947 surplus should  be withheld had not been settled by the Adminis-  trative Commission and, while the United States  proposed to use it for the implementation of the
1951 Programme, the Executive Board was in  favour of applying it to the accumulated  deficiency of contributions;
(3) The suggested economies might or might  not produce $100,000.  The budget ceiling could not be based on a  series of assumptions which prejudged the Ad-  ministrative Commission’s decisions: it was the  Australian Government's view that the Budget  should not exceed $8,000,000 and that the Com-  mission should take into consideration the resolu-  tion of the General Assembly of the United  Nations to keep the expenditure of the Special-  ized Agencies within the amount of income which  could be reasonably expected for the current     financial year. On behalf of his Government,  Dr. Walker moved the adoption of an $8,000,000  celling.  [17] Mr. HARDMAN (United Kingdom) associ-  ated himself with Dr. Walker’s warning; but it was  not possible to pick and choose projects in order  to establish the concentrated programme strongly  favoured by the United Kingdom delegation with-  out a provisional ceiling. In his opinion, the  total Budget should be $8,200,000; but the fact  that the whole of that sum would not be received  should be faced and therefore a reserve within the  total was necessary. The three sources of addi-  tional income referred to by Mr. Sargeant might  enable the Director-General to carry out an  $8,200,000 programme on a $7,600,000 income,  but unfortunately those additional assets were by  no means guaranteed. Accordingly, he was in  favour of one Budget in two parts: (1) $7,600,000  for those projects on which the Director-General  might confidently embark, and (2) $500,000 for  the projects to be initiated only if the Director-  General was assured of the necessary income.  [18] The CHAIRMAN explained that the Commis-  sion had to take a provisional decision regarding  the budget total for 1951.  It had two formal proposals before it, one put  forward by the United States delegation and the  other by the French delegation, fixing the budgel  at $8,200,000 and suggesting means of raising  that sum.  In addition, the Head of the United Kingdom  delegation had suggested a similar figure, but had  gone further in proposing both a maximum of  $8,200,000 and a minimum of $7,600,000, to  allow for the possibility of certain income being  received in 1951. However, the alternative pro-  posal was liable to give rise to some confusion in  the minds of delegates, and the Chairman thought  it preferable for the United Kingdom delegate to  keep to one figure only (either $8,200,000 or  $7,600,000); an amendment dealing with a  supplementary budget could be drafted, should  there be any possibility of the Organization's  having an actual budget of $8,200,000.
