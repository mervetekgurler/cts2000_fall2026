ELEVENTH MEETING Wednesday, 19 November 1947, at 11 a. m. Chairman : Statement by Dr. Behanan on Liaison with the Trusteeship Department of the United Nations. The CHAIRMAN asked Dr. Behanan to speak, although the subject of his statement referred to Item 9 of the Agenda, because he had to return to Lake Success. Dr. BEHANAN (United Nations) mentioned the increasing contacts between Unesco and the United Nations for information regarding trust territories and non-self-governing countries. He drew atten- tion to two points of procedure arising from the organization of the Department concerred in the United Nations. The Trusteeship Department has two Divisions, one for non-self-governing territories and the second for Trust territories at the service of the Trusteeship Council. The former was not presided over by a statutory body of government members and, if it referred matters to Unesco, the expert advice of the Unesco Secretariat was sufficient, In this connexion he recalled that the United Nations might be debarred by political considera- tions not affecting Unesco from carrying out the advice received, for which, however, they were always grateful. The Trusteeship Division, on the other hand, was presided over by the Trusteeship Council, an organ of Member States. When this body referred aquestion to Unesco, it was expected that the reply would be rendered in association with a government body, i.e. in the case of Unesco, the Executive Board. If this was not done, the United Nations Secretariat was placed in an embarrassing position and the Trusteeship Council were unable to give the weight they would wish to the opinion of Unesco Hungarian Request for Reduction of 1947 Contribution. The CHAIRMAN announced that the General Committee had referred the Hungarian request for a reduction in their 1947 contributions back to the Administrative and External Relations Commission. The DEPUTY DIRECTOR-GENERAL, in answer to a request made at the previous meeting by Senator Brookes for a statement on the effect on the morale of the staff of the passage of certain of the resolutions proposed by the Commission, took the opportunity to lay before the Commission some facts about the first year’s work of the Secretariat which were rele- vant to the present discussion,and to a later item dealing with the organization of the Secretariat. He had followed with great interest all that had been said about the qualifications and competence of members of the Secretatiat, their salaries and allow- ances. The aims of the Secretariat were the same as those of the delegates : the prompt execution of the work to which Unesco was dedicated and for which the annual programme was formulated. For this there must be an international Secretariat capable of carrying out the work of the Organization, and a clear-cut programme designed to make a real contri- bution to peace and security. He wished to give some account of the assignment and working condi- tions with which the Secretariat had to contend since the last Conference. That Conference had adjourned last year, leaving a general series of resolutions instead of a work programme and a sum of $ 6,000,000 instead of a budget. The Secretariat had therefore had to prepare a pro- gramme and a budget, the only staff available being those remaining from the Preparatory Commission. Work was exceptionally heavy, documents had to be produced for the meetings of the Standing Committee of the Executive Board in January and February and for the Board itself in April. Two months after approval of the 1947 programme, work had to start on the 1947 Programme and Budget. He referred also to the work entailed by the meetings of the Executive Board Committees, including those on Finance, Nominations and Non-Governmental Organizations, and of the summer meeting of the Board itself. Finally the valuable and important Conference in Mexico constituted a major under- taking in itself. — 575 —             He hopedthese remarks would answer suggestions that a completely new staff should have been recruit- ed in February. Recruitment could not begin until the Programme had been agreed. Moreover, the methods of recruitment imposed on the Director- General entailed inevitable delays only short- term contracts could be offered and the procedure of advertising vacancies throughout the Member States took time and did not always give encouraging results. Further, many people were reluctant to work for international organizations away from their accustomed milieu, and many governments were reluctant to release first-class people. He drew attention to the difficulty of attracting prospective staff to Paris under the present difficult conditions : housing was scarce and costly, the coal shortage acute (staff last winter were working in overcoats and gloves), food supply was uncertain except at high prices. If Unesco were able to import food, duty free, conditions might be improved consider- ably, particularly for staff with families and small children. There were also problems arising from the political instability and the wide disparity between the official rate of exchange and the rate reflected in market prices. These conditions were not worse than those experienced by the French people, but they were bound to affect morale and efficiency and to act as a deterrent to recruitment of good staff. The Secretariat had also been hampered by lack of office equipment, from typew riters to paper. The distance involved in communication with Member States involved delay in the transmis- sion of information : a letter by airmail took one month to reach the Philippines from Paris. The Director-General was aware of the shortcomings of the Organization, but since the organization must reflect the programme, plans for re-organi- zation were not being put into effect until a clear indication of the 1948 Programme was obtained. Time had been given to co-ordinating the work of Unesco with that of the United Nations on such matters as programme as well as on financial and personnel matters. Such co-ordination demanded personal consultations between experts in the diffe- rent activities; this meant an additional drain on the staff and increase in transport costs. The objective was to build an international and competent Secretariat, devoted to the United Nations’ view of the problem with which Unesco was concerned. He did not ask that the short- comings be overlooked, but that the facts he had given be borne in mind when discussing the work of the Secretariat. In conclusion, he suggested that the building of any organization took time, particularly in the case of an international body made up of stafl of many nationalities with different backgrounds and work habits. More over this Or- ganization was a completely new institution,as it was an attempt to blend educational, scientific and cul- tural interests with the diplomatic and political practices normally required of international organi- zations. Dr. MARTINEZ BAEZ (Mexico) congratulated the Deputy-Director-General on his statement. If he had criticized the Secretariat it had been constructively and with the best of intentions. From one and one- half year’s work in the Unesco building he had seen that the majority of the staff were working con- scientiously, and it was impossible to expect good work under bad conditions. While it was a privilege to live in Paris, conditions were hard; he supported all the Deputy Director-General had said about the difficulties of living in (Paris. The staff was not a machine; it was composed of human beings who could not give of their best if forced to contend with an impossible state of affairs. If the Organization was to function properly, conditions must be improved, in spite of the expense involved. Dr. HAVRANEK (Czechoslovakia) also agreed with the Deputy Director-General on living conditions in Paris. Referring to recruitment methods, and particularly to the last paragraph on page 88 and the third paragraph on page 89 of the Director- General's Report, he thought that the circulated descriptions of posts were too precise, and also that world-wide competition tended to discourage rather than encourage the best people to apply for posts; it might be better to accord preference for certain posts to nationals of certain specified countries. While the documents circulated at Mexico were very interesting, many were distributed too late for careful scrutiny and their very number hampered discussion, since members of smaller delegations did not have time to read all documents placed before them. He queried the necessity for an Information Officer for such small geographic areas as the Scandinavian countries, considering that Europe as a whole had already one Information Officer. Mr. ASHFAQUE HUSAIN (India) paid a tribute to the restrained tone of the Deputy Director- General’s statement. The difficulties which the Organization had to face were fully appreciated. All the same he was dissatisfied with the organi- zation of the Conference, the distribution of infor- mation to Member States and the administration of the Organization. Delegates were not satisfied that all the staff were sufficiently highly qualified for their work, that they all deserved the high salaries paid, that the staff represented adequately the geographical areas of the world, and that methods — 576 —    of selecting staff were sufficiently impersonal and careful. With regard to the remarks of the Deputy Director-General on the difficulty of blending into one team staffs of different nationalities, the dele- gate of India insisted this difficulty had to be over- come : the staff must reflect the membership of the Organization. In order to reduce the suspense which might result from the introduction of the resolutions of the Commission, he urged that the review of the staff be carried out quickly. For all these reasons he urged support of the three resolu- tions to be submitted by the delegates of the United States of America, Mexico and himself asking for an improved balance in the geographical repre- sentation on thestaff and a review of the qualifications of the staff. He suggested that a small sub-com- mittee of the Executive Board be formed to help the Director-General give effect to these resolutions. The meeting continued the discussion of the pre- vious meeting. Seven speakers were scheduled to speak on the statement delivered by the Deputy Director-General. Dr. WALKER (Australia) said his delegation had fully appreciated the grave difficulties encountered in setting up a permanent Organization and the excel- lent work done in the Secretariat. The procedure followed-carrying on with the staff assembled for the Preparatory Commission in London and building the permanent structure on these foundations - was entirely correct. However, it would be necessary to gather not only personnel who were experts in their own speciality, but who were also experts at working in the international field. This would require careful direction by the management and systematic training of staff. The Australian delegation fully appreciated the difficulties of working in Paris and hoped that the French Government would help in attacking the difficulties met by the staff of the Organization. Professor de BERREDO CARNEIRO (Brazil) explained that Unesco’s experiment was without precedent. It could not be compared to any of the existing international organizations. The tasks of the Secretariat were not clearly defined; some coun- tries thought of the Organization as a power house of ideas; others saw it as a permanent round table for discussions of the sciences, education and culture. In fact, Unesco’s Secretariat was crushed by its daily administrative routine, and the problem facing the delegates had to be seen against this background.
