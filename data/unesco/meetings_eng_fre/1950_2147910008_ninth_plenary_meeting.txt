166  it is taking place, its complete success would al-  ready be assured. And we should have our gra-  cious hosts to thank for that.  [30] The South African delegation has studied  with great interest the report of the Director-  General on the activities of the Organization, and  welcomes this opportunity of associating itself  with the tributes which have been paid to the  Director-General on the efficiency and zeal with  which he and his staff have carried out the pro-  gramme entrusted to them by the last session of  the General Conference. We are gratified to note  that commendable steps have been taken to curb  further increases in the budget, following on the  desire, widely expressed at the last Conference,  that the Organization's activities should be re-  assessed and consolidated. The Director-General  has rightly stated, however, that such stabilization  as has been attained is more apparent than real,  although scarcely any new projects were added to  the programme. This is partly due, he aptly  points out, to the fact that “the same resolu-  tion every year necessitates work on a wider  scale simply because of the impetus acquired’.  It is important that we should bear this mind,  for it shows that, if concentration is to be achieved,  it must be not only in respect of new projects  but also in respect of winding up existing  programmes. It is clear from the statements  of previous speakers that there is a widespread  recognition of the necessity for further con-  solidation and simplification of the programme,  and in view of the experience of the past year the  South African delegation cannot but associate  itself with this view.  [31] The Director-General’s report shows how,  in spite of attempts at simplification, it has been  necessary to increase the Secretariat by 13 per cent  to the position where, even if the common services  are deducted, payments to personnel amount to  five-eighths of the over-all budget. This is not a  healthy state of affairs. But we cannot blame the  Director-General therefor. He and other speakers  have stressed the extremely heavy burden being  borne by the Secretariat. The obvious conclusion
1s that we, the General Conference, have entrusted  the Director-General with duties without accord-  ing him resources commensurate with what is  necessary for the execution of those duties. This  state of affairs only we, the General Conference,  and not the Director-General, can remedy. It is  our business to find the remedy. Either we must  find the means for an expanding programme or  we must simplify the programme. This is a dif-  ficult decision, for we are all agreed, I am sure,  that the opportunities of action for the purpose of  advancing, through education and scientific and  cultural relations, the objective of international               peace and the common welfare of mankind, are  practically unlimited. On the other hand, we  must face the fact that what is limited are the  financial means for providing for the exercise of  those opportunities, bearing in mind the numerous  other commitments of Member States in the wide  field of present-day international co-operation. _It  is appropriate, therefore, that the Director-  General has stated that we should seriously  take stock of our resources and our responsi-  bilities. I think it will be agreed that however  wide may be our responsibilities, the action which  we take to meet those responsibilities must  be governed by our resources. This 1s but  simple common sense. These resources are  dependent upon contributions from Member  States, and the difficulty which some Governments  are experiencing in meeting their obligations in  this regard is strikingly evident in the extent of  arrear contributions. The position 1s aggravated  by the urgent need for increased action on a na-  tional basis. Much emphasis has been placed on  this by the Director-General, the Chairman of the  Executive Board, and many delegates. We in  South Africa are acutely aware, from experience,  of the large governmental expenditure necessary  for effective action in this sphere. It is clearly  necessary to strike a proper balance between inter-  national and national expenditure. However  enthusiastic supporters of the ideals of Unesco we  may be, we are all agreed that it is the primary  duty of a State to provide for the fundamental  education of its members, both juvenile and  adult. It is therefore understandable that Member  States insist on a close scrutiny of the purposes  to, and manner in which, their contributions to  international organizations are applied. The South  African delegation considers, therefore, that it is  essential that the General Conference determine  its resources by fixing a realistic over-all budget  which is within the means of Member States,  bearing in mind their other responsibilities. Only  when the resources have been so determined can  the Organization decide how best to cut its suit  according to its cloth. Two lines of action will  be necessary:  [32] In the first place, we shall have to avail  ourselves of every opportunity of avoiding any  administrative or overhead expenditure which is  not absolutely essential. In a budget in which  administrative expenses account for such a large  proportion of total expenditure I am not without  hope that considerable savings can be made. We  already have before us the welcome proposal for  biennial instead of annual sessions. Further  savings should be possible on items such as trav-  elling expenses of officials, printing, and many ;  others enumerated by previous speakers, notably  the delegates of Denmark, Canada, the Nether-  lands, and the United States of America.  [33] In the second place, we can further simpli-  fy and concentrate the programme. For this the  General Conference must assume primary re-  sponsibility. The Director-General will execute  the programme as laid down by us, and it is we  who must curb ourselves and not allow our  enthusiasm to overwhelm our realism and good  judgment. The Executive Board could greatly  assist by allocating the priorities to be applied to  the projects submitted to the General Conference.  If we find that certain matters are being post-  poned in deference to others of higher priority,  we must remind ourselves of the possibility that,  if Unesco’s programme is extended over a field too  wide in relation to its resources, the sum total of  its efforts may well be inappreciable in the final  reckoning for peace or war. If Unesco is to make  an effective contribution towards world peace, it
1s surely preferable that, if its programme must  necessarily be limited, it should be concentrated  on the matters of more immediate importance, and  not on projects of which the final effect may be  so far in the future that it may easily be upset by  other more important developments which cannot  be foreseen at present. We feel convinced that  our able and energetic Director-General will still  find in this more circumscribed field of activities  ample scope for his driving power and his genius  for organization, and that Unesco will reap more  immediate and not less important results from  such a policy of concentration and intensification  of its efforts. Let us now consider  what strength we have. Unesco consists of
59 Member States, some of which are to be found  in every continent, representing every school of  thought, and united in their consideration for the  dignity of the individual and their care for inter-  national peace. This session of the General Con-  ference is exceptionally important. It is consider-  ing the problem of the aims of our Organization  and trying so to co-ordinate the programme that  it shall be more directly related to those aims;  the Conference is investigating Unesco’s methods,  with the object of increasing their speed and  efficiency. Even more, the Organization must  assess the extent to which it can play an effective  part in the present condition of the world, decid-  ing not only what it can do to secure peace for  tomorrow but what it can do for peace today. 1  have been glad to see how readily the Executive  Board’s appeal on this point has been heard and  how seriously it has been considered. Several  delegations have responded to it. Although they  are not unanimously agreed upon the solutions to  be suggested, they are all, or nearly all, inspired  by the same desire to fulfil the great obligations  imposed upon them by the Constitution, under  which Unesco is, by definition, not a mere office  of intellectual co-operation but an organization  for the defence of peace. Various speakers have  advised us to bring prudence to our task. But  what is the nature of that task? Until the Con-  stitution of London is amended or until its  Preamble becomes a dead letter we are required            explicitly to serve peace without weakness or  reserve.  [58] During this general discussion the hope  has several times been repeated that Unesco will  not get mixed up in politics. That should of course  be the rule for the Secretariat. A political Secre-  tariat in Unesco is inconceivable. But the General  Conference is an assembly of delegates of gov-  ernments and as such necessarily has political  authority and a political character. That being so,  it is hard to see why the political authority so  much in evidence in dealing with certain specific  cases should suddenly become perilous when it is  sought to use it to strengthen the foundations of  the United Nations’ work for peace which is our  essential task and the reason for our existence.  Our Constitution makes no distinction between  peace tomorrow and peace today. Of course the  political centre par excellence of the United Na-  tions’ system is Lake Success; but Unesco is not  a cloister utterly withdrawn from that system.  [59] Several policies have been suggested for the  Organization, among which the General Confer-  ence will have to decide. May 1 say that, in my  opinion, these policies are not mutually exclusive,  but, on the other hand, do not seem calculated  to complement one another. If we take a more  active part in the present we are likely to win the  sympathy and assistance of all the men and wo-  men concerned with the present drama of the  world, and that sympathy and assistance must  necessarily be of help in directing our programme  towards the accomplishment of the basic practical  tasks on which the groundwork of tomorrow's  peace depends. As several speakers have em-  phasized, Unesco’s field of activity offers definite  opportunities of assisting the United Nations not  inconsiderably in their work for peace concerned  with current problems. Specific proposals for this  purpose have been put forward, and I must pay  tribute to the constructive attitude in which they  have been advanced. As many delegates have  pointed out, the arms Unesco wields in this  struggle for peace are those of truth and tolerance.  They are strong and honourable arms, so that we  need not fear the strife in which they will be  tried. In my opinion it is Unesco’s mission, now  more than ever, to conduct an unceasing crusade  for truth and tolerance. We cannot stand aside.  We are living in an era in which an ivory tower  is a tomb. Personally, it causes me no anxiety to  see Unesco entering the arena of current affairs.  Only—I must repeat once more— there would be  no point in laying down a militant policy for the  Organization unless the same militant spirit were  to inspire the national policy of each one of the  Member States. We cannot conceive of a Unesco     policy of peace apart from that followed by the  Member States themselves. Here again, ladies and  gentlemen, you have to make the necessary deci-  sIONns.  [60] Years ago, when I first visited Florence,  I slowly climbed up the road which leads to the  Piazzale Michelangelo, as many of you have prob-  ably done. When I reached the top, I looked for  some likeness of the artist. There was none. The  incomparable silhouette of the great sculptor’s  David stood out against a clear blue sky; in the  distance, the countryside rose to the horizon in  a succession of light and shade, like the stanzas  of a well-constructed poem. Down in the valley  lay the city. What was the significance of Michel-  angelo’s absence from this place commemorating  his renown? But was it right to speak of his  absence? No man can ever be absent from places  where his memory is perpetuated by his works.  The immortality of the greatest creative artists  lies in the fact that their survival does not depend  on their material presence, even in the form of a  statue. When Unesco has really accomplished  the task assigned by history, it is unlikely that any  promenade will bear the name of the Organization  to commemorate it in the same way as the  sculptor of Moses. But, in the minds of all free  men and women, there will be a free force for  ever exalting the ideal of brotherhood, justice and  peace which we now serve with humble devotion.  We ourselves shall probably not live to see that  day. But that does not matter. Promises are  gages. Those who believe in their duty keep their  eyes fixed on the future. The time for talking is  past; the hour for decision has struck. We have  said what had to be said and you, gentlemen,  have said it very well. We now have to do what  there is to be done.
