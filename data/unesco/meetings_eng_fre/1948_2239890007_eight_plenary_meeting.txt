Hence, the more good  Unesco can do in the world around it, the more  it will have the sympathy of everyone, and will  thus be able to secure the acceptance by all its  sympathizers of its programme for building up on  atmosphere of peace, happiness, well-being, justice  and charity. It follows then that a larger part  of Unesco’s budget should be expended in practical  beneficence of all kinds to relieve human misery.  Thirdly and lastly, I turn to Unesco's activities  in Luxembourg. Among us, all children must  attend a primary school from the age of six to  twelve. . Illiteracy is unknown among us. In  consequence of our geographical position, between  France and Belgium on one side, and Germany  on the other, all our citizens are required to learn,  during their six years at primary school, our own  dialect, French and German. During the seven  vears of secondary school, from the seventh to the  fourth class, all secondary school subjects, such  as history, geography, science, etc., are taught in  German while from the third class up to Rhetoric  class, the same series of subjects are studied again  in French. As Luxembourg has no university, we  have ever, from force of circumstances, followed  Unesco’s recommendations before Unesco itself  existed in sending our university students abroad  to study, thus to learn to know and love other  nations. Our students go to France, Belgium, the  Netherlands, Switzerland and Italy and, prior to  the last war, they went to Germany too; since  then a proportion has been going to England.  Indeed it was in England that our Crown Prince  Jean completed his education.  Moreover, though Unesco itself did not create  it, there is an excellent Unesco establishment in  the north of my country. In that house there live  in community more than a hundred men of great  intellectual, artistic and moral eminence. They  come from many nations; Frenchmen, Belgians,  Luxembourgers, Dutchmen, Italians, Danes, Nor-  wegians, Swedes and even, before the second  world war, Englishmen, Americans and Germans  have passed through it. Without exception they  did and do live in perfect harmony, because all  are hard workers, all dedicate themselves with  fervour, all know the rules of give and take, all  are inspired with religious and brotherly love.  This seat of an ideal Unesco is the Abbey of Cler-  vaux. The motto which it shares with the whole        Benedictine Order is “Pax”, Peace. That surely  is Unesco’s key-note too, to give Peace to mankind.  Time and again, weary souls come to Clervaux  for rest, for edification, for refreshment, for spi-  ritual stock-taking, for there they find the calm,  the tranquillity and the balance that they need to  live the high-speed and troubled life of the  Twentieth Century. In this house too, as in  hundreds upon hundreds within my Order, they  have for long centuries pursued Unesco's secon-  dary objects, the art of Gregorian plainsong, the  making of splendid ornaments and sacred vessels  for the service of the Most High, for, with Joan  of Arc, we hold that the Lord God must be served  the first.
