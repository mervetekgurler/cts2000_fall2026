Presentation by the Director-General of the  Budget Estimates for the Financial Year
1951 [document 5C/5 (IT)].  [7] The DIRECTOR-GENERAL presented the  Budget Estimates for the financial year 1951, stat-  ing that they totalled $8,149,985; this represented  an increase of $150,000 over the total budget for  the current financial year. This sum of  $8,149,985 was but a small contribution by fifty-  nine States to the promotion of peace and  security through education, science and culture.  [8] Many speakers had emphasized the discre-  pancy between Unesco’s aims and the means for  their attainment placed at its disposal. Therefore,  in studying the Budget Estimates, the delegates  should not confine themselves to considering  them in relation to the Programme of the  Executive Board and to the comments of the Bud-  get Committee, but should also consider them in  relation to the programme which would be en-  trusted to Unesco for the year 1951. If the bud-  get were examined in this spirit, the General Con-  ference would be likely to grant the Organization  a budget sufficient to put into effect a programme  commensurate with the tasks undertaken by  Unesco.  [9] The Director-General pointed out that  Unesco must not be considered merely as a secre-  tariat, but as an instrument for collective action  by Member States whose efforts should merely be
313  stimulated and co-ordinated by the Secretariat.  Governments and National Commissions must be  encouraged to take effective measures which  would enable Unesco to establish contact with  every people, to make education available to  greater numbers, to win acceptance for a more  humane conception of science’s part in the service  of peace, and finally to secure a fuller apprecia-  tion of the cultural values of civilization. Hence  it was essential to develop energetically the Na-  tional Commissions of all Member States. At the  present time, however, National Commissions  were not in general sufficiently developed to  permit the Organization to pass on to them the  execution of the programme to the extent that  was desirable. If, in a large number of States,  there was no National Commission capable of tak-  ing part in any particular project, that project  would be deprived of its necessary international  basis.  It was above all this inadequacy which  restricted the budget to its present size. The 1m-  portant thing was to guarantee the continuity of  Unesco’s task until such time as the intensifica-  tion of the part played in the programme by Mem-  ber States would enable the financial burden now  resting on the Secretariat to be reduced. Such  continuity required that a minimum of services,  and consequently of resources, be ensured. The  Budget Estimates were in the neighbourhood of  that minimum, taking into account the draft pro-  gramme to which they referred. The Director-  General believed that a big reduction in the bud-  get would tend to compromise either the  efficiency or the continuity of Unesco’s work.  [10] He recalled that the advance draft of the  budget presented by him to the Executive Board  in February 1950 had amounted to $9,500,000.  The Executive Board, believing this figure to be  too high, had decided to suppress a whole series  of activities, among them certain projects adopted  in November 1949. Those eliminations had been  dictated by a wish to concentrate the programme.  In that connexion, the Director-General read out  the following passage from his Introduction to  the Budget Estimates for the Financial Year 1951  (document 5C/5 (IT), pages VII and VII):  | quite understand the difficulties which Member  States are experiencing. But I should be less than  frank if I did not point out that, without this addi-  tional effort on the part of Member States, the main-  tenance of the Organization’s budget at a figure only  a little higher than that for the present financial  year will in fact mean a considerable reduction in its  activities, and that it is impossible to restrict the  development of an Organization in its early days  without the risk of seriously compromising its future.
