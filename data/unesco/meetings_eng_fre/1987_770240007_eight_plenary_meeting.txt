2.1 Mr TAXELL (Finlarid) :  Mr President, Mr Director-General, ladies and gentlemen, I should like  to join all those who have congratulated you, Mr President, on your election to  the presidency of this session of the General Conference. During this session,  decisions will be taken which are of great significance for the future Of the  Organization. I am convinced that, under your leadership, our deliberations will  take place in a constructive atmosphere.
2.2 May I also take this opportunity to express my thanks to the Director-  General for the excellent preparations for this General Conference, which are  concrete evidence of your continued devotion to our Organization. May I also, on  behalf of my government, express to you, Mr M'Bow, my thanks for 13 years of good  co-operation.
2.3 We shall, later during the session, elect a new Director-General for the  Organization. My government endorses the decision of the Executive Board,  nominating Mr Federico Mayor for this post. I hope that the process of transition  will take place in an atmosphere of goodwill and consensus.
2.4 *Mr President, in recent years the United Nations system has been widely  dedated. Its efficiency and, indeed, its viability have been questioned. Many  have even seen the future of multilateral co-operation as being endangered. This  debate has resulted in a process of renewal, and a new trust in the United  Nations system now seems to be emerging. It is natural that Finland, a small  neutral country, should warmly welcome this development and reaffirm the  importance which it attaches to the United Nations and its Specialized Agencies.  The Nordic governments have recently stressed the importance of the present  positive development in the United Nations system, which will have to face  important challenges before the turn of the century. In this context, they also  stressed the need to strengthen Unesco and to continue the reform process within  the Organization.
2.5 The continuity and consistency of Finland's support for Unesco is well  known. We believe that Unesco is a unique forum for exchange of information and  ideas on the basis of the equality of nations. No other body can replace it. Our  approach to the Organization is, however, pragmatic : while recognizing the  potentialities of Unesco and the importance of its tasks, we are also aware of  its shortcomings and weaknesses and the need to find remedies for them.
2.6 It is evident that an organization like Unesco should, after 40 years of  existence, review its methods and forms of action with a critical eye. This work  was initiated some three years ago by the Executive Board and the Secretariat,  but is far from being completed. Rather ,than as a single project, it must be seen
8  as an continuous process, carried out in close co-operation between the governing  bodies and the Secretariat of the Organization as well as its Member States. The  ultimate aim of this process should be to strengthen Unesco, restore confidence  in the Organization and re-establish its universality. We should all work  together for this purpose. No country or group of countries can claim special  advantages or privileges in this*respect.
2.7 Mr President, when Unesco celebrated its 40th anniversary last year, it  was clear that many of the challenges which it faces today are similar to those  which led to the founding of the Organization. Many of the tasks for which Unesco  was created are of a permanent nature, but new challenges have also arisen in its  fields of competence and continue to do so at an increasing pace. In order to  meet all the new demands and at the same time secure the continuation of older,  time-proven programmes, it is necessary to weigh carefully our priorities and the  resources, both intellectual and material, at our disposal.
2.8 The concept of programme concentration, which my delegation, together  with the other Nordic countries , has advocated, has now entered the Unesco  language. It seems to me, however, that it means different things to different  people. I should therefore like to clarify how my government interprets this  concept. In our view, concentration is not synonymous with reduction of the  programme, neither is it an attempt to alter the basic orientations which we all  have approved by consensus. By concentration we mean identifying priorities  within Unesco's fields of competence and allocating increased resources to the  corresponding priority activities. This may necessitate a reduction of activities  of lesser importance.
2.9 Thoroughly planned strategic approaches should be the backbone of  Unesco's programmes in the future. Only if we concentrate our efforts on priority  tasks cari the scarce resources of Unesco be used in an efficient manner. One such  task is, for instance, the challenge posed by the development of science and  technology and their use for a harmonious anci humane development.
2.10 Challenges such as these should be taken into account in the preparation  of the third Medium-Term Plan of Unesco. I firmly believe that, in á changing  world, Unesco must be able to respond to changing needs.
2.11 Furthermore, the third Medium-Term Plan should be of a quality which  restores confidence in the work of Unesco not only among those who work in its  fields of competence but also among political decision-makers and the general  public. The Plan should have a structure which makes it understandable to all  those for whom it is designed.
2.12 Finally, the Medium-Term Plan should be a concise and clear policy  directive, in other words a framework for programme elaboration. On th& other  hand, it must not attempt to replace the Constitution and it must not restrict  the decision-making powers of the Member States at the General Conference.
2.13 I hope that our discussions concerning the third Medium-Term Plan will  be open and frank. Meetings concerning the Plan have taken place in different  parts of the world. Finland organized a regional consultation concerning the Plan  among European National Commissions. Discussions at this meeting were enriched by  the participation of several observers anci guests from other regions. There was a  considerable convergence of views among the participants concerning many aspects  of the Plan. The report of the consultation has been distributed to all  delegations at this session. My delegation will, together with the other Nordic  delegations, make more detailed comments on the Medium-Term Plan in the Programe  Commissions.
2.14 Mr President, when discussing medium-term perspectives, we must not  forget the material basis. The financial situation of our Organization continues  to be critical. Having already reduced its budget by nearly a thirã, Unesco is  facing new difficulties in the form of a deficit due to currency fluctuations.  Not only has the deficit of the past biennium to be covered but, even more  importantly, the financial basis of the next biennium and the period beyond it is  at stake. Without a clear understanding on financing, there will be no firm  ground for the planning of future activities.
2.15 My government's position is clear: all Member States have to bear their  part of the collective responsibility and meet their financial obligations  towards the Organization at a resource level which is necessary for the execution  of the jointly approved programme. If there is political will the appropriate  methods will be found to solve the technical problems.
2.16 Mr President, at present the priority areas on which Unesco's programme  should be concentrate6 are being considered. There can be no doubt thöt the
253    struggle against illiteracy must remai-n one of Unesco's major tasks in the  future. It is a good example of a strategic target which falls fully within the  competence of Unesco and where the Organization's action will contribute not only  to the splution of a major problem but also to the strengthening of its own image  and authority within the international community. During the previous session of  the General Conference, some important decisions were taken concerning the thrust  of Unesco's work in the field of literacy. These decisions should now be  translated into a coherent strategy and plan of action. The in-depth study by the  Special Committee of the Executive Board on achievements, strategies and further  action in Unesco's literacy work has been a valuable step in this direction. The  rapporteurs of the study consider it of paramount importance for Unesco, and I  quote 'perhaps even as an "existential question" for the Organization to  reinvigorate its efforts in this field'. I am in full agreement with this  conclusion. A comprehensive literacy strategy and an action plan could mobilize  the international community towards a common goal. Unesco's role will be a  catalytic one, to offer the leadership and to help channel expertise and material  assistance. I am convinced that in this way it would also be possible to attract  more outside financing from a variety of sources.
2.17 My government considers the World Decade for Cultural Development  ano'ther priority area on which Unesco should focus. Like the Literacy Year, the  Decade will help to create a better image of the Organization. The four main  objectives of the Decade should constitute the cornerstone of Unesco's activities  in the field of culture and should, accordingly, be taken into account in the  Medium-Term Plan.
2.18 Development cannot be seen only in terms of economic growth or  technological sophistication. We should, in particular, stress the cultural  dimension of development. Development is an all-embracing improvement in the  quality of life.
2.19 The protection of the cultural and natural heritage is an issue which  has appeared on the international agenda since the publication of the report of  the World Commission on Environment and Development, led by Dr Gro Harlem  Brundtland, Prime Minister of Norway. The report demonstrates that economic  growth and a more equitable development are possible without endangering the  environment. My delegation considers that sustainable development should be a  guiding principle in the United Nations system and consequently also in the  programme of Unesco.
2.20 It is evident that Unesco's major intergovernmental scientific  programnies can contribute and will continue to contribute to the implementation  of this principle, and that. they should therefore also continue to have priority  in the Organization's programme. Unesco and its Member States should also  actively promote environmental education and take into account the  recommendations of the recent International Congress on Environmental Education  and Training.
2.21 The priority areas which I have mentioned illustrate what we consider to  be the role of Unesco in international co-operation today. The United Nations  system was created for the maintename of peace, human rights and international  understanding. A major part of its work and resources is now focused on the  solution of development problems. This is natural, considering the needs of the  greatest part of mankind. The unique task of Unesco in this context is to  safeguard human and cultural values at all levels where the international  community formulates development policies and takes decisions on their  implementation. Let us all join in helping Unesco to carry out this task for our  common benefit. Thank you, Mr President.

6.1 Mr RUHFUS (Federal Republic of Germany):  Mr President, ladies and gentlemen, on behalf of the Government of the  Federal Republic of Germany I congratulate you, Mr President, on your election.  My delegation will strive to ease your task by playing a constructive role in the  work of the Conference so that it may achieve success under your guidance.
6.2 When I say 'success', I am thinking quite specifically of our last  General Conference in Sofia. I would go so far as to describe that Conference as  a milestone in the history of our Organization for it marks the beginning of  Unesco's reform process, both among Member States and within the Secretariat. I  trust that this Conference will demonstrate the same spirit, the same desire to  work together harmoniously. Growing interdependence requires every government and  every individual to seek to remove rather than create disparities. This is not to  say that we should not have controversial debates. Such discussions are part and  parcel of the free exchange of ideas, to which my government is whole-heartedly  committed and which is the pre-condition of any form of co-operation aimed at  safeguarding peace. Unesco, like all agencies within the United Nations system,  is a political organization. That is why its mandate includes debates on those  political issues which fall within its sphere of competence. But we are opposed  to the injection of political subjects that are primarily the concern of the  United Nations itself or other Specialized Agencies. Matters relating to  disarmament, a new international economic order, the development or conception of  new human rights or rights of nations are not, in our view, the responsibility of  Unesco. U~iesco must confine its political debates to areas mandated by its  Constitution and these debates must make it possible to arrive at results that  are acceptable to all. This is not easy in a world characterized by cultural  diversity and ideological disparity. But it is precisely in such a world that  consensus is needed in order to bridge existing gulfs and to discover unity in  diversity.
6.3 Yet not everything in this world lends itself to consensus and  compromise. Our views differ considerably on fundamental issues, and especially  with regard to the relationchip between the individual and society, culture and  State.
