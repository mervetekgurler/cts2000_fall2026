How Unesco can best discharge its functions  as a world centre for the exchange of  information (documents 3C/3, 3C/5, 3C]6,  C171):  The CHAIRMAN remarked that the Joint Com-  mittee was a sort of committee of enquiry, re-  sponsible for asking the Secretariat a number of  questions about what it understood by the “World  Centre for the Exchange of Information”, dealt  with particularly on page 68 of document 3C/6.  The DEPUTY DIRECTOR-GENERAL explained  that the title of the Centre had been taken from  a resolution adopted by the General Conference  in Mexico City. The Department of Exchange of  Information was concerned with Secretariat acti-  vities which were closely related to the work of  the World Centre : the keeping of Unesco's  archives, a central documentation service, the  Unesco library, Unesco publications, collection  and analysis of statistics, etc. It had been the  Director-General’s intention to link the budget  estimates to the corresponding resolutions of the  General Conference; in the particular case of the
4                      d  p o y   b e d   b e a d   p e d   N N   p e e                    World Centre, the preparation of the budget  estimates had been rather more complicated,  because, in addition to resolution 5.8 cited on  page 68, the World Centre embraced a whole  series of resolutions relating to Reconstruction,  Libraries, Exchange of Persons, Mass Commu-  nication, Fundamental Education, Adult Edu-  cation, the Arts in General Education, Museums,  etc. The General ‘Conferences in Paris and  Mexico City had expressed the wish that Unesco  should assemble all the information necessary  to carry out the programme and to provide infor-  mation to the National Commissions and Co-  operating Bodies. To meet that wish, the Secre-  tariat had thought of publishing an Educational  Yearbook with statistics and other data, but, in  view of the Mexico City resolutions, it had seemed  better to leave that task to the Member States.  On the other hand, it had not seemed desirable  to have a separate service within each section of  the Secretariat for collecting the necessary infor-  mation, as such decentralization would have been  uneconomical. For that reason, the Director-Gen-  eral had suggested creating a single Centre,  operating not independently of the other Sections,  but at their request; by so doing, the Director-  General hoped for a more effective and more eco-  nomical service. It should be added that the ser-  vice was intended for the internal use of the Secre-  tariat and that its title should not suggest that  Unesco was becoming a world depot to which  anybody could apply for information.  M. SEYDOUX (France) asked two questions :  |. was resolution 5.8 to be interpreted as  asking for a single central service? Or, as  seemed more logical, did it propose that the doc-  umentary material required by each Service should  be established under the Head of that Service?
2. the budget figure proposed for the World  Centre for the Exchange of Information showed  a disparity between the appropriations for staff  (fifty-three persons and nearly $300,000 in sala-  ries) and the appropriations for subscriptions to  publications, or purchase of books; was it to be  understood that the World Centre would be able  to draw on other funds?  The DEPUTY DIRECTOR-GENERAL explained  that :
1. nothing in the debates or resolutions of  the Mexico Conference indicated how Unesco  could best organize that kind of work; the Dir-  ector-General had, therefore, had to choose, in the  light of his experience, which he thought the  best of various possible solutions. In that con-  nexion, the Deputy Director-General wished to  dispel one anxiety; the type of material required  was not decided by the Exchange of Information  Department, which was responsible only for clas-  sification and distribution;
2. with regard to the budget, it should be  pointed out that the library was only a very small  part of the World Centre and that the staff allo-  cated to the Centre dealt with many other matters,  as would be seen from the six points listed on  page 69 of document 3C/6.  Sir John MAUD (United Kingdom) observed  that, at the request of the General Committee of  the Conference, the Committee of Fifteen was  examining a question relating to the internal  organization of the Secretariat. lt was at once  apparent that the title “World Centre for the  Exchange of Information” was particularly mis-  leading, since that part of the Organization's ma-  chinerv was performing a number of tasks, most  of which were intended to help the Programme  Departments; it was therefore primarily part of  the internal machinery of the Secretariat.  Furthermore, reference to the resolution passed  by the Mexico City Conference showed that at  alluded to a “World Register” and that it asked  the Director-General to expand work already 1ni-  tiated. In speaking of activities “in selected fields  of education. science and culture”, the Mexico  resolution did not contemplate such heavy expen-  diture. Actually, the sum of $312,544 set aside  in 1948 for the “World Centre for the Exchange  or Information” had done more than merely  ensure execution of the Mexico City resolution; it  had also served to accomplish various work de-  volving upon different branches of the Secretariat.  As for the title “World Centre for the Exchange  of Information”, it appeared in another resolution,  but undefined.  From the information in his possession, the  delegate of the United Kingdom had the impres-  sion that :  I. the central service in the Secretariat had  done excellent work in certain fields; it was neces-  sary, for example, to co-ordinate the question-  naires sent out to different Member States;
2. paragraph 2 on page 69 of document 3C/6  said that the Director-General proposed to continue  the work by adding to the “World Centre” a  certain number of specialists in different fields,  the Organization was therefore endeavouring to  provide itself with experts in those fields, in order  to assist the corresponding Programme Depart-  ments.  The question was whether, having regard to the  experience of the last twelve or fifteen months,  the Organization was justified in its wish to cen-  tralize the information needed by the Programme  Departments. The United Kingdom delegate              thought it was not, and that was why he wanted  the Conference to ask the Director-General to drop  the experiment. At a moment when the General  Conference had just declared that the Education  Department, for instance, was to take on clearing-  house functions, was it reasonable to ask another  Department of the Secretariat to concern itself  with some of those functions? Moreover, in  another part of the programme adopted in Mexico  City, there was reference to a World Centre of  Scientific Liaison. A central service for the ex-  change of information could only duplicate work  and irritate people working in the Programme  Departments; it would also cost more and more  and would lead to the absurd position that the  work would be done by people other than the  specialists in the Departments concerned.  Finally, Sir John asked the Committee to take  note of the failure of the experiment and to request  the Director-General to decentralize the services  of documentation and exchange; that would help  to promote a form of organization which would  place more responsibility upon the Programme  Departments (the specialists provided for the  “World Centre for the Exchange of Information”  would then be directly attached to the Departments  concerned with their speciality), and the World  Centre would be left in charge of its own particular  work : library, archives and the presentation of  publications.  The CHAIRMAN thought that the Committee  could already agree that the title “World Centre  for the Exchange of Information” was misleading,  as it only centralized activities within the Secre-  tariat.  Agreed.  The CHAIRMAN pointed out that the term  “dissemination” employed in resolution 5.8 went  beyond the idea of internal use on which the  Committee had just taken a decision. The text  should therefore be revised accordingly.  Agreed.  The CHAIRMAN proposed that the Committee  should review the six functions attributed to the  World Centre on page 69 of document 3C/6.  Paragraph 1.  The CHAIRMAN thought that paragraph could  very well be combined with paragraph 6.  Sir John MAUD (United Kingdom) did not see  any great difference between the functions men-  tioned in paragraph (1) and those in paragraph
(2).     Mr. BESTERMAN (Head of the Department of  Exchange of Information) explained that the term  “documentation” had a different meaning in the  two paragraphs : in paragraph (1), documentation  meant the possible provision by the library of  copies (microfilms, photostats) or abstracts of  books the Secretariat was unable to obtain; in  paragraph (2), on the other hand, documentation  meant research for the purpose of obtaining back-  ground information.  Paragraph 2.  The CHAIRMAN wanted to know whether the  list of “specialists” given in that paragraph corres-  ponded to all the fields with which the Programme  Departments were concerned and, if not, why  those fields had been chosen in preference to  others.  Mr. BESTERMAN (Head of the Department of  Exchange of Information) explained that the  scheme was primarily an experiment and that the  specialists mentioned had been selected because  the corresponding Departments were very anxious  to have such help. Those Programme Depart-  ments which could provide for their own needs  would do their work without the necessity for  specialists in the World Centre for the Exchange  of Information.  Dr. ZEISSL (Austria), in his turn, referred to  the confusion caused by the use of the term  “dissemination” in resolution 5.8. The Yearbook,  to which the Deputy Director-General had referred,  seemed likely to duplicate a similar publication  by the International Bureau of Education in Ge-  neva; would it not also appear among the publi-  cations referred to in paragraph (4)?  The CHAIRMAN explained that that question  would be studied when the Committee considered  item 5 of the Agenda. The CHAIRMAN remarked that the Deputy  Director-General had already given all the neces-  sary explanations on that point.  Paragraph 5.  Sir John MAUD (United Kingdom) wished to  know the scope and extent of the statistical acti-  vities of the “World Centre”; was it to be a special  service or was it only to collect statistics?  Mr. BESTERMAN (Head of the Department of  Exchange of Information) explained that, in the  matter of statistics, Unesco had special responsi-  bilities towards the United Nations under the  Agreement between Unesco and the United Na-  tions; the Department of Exchange of Information,  acting in co-operation with the other Departments  of the Secretariat concerned, had a definite sta-  tistical programme. In addition, its services were  at the disposal of other Unesco Departments to  furnish them with any information they might  require.  The CHAIRMAN reminded the meeting that the  Committee was not called upon to study the ques-  tion of the budget, but the efficiency of the World  Centre which was to perform the services; above  all, they had to discover whether better results  could be obtained with a centralized or a de-  centralized organization. She asked Mr. Luther  H. Evans of the Library of Congress in Washing-  ton for his opinion on that point.            Mr. EVANS (United States of America) indicated  the difficulties encountered by the Library of Con-  ogress and how it had tried to overcome them.  As a basic principle, a central service was intended  to help the Programme Departments; the latter  should be satisfied with the help given. Unesco's  programme contained a vast area within which  all the Departments wanted the same kind of  information; it therefore seemed well to have a  centralized source from which each could draw  what it wanted. As for the relative merits of  centralization and decentralization, Mr. Evans  thought that, in the situation depicted by the  Deputy Director-General and Mr. Besterman, cen-  tralization would be better, provided that certain  conditions were observed. An essential condition  was that the staff of the World Centre should be  sufficiently specialized and expert to meet the  requirements of the Programme Departments;  good administrative relations between the one and  the others were indispensable to achieve that  confidence which could be won only by the wish  to serve and not by the desire to set up a little  private kingdom. In conclusion, it appeared  that those who were responsible for laying down  the policies of the Organization should examine  and continually re-define the functions of the De-  partment of Exchange of Information.  The CHAIRMAN thought that the Secretariat  ought to amend its proposals to avoid the diffi-  culties of terminology mentioned by several dele-  gates.  Sir John MAUD (United Kingdom) pointed out,  in that connexion, that the resolution of the  Mexico City Conference and the current year's  draft resolution 5.8 contemplated the dissemi-  nation of certain information. It seemed, how-  ever, that the Committee was agreed that, for the  time being, the Department of Exchange of Infor-  mation should confine itself to collecting infor-  mation for the internal use of the Secretariat only.  Sir John, therefore, thought that it was not enough  to revise the wording of the work plan proposed  by the Director-General to meet the general aims  of the Organization; for that reason, and having  regard to what might be considered the opinion  of the “users”, he proposed the following draft  resolution :  The General Conference invites the Director-General  to reconsider as soon as possible the organization and
428  title of the World Centre for the Exchange of Infor-  mation, and the work plan suggested for 1949, with a  view to possible reduction in the scope of its activities,  especially those concerned with the provision of infor-  mation services to the Programme Departments by  specialists, and those concerned with statistics, in  order to avoid duplication with work necessarily  coming within the scope of Programme Departments.  The CHAIRMAN asked that the text of the reso-  lution should be distributed to delegates the fol-  lowing morning.  The DEPUTY DIRECTOR-GENERAL emphasized  that time was an essential factor in the develop-  ment of an international organization. He did  not deny that he was a little distressed to hear  it said that the organization of the World Centre  had proved defective. The Deputy Director-Gen-  eral retraced the history of that organization and  showed that, although it dated back to early 1947,  it was not until July 1948 that the Director-Gen-  eral had been able to secure the services of the  staff needed for the accomplishment of the World  Centre’s duties. Since then, requests from the  Programme Departments proved how much they  appreciated the usefulness of that central service;  there might have been some friction, but good  working relations had gradually been established  between the Exchange of Information Department  and the various Departments which called upon  its services. The form of organization developed  avoided the overlapping which would result if a  decentralized system was adopted and, before long,  the centralized system would prove itself more  effective and more economical.  M. SEYDOUX (France) pointed out that certain  members of the Executive Board had drawn atten-  tion to serious disadvantages that might follow  from that policy.  The DEPUTY DIRECTOR-GENERAL felt justi-  fied in saying that the discussion on that point  had not gone very far and that, in any case, no  general opinion had found expression and the  Board had adopted no resolution. However, In  proposing the system under discussion, the Dir-  ector-General had taken account of the obser-  vations made by the Executive Board.
