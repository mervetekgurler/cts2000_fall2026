SIXTH MEETING  Friday, 26 May 1950, at 10 a.m.  Chairman:  Examination of the Budget Estimates (con-  tinued) [document 5C/5 (II)].  Mass Communication [Part III, Chapter 6].  PROGRAMME Costs [III.6.A].  [1] Mr. WEEDEN (Australia) thought the staff  of the Director’s Office was rather large, although  the Department itself was not the largest in the  Organization.  [2] Mr. POLLOCK (Canada) queried the necessity  for the posts of Executive Officer (grade 12),  Administrative Officer (grade 10) and Assistant  (grade 6), in view of the elaborate structure of  the remainder of the Department.  [3] Mr. SCHNEIDER (Director, Department of  Mass Communication) explained that the relative  size of the Director’s personnel was conditioned  by the fact that the Department had much more  varied activities than the others. The Executive  Officer was the Director’s personal representative:  he supervised all the Division’s work, dealt with  correspondence and received reports of outside  groups on purely Programme activities. The  Administrative Officer and the Assistant looked  after liaison with the Administrative, Budget and  Personnel units.  [4] Mr. POLLOCK (Canada) drew attention to  the disproportion between the total number of  posts of the Department (111) and the number  of secretaries, stenographers and typists (41): the  Division of Improvement of Means and Tech-  niques, for instance, comprised as many as seven  secretaries.  [6] Mr. SCHNEIDER (Director, Department of  Mass Communication) stressed that the general  means of communication such as radio scripts,  Special articles, press releases (in many copies),  etc., demanded a higher proportion of clerical  assistance than in the other departments.  [6] | Sir Ronald ADAM (United Kingdom) thought  considerable economies could be made by having  à stenographic pool instead of personal secre-  taries.     Dr. FF. BEnpEr (Netherlands)  [7] Mr. WEITZ (Head, Bureau of Administra-  tive Management and Budget) explained that  most of the secretaries shown were not attached  to a particular person; they worked for any  member of their division who happened to need  it. It was a fact, however, that the efficiency of  secretarial staff was increased by assigning them  repeatedly to the same type of work. The grade
6 secretary was attached to the head of the div-  ision, while those of grade 4 took bilingual  dictation in shorthand from the programme  specialists and assistants.  [8] Mr. ROSEMAN (United States of America)  thought the whole question of secretaries and  clerical staff ought to be discussed in a general  way and the CHAIRMAN agreed with him.  IMPROVEMENT OF MEANS AND TECHNIQUES OF CoM-  MUNICATION [III.6.B].  [9] Mr. ROSEMAN (United States of America)  stated that his delegation was very interested in  paragraph 1 (page 224). He asked whether the  general trend was towards an increase or a  decrease of clearing-house services.  [10] Mr. SCHNEIDER (Director, Department of  Mass Communication) explained that in 1949  there had been no clearing-house; in 1950 the  service was being prepared and started, and it  would be fully fledged in 1951. The clearing-  house services would make available the informa-  tion for which there were numerous requests, and  the standing consultative committees would be a  permanent core of highly qualified experts to be  consulted by correspondence.  [11] Mr. WEEDEN (Australia) hoped National  Commissions would be consulted in the formation  of those committees. He asked for information  concerning Other Contractual Items ($7,000).  [12] Mr. SCHNEIDER (Director, Department of  Mass Communication) assured him that National  Commissions would be applied to; in fact the  clearing-house idea was the development of a sug-  gestion made by a member of a National Commis-  sion. The $7,000 could be broken down into  two parts: $5,000 for the continuation of a service  which had existed since the very beginning of         Unesco for the collection of press clippings and  £2,000 for specific clippings on Mass Communica-  tion matters.  In reply to Mr. ROSEMAN (United States of  America), Mr. SCHNEIDER stated that the press  clippings service collected and analysed major  articles mentioning Unesco, on a weekly basis.  [13] The CHAIRMAN, speaking as delegate of  the Netherlands and referring to paragraph 11 on  page 224, thought the cost of the surveyors rather  high, especially for reports which might be  obsolete by the time they were issued. He asked  whether it was really necessary to have an expert  committee costing $8,000, when the printing of  its report cost $7,000.  [14] Mr. WEEDEN (Australia) held that costly  personal visits of Unesco officials to remote coun-  tries like his own could often be dispensed with,  and the same results achieved by correspondence.
1156] Mr. SCHNEIDER (Director, Department of  Mass Communication) agreed that it was not im-  possible to obtain information by correspondence,  but pointed out that this method was usually ex-  tremely slow. The Unesco officers who went on  these journeys established very useful personal  contacts for the future, which would help to keep  the information up to date after the survey period  was over, that is after 1951. The 1947 surveys  had revealed the fact that salary, per diem, etc.,  was a more expensive method than the payment  of fees. Finally, the findings of the surveys  would be submitted to meetings of experts and  representatives of the countries surveyed for  approval and advice as to the action to be taken,  not only in 1951, but also in years to come.  [16] Mr. ROSEMAN (United States of America)  wished to know the number of clippings collected  over a certain period and to see a sample of an  analysis. He asked how much staff-time would  be devoted to the activities under paragraph m1  and what financial basis there was for the state-  ment that it was cheaper to employ outside sur-  veyors rather than staff members.  [17] Mr. SCHNEIDER (Director, Department of  Mass Communication) stated that the number of  clippings varied from 600 to 800 a week, rising to
1,000 during major conferences. He informed the  committee that he would cause an analysis to be  sent from Paris immediately. Keeping the  surveys up to date absorbed about 10 per cent of  staff-time, and this figure would increase in pro-  portion to the number of countries surveyed; there  were some thirty left. The reason why surveyors  were cheaper to use than staff members was that  they received a flat fee of $100 a week.

The Ex-  ecutive Board believed that Unesco could and  should promote trade agreements of benefit to  its aims. He felt certain that a good deal could  also be accomplished by Unesco at Bangkok and  Torquay. Dates for the 1951 conferences had not yet  been fixed and no possibility of economy was  being overlooked by the Department.
