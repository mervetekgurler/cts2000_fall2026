Discussion of the Draft Programme for 1952 (continuation) [document 6C/5 (I)]. Cultural Activities (continuation). PROTECTION OF WRITERS AND ARTISTS. Items 4.31 and 4.32. [2] Mr. SIWASARIYANON (Thailand) drew atten- tion to the need for regulating the exercise of copy- right in a manner likely to promote the spread of knowledge and to further international understand- ing. He suggested that the parties concerned should negotiate with authors, through Unesco, for the reduction or abolition of authors’ royalties for trans- lation in certain cases; in countries like his own, with a comparatively small reading public, those rights were instrumental in restricting the spread of knowledge. [3] Thailand did not produce enough paper and newsprint to meet its needs, and the cost of import- ing paper and newsprint was continually rising. Furthermore, translators were few and their services costly. The payment of high royalties for transla- tion, over and above the actual cost of publication, was raising the price of production to such an extent that it actually discouraged the publication of new translated work. Mr. Siwasariyanon, therefore, appealed to Unesco for some action to improve the situation. [4] Mr. HABIB (India) suggested that, in view of the impending meeting of the Bureau of the Inter- national Commission for the preparation of a scienti- fic and cultural history of mankind, the Programme Commission should defer discussion of draft Reso- lutions 4.31 and 4.32 and pass immediately to item 4.48. [6] Agreed. Item 4.48. [6] The CHAIRMAN opened discussion on item 4.48 (schema for a Scientific and Cultural History of Mankind). [7] The Budget Committee had made the follow- ing observations in its preliminary report (docu- ment 6C/BUD/1): According to a documentdistributed to the Budget Com- mittee, the expenditure contemplated by the Commission would be $70,560 in 1952. The Committee wishes to draw the attention of the Conference to the special character of the administrative arrangements proposed, and regrets that Unesco plans to relinquish control over the staff, administration and publications of a commission to which some $400,000 will be allocated for a period of five years. It considers, in any case, that the running expenses are rather high. [8] Mr. THOMAS(Director, Department of Cultural Activities) wished to make it clear that the Secre- tariat’s request for appropriations did not relate to the sum of $91,000, but to $68,000, which might be reduced to $55,000. Moreover, the postponement of certain activities would permit new savings without impairing the execution of the programme. [9] Mr. de BERREDO CARNEIRO (Brazil) con- firmed the information given by Mr. Thomas: the Bureau had made a detailed study of document 6C/ PRG/12 (Annual Report of the President of the International Commission for a Scientific and Cul- tural History of Mankind) in order to adapt it to the financial restrictions imposed by Unesco. A saving of $13,000 had been effected, which would bring the appropriations requested for the scientific and cultural history of mankind down to $55,000. Expenditures on supplies, correspondence, reproduc- tion of documents and general expenses had been reduced from $5,700 to $4,200. Fees to contri- butors had been cut from $15,000 to $5,000; lastly, the $2,500 contemplated for the preparation of maps, diagrams, etc., had been cut to $1,000. [10] Mr. BLINKENBERG (Denmark) had to report that the Danish National Commission had made very serious criticisms of the project. In the first place it found that, in spite of the support of a number of international learned societies, it was doubtful whether it would be possible to reach the necessary unanimity. Whether consciously or unconsciously     IV-9 PROGRAMME such a work would be explicitly or implicitly based upon a philosophy of history, without which it would be nothing but a mass of material devoid of significance. But Unesco could not and should not propound a philosophy of history. The second objection was economic: the appropriations for the financial year 1952 had been successively reduced from $91,000 to $68,000 and then to $55,000, but that series of cuts did not, unfortunately, affect the total expenditure of $400,000 over the five-year period. If need were, it would doubtless be possible to count upon income from the sale of the work, but at the moment the budget ceiling imposed un- avoidable economies. If the project were examined on the basis of the criteria set forth by the Economic and Social Council it could be given only a very low priority. It was obviously too late to ask for its cancellation, but the Danish delegation strongly urged the Conference to keep to the original project and to vote a second appropriation of $24,000, pend- ing consideration of a third and final appropriation. At the same time, the Conference should point out that, at the end of the third year of preparation, Unesco should have at its disposal complete and final material for the work, so that one or more publishing houses might undertake its issue. [11] Mr. BRANDT (Switzerland), on behalf of his delegation, warmly welcomed the proposal submitted by the Secretariat. It was undeniably one of the most important items in the programme, and the idea, first decided on by the Mexico Conference, had been repeatedly confirmed by later Conferences. As explained in the report of the President of the Inter- national Commission, it was not a matter of adding one more to the many histories of the world, but of writing a comparative history of the scientific and cultural evolution of man. There was every reason to expect a resounding success. There should be no postponement of such important work, for that would create a bad precedent, and Unesco would lose the confidence shown in it by so many eminent men of learning. In the conviction that Unesco would never make that moral surrender, Mr. Brandt strongly appealed to the Commission to approve the project in its new guise. [12] Mr. van ZYL (Union of South Africa) shared the Danish delegate’s view. He realized that, owing to commitments already entered into, the work could not be postponed, as he would otherwise have advised; but since there seemed to be a considerable degree of scepticism about the value of such a history, he would vote for the lowest figure proposed. [13] Mr. UNGARETTI (Italy) felt obliged to make certain reservations about the advisability of decid- ing on the project at that juncture, in view of the painful but necessary economies with which Unesco COMMISSION 326 was faced. The publication in question was not really urgent, and its planning would involve much detailed study beforehand. He therefore asked the Commission to postpone decision and to request the Director-General to take all necessary steps to submit a new programme to the next session of the General Conference. [14] Mr. van der BRUGGEN (Belgium) said that the form of the plan as it stood made it possible for the Belgian delegation to withdraw the criticisms which it had at first formulated on the methods to be used for carrying it out. The delegation had been especially alarmed by the effect which lack of contact with world public opinion would certainly have upon the plan. The policy set out in the new report showed a decided improvement in that respect and guaranteed that the comparative history of the evolution of mankind would not be conceived in a vacuum. [15] Mr. LHERISSON (Haiti) believed that the publication of such a treatise would constitute a necessary and important achievement. He hoped the meeting would vote in favour of Resolution 4.48 and of the provision of $55,000 for its implementa- tion. [16] Mr. MISSINGHAM (Australia) also supported the Danish delegate's view. He felt that the project might be financed by organizations other than Unesco and would therefore vote in favour of the lowest figure mentioned. [17] Mr. STODDARD (United States of America) recalled that, during the Fifth Session of the General Conference, a group of delegates had attempted to arrange the work of Unesco under 10 headings. The Scientific and Cultural History of Mankind was, in his opinion, one of the most important of Unesco's projects under one of those headings, since it would demonstrate the cultural interdependence of man- kind. It was, moreover, a project which could be carried through by an individual; if it were, its very success would make it suspect. [18] The ingenious system of continuous publi- cation had made the beginning of the work very difficult, but having made that beginning Unesco should on no account abandon or postpone the project. Delegations had expressed the viewthat a minimum appropriation should be voted. That minimum had been put by the Secretariat at the figure of $55,000, and Mr. Stoddard appealed to the Commission not to endanger the success of the project by reducing the budgetary provision. [19] Mr. Stoddard suggested that there was no need for the prevailing pessimism about the revenue to be expected from sales. Although the six volumes would be primarily works of reference, they could        PROG RAM ME and should be accompanied by textbooks and popular versions, which would, in his view, produce adequate revenue. [20] The Commission should not underrate the importance of the History or fail to recognize the excellent influence which it would have among the most independent and cultured elements of society, who were accustomed to work alone and in whom it would induce the feeling that they were united in the service of a common cause. [21] Tt was, therefore, no pro forma support which the United States delegation gave to the project. It considered that the preparation of such a history was one of Unesco’s finest projects, and it was in that belief that it would vote for the appropriation of a sum of $55,000 and would recommend that course to the favourable attention of the Commission. [22] Mr. CAIN (France) said that the extremely important speech by the United States delegate had simplified his task; by referring to previous sessions of the Conference, he had accentuated the duty imposed on Unesco by the law of continuity. Public opinion would certainly be profoundly sur- prised and disappointed if Unesco, at the moment of embarking upon sucha large-scale project, decided to suspend it, after the successive votes of five conferences in its favour. It might be true that the whole principle of the undertaking was open to question, as the Danish delegate had suggested, but Unesco had never contemplated a philosophy of history. As Mr. Cain sawit, it was simply a question of uniting in a common cause men who belonged to different branches of learning and had until then worked alone, and prevailing upon them to bring to the joint work the sum total of scientific knowledge acquired up to the present day. It was obvious that books should be given the place they deserved as a means of intellectual enlightment, but there was obviously something to be said for postponing the enormous undertaking to less difficult times; the preparatory work had however been going on for several years, and a few months previously, scientists and experts had been called into service and had abandoned their normal work for the purpose. If their contracts were broken, their collaboration could not be ensured a year later. An appropriation of $24,000 would be totally inadequate. The project would either have to be dropped immediately or the minimum credit of $55,000 would have to be voted for it. [23] Sir Philip HENDY (United Kingdom) said that, although at the Fifth Session of the General Conference his delegation had supported the creation of an autonomous commission for the preparation of the Scientific and Cultural History of Mankind, it then felt some misgivings about the cost. He asked whether a detailed plan of the work had been COMMISSION submitted to professional publishers for their advice, and whether there had been any estimates of the income on sales to be expected within a specified period, say, five years. [24] Mr. de BERREDO CARNEIRO (Brazil) felt obliged to point out to those who considered it necessary to enlarge the scope of the History of Mankind, that the past had never seen an example of such a vast undertaking, as was witnessed by the wide selection of eminent people who were members of the International Commission and the 80 special- ists collaborating with them. The project would be the fruit of an immense scheme of international co- operation, whence would emerge a global conception of the evolution of mankind. It would not be tinged with any particular philosophy of science or art; its quarterly issues would give an objective comparison of every viewpoint, explaining the facts with the comments and criticisms they had aroused. The work would therefore constitute a tremendous inter- national debate on every aspect of human history. Its high standard would be guaranteed by the emi- nence of the contributors, who would shortly include members of the Arab world and Far Eastern countries. An appropriation of $24,000 would simply be a waste of money. The Commission would have to choose between substantial support or a categori- cal refusal. [25] Item 4.48 was put to the vote in the following amended form proposed by the Executive Board [document 6C/5(T) Corrigendum 1]: The Director General is authorized: to conclude the necessary arrangements for the pre- paration of a Scientific and Cultural History of Mankind with the International Commission created for this purpose. [26] It was adopted by 28 votes to 3, with 4 abs- tentions. [27] The proposed appropriation of $24,000 sug- gested by the Danish delegation was rejected by
24 votes to 5, with 3 abstentions. [28] The appropriation of $55,000 proposed by the Secretariat was adopted by 27 votes to 4. As an amendment (document 6C/DR/14) the Austrian delegation had proposed: [29] (a) That the Director-General be instructed by the General Conference to appoint an international committee of historians and teachers of history for the purpose of working out a concise Outline of World History, pre- ferably in two versions, one adapted to the age of approximately 14, the other to the age of approximately
18 years; (b) That the Director-General be instructed by the General Conference to prepare the draft of a Convention     IV-10 PROGRAMME in which Member States pledge themselves to distribute copies of the said Outline to all children before gra- duation from Primary, Extended Primary and Secondary Schools respectively. [30] Mr. EVANS (Representative of the Executive Board) said that the Board’s view had been that, if draft Resolution 4.48 were approved, the Austrian proposal should not be accepted. [31] Mr. RITSCHL (Austria) said that the intention behind his proposal was not that school textbooks should be replaced by the Outline of World History but that the booklet should be used as a guide to children. It would not be necessary to have original contributions, as a satisfactory guide of the type he had in mind could be compiled from material already published. It would contain information on cultural history distinct, as far as possible, from political history and would not only serve as a guide to children but would be useful to those responsible for the presentation of history in school textbooks. [32] Mr. THOMAS (Director, Department of Cul- COMMISSION 328 tural Activities) recalled that, when the project for a Scientific and Cultural History of Mankind was first submitted, the intention had always been, after its publication, to produce a shorter book which would be easy reading for a wider public, as well asa textbook for schoolchildren. He would therefore like to ask the Austrian delegate whether he did not think that the project described in the amendment would be completely realized if the International Commission could compile the proposed brief out- line of world history in the same objective spirit from the complete Scientific and Cultural History. [33] As for the draft convention mentioned in the second part of the amendment, it raised a rather complex legal question, for the preparation of a draft convention could not be considered until after completion of the book. [34] As the proposal of the Austrian delegation was not seconded, it was not put to the vote. The meeting rose at 6.45 p.m. TENTH MEETING Monday, 2 July 1951, at 10.30 a.m. Chairman: Mr. Jean Pager (Switzerland) Statement by the Director-General on Korea. [1] The DIRECTOR-GENERAL made the following statement: [2] Mr. Chairman, ladies and gentlemen, on 25 June
1950, the Republic of Korea was invaded by armed forces coming from North Korea. That very day, the Security Council was in session. It declared that this action constituted an act of aggression and demanded cessation of hostilities. [3] This means that it has taken more than a year for this appeal to be answered. [4] It means first and foremost that the Security Council, seeing that its request of 27 June remained unanswered, was obliged to decide upon military measures for the re-establishment of international peace and security. [5] Let there be no doubt, ladies and gentlemen, that history will one day acclaim this painful enterprise as an event of immense significance: the first practical   application on the international scale of the principle of collective security. [6] What this vital demonstration has cost in destruc- tion and suffering we know only too well. For a twelve- month, tens of thousands of fighting men have fallen in bloody battles. For a twelvemonth, hundreds of thousands of innocent men, women and children have known the indescribable horrors of modern warfare. Towns have been wiped out, villages razed to the ground, schools destroyed, monuments and places of culture devastated. [7] Unesco could not remain indifferent to this tragedy. On 14 August 1950, the Economic and Social Council invited the Specialized Agencies, under the terms of their agreements with the United Nations, to lend such assistance as the United Nations Unified Command in Korea might request to come to the aid of the civil popu- lation of Korea, and moreover emphasized the need to obtain the understanding and support of the peoples for the action undertaken by the United Nations in Korea.
