6.1 Mr CLARK (Canada) :  Thank you, Mr President. International organizations need dynamism,  dedication and objectivity in their management, particularly at senior levels.  They benefit from a continuing process of appraisal and the injection of new  ideas.
6.2 A periodic change of leadership, on a clear, predictable basis, would  serve these objectives. In turn, this would make international organizations  responsive to current needs an6 more effective in the service of their  memberships.
6.3 At the same time, it is necessary to establish a proper balance. Heads  of organizations who are performing well should have the opportunity to serve for  a sufficient period to contribute significantly to the work of their agencies.
6.4 Achievement of these objectives would be promoted if a limit were placed  on the length in office of heads of United Nations agencies. An upper limit of  two terms in office would provide an appropriate balance between the need to  ensure benefits of continuity while guarding against any tendency towards  rigidity.
6.5 The Constitutions of UNIDO, ITU and UPU place an upper limit of two  terms of office for their Executive Heads. The Constitution of UNIDO is the most  recent expression of the will of the international community.
6.6 In the case of Unesco, the Director-General - as you all well know - is  elected for a six-year term. A limit of two terms would allow a Director-General  who enjoyed the confidence of the membership to serve for up to 12 years.
6.7 Mr President, the Canadian delegation believes that there would have  been considerable advantage in amending Unesco's Constitution at this session.  Member States would have thus been assured that in establishing an important  principle in their Organization they were acting without reference to the  particular situation of any individual person.
6.8 However, Mr President, while we regret that the decision of the Legal  Committee was to recommend deferment of a decision in this matter until the  twenty-€ifth session, we were impressed by the argument that the question  requires fxrther study and concerted action by Kember States on previously agreed  criteria to be applied throughout the United Nations system as a whole. 24.1 Mrs van LIS DONATA (Aruba):  Thank you, Mr President. I would like with your permission to refer to  the fourteenth report of the Legal Committee (document 24 C/131) approved a  moment ago. I would like to draw your attention to the fact that the Constitution  and certain rules and regulations of the Organization are not always consistent  in their reference to Associate Members.
24.2 We have noticed, for instance, that on the one hand Rule 103 of the  Rules of Procedure of the General Conference stipulates that draft amendments to  the Constitution should be communicated to Member States and Associate Members at  least six months in advance, while on the other hand Article XII1 of the  Constitution, which makes the same provision, mentions only the Member States,  whereas Associate Members also have this right in accordance with resolution 41.2  on rights and obligations of Associate Members, adopted by the General Conference  at its sixth session.
24.3 As a second example, I would like to mention Article VII, paragraph 3,  of the Constitution, which gives the Organization the authority to delegate a  member of its Secretariat to serve on the National Commission of a Member State  in order to assist in the development of its work. In a similar context, the  Draft Programme and Budget for 1988-1989 (document 24 C/5) indicates in para-  graph 15461 that Unesco will provide support tor establishing National  Conunissions in Member States. There is no explicit reference to Associate Members  in this paragraph, although 6 C/Resolution 41.2, to which I have already referred  does not exclude such co-operation with Associate Members. I believe it might be  possible to mention the Associate Members in all relevant paragraphs of the  programme and budget where provisions with respect to Member States can equally  be extended to them. This, Mr President, would clarify, for both Member States  and Associate Members, the relationship of the latter to the former as well as to  the Organization as a whole.
24.4 As a third and last example, I would like to mention resolution
24 C/COM.I/DR.S, which was considered in Commission I in connection with its  examination of Major Programme XIV (The status of women). This resolution states  inter alia that the General Conference is 'convinced or the need for Member  States and Unesco' to engage in resol.ute, systematic and large-scale efforts to  ensure that women and men participate on an equal footing ...'. Mr President, the  Associate Members have, in this matter concerning the status of women, the same  need as the Member States. For that reason I wonder why the recommendations  concerning the equality of women and men are only addressed to the Member States.
24.5 kr President, this resolution and several others reflect the ambiguity  which exists with respect to the rights and obligations of Associate Members.  Resolutions adopted by the General Conference enshrine the policy the Organ-  izirtion, which includes both Member States and Associate Members, should follow.  We as Associate Members would therefore like to see these resolutions  also addressed to us, so that our governments can draw equally on their authority  for the domestic implementation of their provisions.
24.6 Mr President, the delegations of Aruba and ot the Netherlands Antilles  would like to propose either a review of all texts with a view to clarifying the  situation I have just described; or alternatively a review of 6 C/Resolution 41.2  concerning the rights and obligations of Associate Members. We will appreciate it  very much if the Executive Board takes this suggestion into account while  pursuing its review of the Constitution and other relevant texts. Thank you,  Mr President.
