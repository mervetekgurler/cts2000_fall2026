2.1 Mrs ALEXANDER (Saint Lucia):  Mr President, Mr Director-General, distinguished delegates, ladies and  gentlemen, may I on behalf of my delegation extend to you, Mr President, congratu-  lations on your election to the presidency of the twenty-second session of the  General Conference. Our congratulations go also to all the countries which have  assumed full membership of Unesco since the twenty-first session, including our  sister Member States - Antigua and Barbuda, the Bahamas, Saint Vincent and the  Grenadines, Belize and Saint Christopher and Nevis. To all of them we extend a  sincere welcome. We also extend congratulations to the delegation of the  Netherlands Antilles on that country's accession to Associate Membership. To  you, Mr Director-General, the Saint Lucia delegation expresses appreciation for  your untiring effort in the production of a Draft Programme and Budget for
1984-1985 which is in keeping with the guidelines established during the fourth  extraordinary session of the General Conference.
2.2 Mr President, distinguished delegates, due to the shocking incidents which  have occurred in Grenada, a sister Caribbean island, it is with a deep sense of  mourning that the Saint Lucia delegation attends the twenty-second session of the  General Conference of Unesco. You have already heard that the Prime Minister of  Grenada, Maurice Bishop, and many of his Cabinet Ministers were brutally murdered,  including Jacqueline Creft, who would otherwise have been here with us at this  General Conference, since as Minister of Education she was the Minister responsible  for Unesco affairs. Further, many civilians were shot to death and the whole of  Grenada's population was virtually placed under house-arrest with the threat of  shooting on sight anyone who broke the twenty-four hour curfew. This flagrant  disregard for human lives, the denial of basic freedoms, as well as the threat  to peace in the whole Caribbean resulted in the decision taken by members of the  Organization of Eastern Caribbean States - Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the  Grenadines, Dominica, Antigua and Barbuda, Saint Christopher and Nevis - as well  as the Caribbean islands of Barbados and Jamaica, to take action in the form of a  pre-emptive defeasive strike to help restore peace, freedom and dignity to the
598    people of Grenada; since we do not have huge armies and sophisticated weapons,  we sought the assistance of a friendly nation, the United States ofAmerica. In  the shortest possible time, a government of and by the people of Grenada will be  established to conduct their own affairs.
2.3 Mr President, I now turn to document 22 C/5. In my country's education  sector plan, we have stressed the goals of providing primary education for all  anderadicatingilliteracy - both of which are objectives of the Major Project on  Education in Latin America and the Caribbean. Major Programme II, 'Education for  all', is therefore of great importance to Saint Lucia, since it deals with these  aspects of education; we feel, however, that in this programme more emphasis  should have been placed on the need for the training of teachers and the provision  of teaching materials and supplies to ensure that the quality of education is not  sacrificed in our efforts to provide primary education for all our children of  school age. We, in Saint Lucia, have been fortunate in that since the terrible  devastating blow inflicted on our schools by Hurricane Allen in 1980, we have  been able to construct fourteen schools to replace schools that were destroyed.  Major extensions have been added to four other schools to help relieve acute  congestion. Bearing in mind the importance of the quality of education at the  primary level and in pursuit of the principle of self-reliance, our Education  Plan makes provision for remedial programmes in both reading and mathematics;  both programmes are already being implemented. These remedial programmes con-  stitute an effort on our part to help reduce the number of primary school-leavers  who remain functionally illiterate. We are also preparing a programme to tackle  the problems of absenteeism and drop-outs, both of which constitute important  aspects of our struggle against illiteracy.
2.4 Subprogramme 11.2.4, 'Promotion of early childhood education', is of  particular interest to us insaint Lucia, since the majority of our children in  the rural areas speak only Creole (Patois), while English is the medium of in-  struction in the schools. We need, therefore to design a special curriculum  in language skills to enable these children to overcome the language barrier and  prepare them better for school life. We are pleased to note that a Unesco mis-  sion is soon to visit Saint Lucia to carry out 2. survey ofour 77 pre-schools;  the task of eradicating illiteracy from our midst must start at this early pre-  school level.
2.5 Mr President, we have estimated that there are approximately 30,000 illi-  terates in Saint Lucia, out of a total population of 125,000: this is a situation  which adversely affects the productive life of the State. At the national level,  we have carried out a feasibility study on a literacy programme, subsequently we  were assisted by the Bonani Report, and we are now ready to benefit from training  assistance under Major Programme II, Subprogramme 11.1.3, 'Training of literacy  personnel'. The Caribbean Network for Educational Innovation and Development  (CARNEID) is currently holding a task force seminar on literacy programmes in  Saint Lucia, in the course of which the resources in the Caribbean will be pooled  to develop a framework for embarking on a literacy programme.
2.6 Our school-leavers swell the ranks of the unemployed, and it is for us  to bring about reforms in our education system to link education with the world  of work, to develop technical/vocational education and to stress the teaching of  science and technology. The provision of equipment for technical/vocational  education and for the teaching of science and technology must not be overlooked,  nor the training of teachers in this particular field. The present graphic com-  munications course sponsored by Unesco in our junior secondary schools is proving  quite successful, and it is hoped that further assistance will be derived from  Subprogramme V.3.3, 'Expansion and improvement of technical and vocational educa-  tion', to enable us to extend the course to the senior secondary-school level.  We are happy to point out that the Caribbean multi-island educational develowment  project based on the promotion and development of technical/vocational education  for the age-range of 12-15 has already reached the stage of implementation, and  includes the important aspects of teacher training and the provision of equipment.
2.1 At the secondary-school level, our main thrust will be to re-establish  and introduce science and technical/vocational subjects in the curriculum and the  plan involves the gradual conversion of some of our secondary schools into  comprehensive schools.
2.8 With assistance from Unesco, we have outlined a plan for the establishment  of nine multi-purpose centres throughout Saint Lucia, in order to impart technical/  vocational skills not only to schoolchildren but also to adults. The project has  been costed, and the various possibilities for funding are now being explored. It  is hoped that this project will help in some measure to relieve our unemployment  situation.
2.9 With respect to culture, let me recall that the need to make a concerted  effort to preserve and develop aspects of our cultural heritage was reflected in  Recommendation 19 adopted at MONDIACULT in Mexico in 1982. Major Programme XI,  'Culture and the future', has our full support, since it is hoped that this par-  ticular programme will enable us to develop the process of cultural contact and  evolution and to benefit, albeit belatedly, from the already approved UNDP/  Unesco Latin American and Caribbean regional project RLA/78/005 for the preser-  vation and development of the regional cultural heritage.
2.10 Mr President, we in the Caribbean have a culture distinct from the rest  of the region, andin order to help us preserve and develop aspects of our cul-  tural heritage we have submitted to the General Conference a draft resolution  requesting the creation of a post of subregional adviser for culture in the  Caribbean. We appreciate the limitations imposed by the $1 million Reserve  for Draft Resolutions, so we therefore suggest that serious consideration be  given to decentralizing a vacant post to the Caribbean subregion.
2.11 We are emphasizing the need for cultural research, the establishment of  an archive, the development of a curriculum in culture for our schools with a  corresponding curriculum for our teachers' college and the establishment of a  cultural centre to permit not only the expression of our indigenous culture but  also those of other cultures.
2.12 In the Latin American and Caribbean region, the Caribbean has played a  relatively peripheral role in Unesco affairs, due partly to the location of the  Regional Offices, some of which are further from the Jamaica office than is  Unesco Headquarters in Paris, but also to the difference in language between the  mainly English-speaking Caribbean and the remainder of the region. This linguis-  tic problem is reflected in the exclusion of the Caribbean from many regional  activities, since interpretation facilities are not always available. We con-  sider it advisable, therefore, to hold seminars and workshops earmarked for the  region in both languages, English and Spanish.
2.13 In Subprogramme 11.1.3, 'Training of literacy personnel', it is indicated  that the Regional Centre for Adult Education and Functional Literacy for Latin  America (CREFAL) will be responsible for the execution of a literacy personnel  training programme as part of the Major Project in Education in Latin America and  the Caribbean. As I mentioned earlier, Saint Lucia has a serious illiteracy prob-  lem, and we would certainly like to benefit from such a programme. In the past,  however, there has been little if any participation by the Caribbean in the work  of CRFFAL. We would not like this trend to continue. We do periodically receive  documents on literacy programmes from CREFAL, but they are all in Spanish, and  therefore cannot be utilized. Greater participation in literacy work can be  effected by using CARNEID. As President of the meeting on the Major Project in  the Field of Education for Latin America and the Caribbean, held in Saint Lucia  in July 1982, I recall it was agreed that CARNEID would be the organization  responsible for the Major Project activities in the Caribbean. This agreement  is not mentioned in document 22 C/5. I therefore strongly recommend that funding  be decentralized to CARNEID in Barbados for all activities relating to the Major  Project in Education for the Caribbean subregion.
2.14 Mr President, since the twenty-first session of the General Conference,  five Member States have been added to the Caribbean subgroup, making a total of  thirteen Member States. Since the Latin American/Caribbean region comprises  thirty-two Member States, we in the Caribbean subgroup form more than one-third  of the total membership, yet we only hold one out of eight seats on the Executive  Board ofUnesCo. The Caribbean is therefore grossly under-represented in this  body; we hope that the imbalance will be rectified during the present session of  the General Conference.
2.15 Mr President, it is said that experience is a wonderful master, but its  fees are terribly high. We in the Caribbean can no longer take for granted the  ideals relating to peace, human rights, freedom of peoples and to the democratic  principle. The shocking incidents in Grenada, our sister Caribbean island, have -  it is to be hoped - taught us a lesson, namely that we must be constantly vigilant  against the forces of tyranny, aggression and repression; we must beware of the  minds of those whose unstinting efforts are directed to the breakdown of all kinds  of defences for peace and human dignity the world over - a goal which flouts the  ideals embodied in the Unesco Constitution and which calls on the courage of all  of us to fight against these inhuman and destructive tendencies.
2.16 Consequently, Mr President, may I sincerely wish you, the Director-General,  the Unesco Secretariat and all the Member States of Unesco a most successful ses-  sion of the General Conference, and hope that in some way our deliberations here  will in time contribute effectively to world peace and the pursuit of human rights,  reflected in an environment of justice, freedom and progressive development for  all our people within each of our national boundaries. I thank YOU.

4.1 Mrs KAUL (India) :  mit me to associate myself with my distinguished colleagues in congratulating you  on your election. I am confident that your sagacious leadership will give the  Conference unique thrust and direction and lead us to fruitful results. I should  also like to convey our thanks to the Chairman and members ofthe Executive Board  for the work they have done. Last but not least, may I express our high apprecia-  tion of the excellent report of the Director-General. To H.E. Mr Ivo Margan, I  would like to express our deep appreciation for the grace and wisdom with which  he assumed the onerous responsibilities of President of the General Conference  twice during the last three years. I should also like to take this opportunity  to welcome the new Member States of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, Fiji and  Saint Christopher and Nevis and the new Associate Member, the Netherlands Antilles.
4.2 Mr President, we view with grave concern the recent events in Grenada.  The killing of Prime Minister Maurice Bishop and several of his Cabinet colleagues  has caused grief and dismay in India and other non-aligned countries. We were  looking forward to receiving him in November at the Commonwealth Heads of Govern-  ment Conference. Mr President, the invasion of Grenada by outside forces has  further aggravated the situation. It is inconsistent with the laws of interna-  tional behaviour. No country has any right to impose a regime or system of  government on another country. We urge the immediate withdrawal of all foreign  forces from Grenada so that it can manage its own affairs in freedom.
4.3 At a time when the contemporary world is passing through a grave crisis,  this session of the General Conference of Unesco provides us with an opportunity  to reflect on and to plan in a concerted way themes which are fundamental to our  quest for peace, co-operation, development and fulfilment. Inflationary and  adverse pressures are shattering the economies of the developing world. Mounting  tensions between the power blocs and the dangerous policy of the arms race accen-  tuate the gravity of the crisis. The reluctance of the North to extend co-operation  to the South poses a threat to peace and development. We need urgent and effective  solutions. And as we read through the pages of the draft programme and budget  presented to us by the Director-General we feel that Unesco has again risen to its  task and has not only given us a realistic appraisal of the present world situation  but also formulated for our consideration significant strategies and plans of action.  Indeed this document, faithful to the spirit and content of the second Medium-Term  Plan, has succeeded to a great extent in achieving the much desired balance between  the demands of reflection and those of action; for this, I should like to compliment  most warmly the Director-General and his colleagues in the Secretariat.
4.4 It is a paradox of our times that precisely at the moment when science  and technology offer bright prospects of universal peace and prosperity in a  foreseeable future, we are building up huge stockpiles of weapons which pose the  threat of the imminent destruction of human civilization. We must resolve this  paradox, but not in a leisurely and academic fashion. No peace today, no life  tomorrow. This urgency is manifest in the tasks that are proposed in the document  before us. And its message coincides perfectly with the New Delhi message formu-  lated at the conclusion of the Seventh Summit Conference of the Non-Aligned  Movementheldthis year, which contains an appeal to the great powers 'to halt  the arms race which is consuming at an ever increasing rate the scarce material  resources of our planet, destroying the ecological balance and wasting much of  our finest scientific talent in sterile and destructive pursuits'.
4.5 This message recommends that human skills and capacities should be used to  revitalize and restructure the world economy and that the resources released by  measures of disarmament should be diverted to promote the development of the  developing countries. The entire question hinges on political will, and respon-  sibility lies with the leaders of today to take the right decision and to change  Mr President, distinguished delegates, at the outset, Mr President, per-
60 1    gears so that the present dangerous trends are reversed. Unesco, as the conscience  of mankind, has a special responsibility to influence those factors which determine  the human powers of volition and decision. In this critical hour, therefore, we  should all join hands in strengthening the capacity and influence of Unesco. In  this context, I should like to refer particularly to Major Programmes I, XII and  XIII, which underline Unesco's determination to take a global view of the world  situation and to intensify the struggle against prejudice, intolerance, racism  and apartheid in order to foster peace, international understanding, human rights  and the rights of the peoples.
4.6 Mr President, the persistence of racism in the modern world is a crying  shame for mankind. How can the denial of freedom to the majority of the people  of South Africa and Namibia be condoned? This is a question that needs to be  asked in all earnestness. Nelson Mandela, the flaming symbol of freedom, and many  others are behind prison bars, but their voices cannot be stifled - they resound  the world over. I should like to submit the consideration to this Conference that  Unesco provide support for publications to make international public opinion more  aware of the history of the national liberation movements and the nature of the  struggle against apartheid. This can best be done under Major Programme XII.
4.7 Similarly, it is important and urgent to explicate more clearly the funda-  mental contents of the ideals of peace and international understanding. Peace is  not a negative concept. It does not mean a mere absence of war. Peace is a posi-  tive state which serves as the foundation of all human rights and duties and of  all harmonious activities. Again, when we speak of international understanding,  we do not mean mere knowledge of other countries' cultures and peoples; nor do  we mean mere exchange of students, scholars and experts among different countries,  important though they are. By international understanding, we mean a responsible  commitment to the idea and practice of the 'family of man'. In other words, in-  ternational understanding implies a commitment to that mode and ideal of con-  sciousness in which mutual dependence of each upon all and of all upon each is  indispensable. It implies a harmony of consciousness in which barriers collapse  and perverse tendencies of domination, exploitation and oppression wither away.  These deeper implications must find explicit recognition in the content and  methods of educatior:. Corresponding to the need for universa.lity and unity,  emphasis should be laid on a dialogue between the harmonization of the cultures  of the East and the West, and of humanities, sciences, fine arts and technology.  A new orientation towards the fullness of personality through a special kind of  value education is necessary for this purpose.
4.8 The new type of human being that we seek to fashion through education for  international understanding should be able to combine in himself the qualities of  the scientist and the humanist. And the important theme to which we must address  ourselves in this context is that of the synthesis of science and spirituality,  which seems to hold the key to the needed transformation of human consciousness.  This means a deeper quest and a bolder programme of research. May I suggest that  Unesco consider enlarging the scope of Major Programme XIII, which should even-  tually lead to the formation of a resource centre for education for international  understanding. The aim of such a centre would be to assist Member States to develop  the content and methods of the proposed new dimensions of education suitable to each  counkry's genius and cultural identity.
4.9 Unesco rightly rejects the view that only a few should climb the heights  of knuwledge and culture while the rest should remain forever on the lower ranges  of achievement. Voicing the cry of the greatest leaders of mankind, Unesco's  concern is not for a few but for a&; it is this concern which has a special ap-  peal to all mankind and, particularly, to the people of the developing countries.  The unprecedented explosion of the population and the rising numbers of illiterates  call for concerted action and mbtual assistance.
4.10 A special problem that holds our attention is that of education for girls  and the status of women. In the recent Conference of Ministers of Education and  Culture of the Non-Aligned and Other Developing Countries held at Pyongyong, it  was declared, in the spirit of Unesco, that 'education for all designed for the  integral welfare of all is the only acceptable ideal, and is imperatively necessi-  tated by the critical times through which humanity is passing today'. Along with  the ideals of education for all, this Conference also emphasized the idea of life-  long education and recommended that opportunities and facilities be provided to  all members of society by developing simultaneously pre-school, school and adult  education and continuing education. Special emphasis was also laid on the edu-  cation of women. In the light of this, Major Programmes II and XIV stand fully  justified, and we have no hesitation in underlining their importance.
4.11 The civilization of today is science-based andthere is anunprecedented  explosion of knowledge. Both these factors impel us to give fresh thought to  the problems of access to knowledge and to the issues relating to communication
602    and sciences and their application to development. The Draft Programme and  Budget for 1984-1985 has responded adequately to these problems and issues under  Major Programmes III, VI and VII. We appreciate, in particular, Unesco's pro-  gramme for the setting up of a regional network for the exchange of information  in science and technology in Asia and the Pacific (ACTINFO). We are happy to  note that special efforts will be devoted to preparing overall designs of the  global network of scientific and technological information. It is also gratifying  to note that the programmes relating to marine and terrestrial resources and to the  human environment have received special attention. We welcome the renewed stress  on the International Hydrological Programme, the Man and the Biosphere programme  and the International Geological Correlation Programme, in which India has been  actively involved. Adequate emphasis seems to be laid on international co-  operation in the natural sciences and their interrelationship with social and  human sciences.
4.12 There are, however, two or three important issues, particularly related  to the advancement of scientific knowledge in all parts of the world, which also  have implications for Major Programmes IV, V, VI and IX. In the first place, we  must acknowledge the disconcerting fact that, despite increasing access to  knowledge, students of the developing countries continue to depend largely on the  developed world and the facilities which they provide at exorbitant costs, in  advanced science and technology. Is it not possible, we may ask, for Unesco to  remedy this situation?
4.13 There is also the issue of collaboration among the developing countries  themselves, not only in the domain of the transfer of scientific knowledge and  technical know-how but also in the domain of cultural interchange. I am happy  to say that these questions have engaged the attention of the non-aligned and  other developing countries and a decision has been taken to strengthen South-South  co-operation along with North-South co-operation in the fields of education and  culture. It would, therefore, be gratifying if this need were reflected in various  programmes for 1984-1985, particularly those relating to access to knowledge,  science, communication and culture.
4.14 Major Programme XI describes its thrust in its very striking title:  'Culture and the future'. This futuristic thrust is imperatively demanded at a  time when the whole of mankind is preparing to build a new world. We speak today  of the need for a better international economic order. We also speak of a new  world order. In a recent speech delivered by our Prime Minister, Mrs Indira Gandhi,  at the United Nations General Assembly in her capacity as Chairperson of the Non-  Aligned Movement, reference was made to the concept of a new international order  of humanity. It is heartening, therefore, that in its draft programme and budget,  Unesco is called upon to promote knowledge of culture and to encourage greater  awareness and expression of cultural identity. It is also gratifying that our  Organization has embarked on a revision of the History of the Scientific and Cul-  tural Development of Mankind, and is to conduct studies on the impact of technol-  ogical and industrial development and to undertake a range of activities high-  lighting the specificity and the universality of the values of various cultures.
4.15 It is also heartening that due attention is proposed to be paid to pro-  grammes relating to youth. For youth is central to education and culture, just as  education and culture are central to youth. This reciprocal relationship can very  well be brought about through various programmec outlined in document 22 C/5. And  I am confident that these programmes will enable us to prepare adequately for  International Youth Year in 1985. I should like, however, to urge that special  attention be paid to such programmes as thatof Auroville in India, which aim at the  creation of permanent sites dedicated to youth, to unending education, to interna-  tional understanding and to universal harmony. The world today needs experiments  of unusual dimensions, not necessarily in quantitative but in qualitative terms.  We need to encourage, in different parts of the world, the coming together of  people from different cultural backgrounds to live together and to strive together ,  to educate and learn from each other and to establish a new collective pattern of  cultural interchange and harmonization of various streams of cultural aspirations;  this is what is being attempted at Auroville. AS Mrs Indira Gandhi said recently,  'We have to strive to create a new international order of humanity, where power  is tempered with compassion and where knowledge and capability are at the service  of all humanity'.
4.16 Mr President, the Government of India has taken note of the comprehensive  recommendations of the Executive Board concerning the Draft Programme and Budget  for 1984-1985 which were adopted at its 116th session, and of the final recommenda-  tions on the proposed budget made by the Board at its 117th session. We fully  support these recommendations. We also subscribe to the view that the financial  constraints affecting many Member States do not reduce, but rather enhance the  exigencies of reinforcing international co-operation within the framework of  Unesco's competence. Indeed, at a time when the strengthening of the human spirit
603    for the defence of peace is more necessary than ever before, and when the needs of  Member States in the fields of education, science, culture and communication are  more urgent than ever before, it is in the interest of the world community as a  whole to strengthen Unesco in every way possible so that the Organization in its  turn may help us more effectively and thus enable us to help ourselves in meeting  the challenges of national development and world peace through international co-  operation. It is in this spirit that I should like to appeal to all Member States  whose representatives are assembled here to adopt the Draft Programme and Budget  for 1984-1985 by consensus as a measure of solidarity and international co-  operation in the urgent tasks that lie ahead of us.

The effectiveness of intersectoral collaboration will be measured not by the number  of intersectoral committee discussions that take place at Headquarters, but by the  amount of on-the-spot intersectoral planning and execution which takes place in a  particular country and on a particular project. And I would point out that the  administrative machinery capable of delivering such an intersectoral impact to a  country or a region is still only in its infancy in Unesco itself, and largely non-  existent in respect of the rest of the United Nations system.
8.19 Mr President, the Director-General will certainly recall that Caribbean  Ministers of Education discussed with him in Saint Lucia last year the importance  of this intersectoral approach. As a result, Unesco has established in the  Caribbean its first intersectoral office. Undoubtedly this office will be more  responsive to the priorities of our subregion. The intersectoral team approach  is already proving valuable in analysing the problems of the countries of the  subregion and in developing strategies that are even inter-agency in their scope.
612    The effectiveness of this approach will be considerably enhanced by the appoint-  ment of a science adviser and by creating and filling the post of cultural adviser.  Nevertheless, there are still administrative problems to be overcome in decentral-  izing to the intersectoral office the financial resources and decision-making  capacity required for achieving a rapid response to the subregion's priorities.

In our view there is now need for the composition of the Executive Board  to reflect the results of the historical evolution brought about by the process of  decolonization in our region. This, we believe, will be compatible with Unesco's  practice and with equitable relations between states.

Unesco is particularly well placed to prepare the ground in this respect, since it  works in key areas and can do practical, down-to-earth work. At the same time it  is important that Unesco co-ordinate its efforts with those of other United Nations  Organizations, in order to avoid duplication.
18.6 Mr President, let me move on to another important part of the Draft Pro-  gramme, the one dealing with communication. It is hardly necessary to point out  the importance of access to systems of information and communication. Here  inequalities exist within countries as well as between countries. Developing  countries, in particular, face great difficulties when they try to establish com-  munication and information systems adapted to all sectors of society, literate as  well as illiterate. Expensive advanced technology may not always be the best  solution. To develop and adapt new technology to suit today's needs and at the  same time use and improve older local forms of communication becomes a vital prob-  lem, which is linked with the question of cultural identity. A balance must be  struck between the need for international impulses and the development of the  national cultural heritage.
18.7 The International Programme for the Development of Communication shows that  Unesco is prepared to involve itself actively in solving the problems of developing  countries in this field. There are aspects, however, which I feel should have been brought out more clearly,  notably the obstacles created by censorship and the role which should be played by  the press in exposing abuse of power. Our general attitude to communication prob-  lems is determined by a wish to secure the free flow of information across national  borders and to strengthen cultural ties between nations through the exchange of  experience and knowledge, which we see as the only way to international understand-  ing and peaceful development.

I should like in this connection to stress  the fundamental role that the fight for human rights should play in the everyday  work of Unesco. We have not, unfortunately, made sufficient strides forward in  our endeavour to prevent violations of human rights. Every day, these rights are  violated in most parts of the world.
18.11 But there are also some signs of hope. I think we have made progress in  our efforts to focus on such violations and to awaken public opinion.
