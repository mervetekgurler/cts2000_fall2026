But though  material resources may be inadequate, there  remain the intangible resources of “our faith, our  will and our devotion’—in a word, our readiness  for sacrifice. Civilized peoples are, by instinct  and natural inclination, pacific. History—unwritten history—tells us that  arms are always taken up in the name of the  people, without regard to the justice or otherwise  of the cause. While we cannot escape from  history, we can no more escape the moral conse-  quences of our own actions; just as the shadow  follows the body, and we create an inner philo-  sophy that is externalized in our own conduct, so,  consciously or unconsciously, we create a religion  from the process of our everyday thoughts. To  use poetical language, charity is “the milk of  human kindness”, and the justice to be safe-  guarded is that which, like that of light, is “la  justice aux armes sans pitié”. This kind of criticism can be  brought to silence by more information and I hope  the Director-General will take into consideration a  little more than has been the case so far the fact  that Unesco’s publications of a more general char-  acter cannot, in a majority of the Member States,  reach the “man in the street”, as long as they are  available only in English, French or Spanish.  Only in, at the most, 25 of the 55 Member States,  are these three languages universally understood.  [71] A word we have heard again and again  during these discussions is the word concentra-  tion, but delegates seem to be at variance on what  kind of concentration is meant. Unesco's work  must, by its very nature, be multifarious. It is  very easy to pick up a single item, for instance,  the determination of the tone pitch of A in the  musical scale and make a witty and malicious  remark on “fiddling while Rome burns”, etc.  This is the kind of unjust criticism we are wont  to meet in chatty articles. The kind of concentra-  tion we would like to recommend has more to do  with priority and quality, along the lines suggested  by the leader of the United States delegation. We  have for Unesco a very restricted budget and I am  sure that many governments (like mine) have  instructed their delegates to vote against any rise  of the budget ceiling. We may regret this but  we, like all bodies of this kind, are obliged to cut  our coat according to our cloth. As one of the  members of this Conference, who has attended  all meetings of the General Conference, I have the  impression that not only the Director-General’s  Report, but also the discussion on this Report have  become more realistic and business-like. Prob-  lems cannot be solved by eloquent speeches, but  only by action. We hope that, in short, the  burden of the Secretariat will be made easier by       the adoption of the proposal for biennial confer-  ences, and that the Secretariat—and here 1 quote  the Report—will get rid of this Conference men-  tality and find time to think of the programme  activities instead of writing reports which are ends  in themselves.  [72] We hope that by these few remarks we have  made clear our position and have succeeded in  convincing this body of our spirit of co-operation,  of our desire to take our job seriously, of our con-  fidence in the Director-General and of our loyalty  to Unesco.     [73] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] 1 call upon the delegate of Norway.  [74] Mr. Hans MOHR (Norway) — Mr. President,  delegates, the Norwegian delegation entirely agrees  with those speakers who have expressed their  admiration for the excellent work done by the  Director-General and the Secretariat. In this  connexion, the Norwegian delegation would like  to join the United States delegation in expressing  the wish that Unesco’s work in Germany should  be intensified.  [75] The Norwegian delegation admits that there  may be things in the work of Unesco which may  be criticized. We think there are too many  projects, a fact which again necessitates too much  administration. But here we must remember  that the projects are given to ‘he Director-General  by the General Conference. It is for the General  Conference to reduce the number of projects.  [76] One important criticism was, it seems to  the Norwegian delegation, incorrect and unjust.  It has been said that Unesco has not succeeded  in making itself a real living organism in the  world, in the thinking of the peoples, in the minds
119  of men. The Norwegian delegation considers that  if this criticism is addressed to the Director-  General and the Secretariat, the Director-General  has every right to forward it to the Member States  and to the National Commissions. The National  Commissions in most countries have certainly an  unpleasant feeling of not having done enough.  At any rate we in the Norwegian National Com-  mission have that feeling. One reason is the dif-  ficult financial situation. Unesco's task 1s to  direct, to stimulate, to spread ideas, and Unesco  has done that. The task of the National Commis-  sions 1s to do information work in their own  countries. We, the Member States and the Na-  tional Commissions, have to make Unesco a living  organism, each in our own country, and the  Norwegian delegation entirely agrees with the  Director-General when he in his Report under-  lines the responsibilities of the National Commis-  sions. The Norwegian delegation would like to  express the wish and the hope that we all, during  this Session, may have a growing feeling of the  importance of the National Commissions, and that  after the Session, we may return to our countries  with a clear understanding of the responsibility  of the National Commissions and of the necessity  for the National Commissions and Member States  to do better work, to do our best.
