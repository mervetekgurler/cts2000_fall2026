2.4 Mr. President, the Director-General, both in his opening address and in the documents he hag  laid before this Conference, made it clear that Unesco can only expect its activities to be Success.  ful if they are based on an analysis of the major problems the world is facing, and if it seeks ways  to further a solution of these problems through education, science, culture and communication, Tf  we look back upon the two years that have elapsed since the preceding session and if we survey the  world around us today, we see contrasting developments, some of them encouraging, others giving  cause for anxiety, A joining of forces for peace and the fact that the realities of today's world have  been widely recognized have resulted in a strengthening of the movement for détente in world affairs,  One highlight of this process was reached in Helsinki in 1975, at the Conference on Security and  Co-operation in Europe; in the Final Act of that Conference, European leaders declared their inten-  tion to turn Europe into a continent of peace, security and co-operation. For her part Hungary also  assumed her share of effort in each of these undertakings.
2.6 Mr. President, ever since the 1960s, Unesco has been giving increasing attention to the prob-  lems faced by the developing countries. All developed countries will, I am sure, agree that this  is both natural and necessary. In our opinion, Unesco's efforts have been commendable in this field  over the past two years, for the number of ministerial conferences that have been held and, above  all, for the comprehensive document, now before this General Conference, which lists the tasks  Unesco is expected to undertake to help establish a new international economic order, to which  the recent Colombo conference of non-aligned nations devoted so much attention, Hungary's con-  tribution towards Unesco's work for the developing countries involves chiefly the sending of experts  and organizing post-graduate courses. Our professional people are willing to participate in such  activities partly because they are ready to offer assistance, and also because they are thus given  opportunities of improving their own knowledge, broadening their intellectual outlook and gaining  a better understanding of the world's problems,

We also deplore the fact that,  despite the move to cut down administration costs, 60 per cent of the draft budget is still appropri-  ated for personnel and administrative expenditure.

8.1 Mr. HA KOO YEON (Republic of Korea):  Mr. President, Mr, Director-General, distinguished delegates, ladies and gentlemen: On  behalf of the delegation of the Republic of Korea, I wish to join with previous speakers in express:  ing my sincere and heartfelt congratulations to you, Mr. President, on your election to the high  office of President of this historic nineteenth session of the General Conference of Unesco, Ihave  no doubt that the nineteenth session, under your able leadership, will mark three decades of Unesco  activities by proving to be the most productive General Conference ever experienced by our  Organization,
8.2 The delegation of the Republic of Korea also wishes to express its most sincere appreciation  to the Government and people of the Republic of Kenya for hosting this Conference and providing  such excellent facilities for the delegations from all parts of the world. I believe that the promise  of many fruitful results which we are anticipating at this session has already been enhanced by the  fact that we are holding the Conference in this beautiful city of Nairobi, the great capital of the  Republic of Kenya,

410      te that much of the enthusiasm for a renewal of vigorous activity i i i  De itwted to the energetic leadership of our new Pott, I  only served in the capacity of Director-General since the eighteenth session of the General Confer-  ence, he has amply demonstrated that a long association with Unesco activities in the past, plus a  vision of the future - and of Unesco's role in that future - can yield tremendous results These  results can be seen before us today - not only in the reorganization of Unesco's bureaucratie struc-  ture, but in the approach to Unesco's mission, as outlined by the Draft Medium-Term Plan,
8.4 The Draft Medium-Term Plan provided us with a good analysis of the world's major problems  and should serve as a blueprint to guide us in our efforts to overcome them, The common theme  that runs through all the "world problems'' delineated in document 19 C/4, is the emphasis placed  on development, and more specifically, on the fact that development must be "endogenous" - that  is, it must be characterized by "growth from within", This concern for endogenous development  is central to document 19 C/4, and reflects the widespread desire for the establishment of a new  international economic and social order, Emphasis must be placed on the construction of indigen-  ous scientific and technological infrastructures to enable developing nations to "develop" themselves  through the means best suited to their societies, The construction of such indigenous infrastructures  and methods ensures that development, when consistent with the socio-cultural values particular to  each country, will prove to be effective,
8.5 The Republic of Korea is perhaps particularly well qualified to speak on the issue of develop -  ment, having been fortunate enough to have enjoyed an astonishingly high growth rate throughout the  First and Second Development Decades, Furthermore, the importance of development, tailored  specifically to meet the most pressing needs of our society, has, indeed, been recognized. In  order to bring about balanced, multi-faceted development, the New Community Movement, which  began in 1970 as a voluntary grassroots socio-economic movement, continues to receive strong  support and assistance from the government, We strongly recommend - in the light of the crucial  role which development plays, and will continue to play, in efforts to establish a new economic  order - that Unesco render full assistance and support to this area,
8.6 The Draft Medium-Term Plan that is before this Conference should serve as a link between  the broad aims of Unesco, as expressed by the Constitution, and the projects and programmes  specified in the biennial Programme and Budget. The need for such a Medium-Term Plan has  long been felt and it is therefore with satisfaction that we regard document 19 C/4, The use of  such a plan guarantees that programmes and projects will be planned in terms of continuity and  objectives, Furthermore, the employment of a Medium -Term Plan will hopefully ensure that  projects under way or initiated under the biennial programmes will be subject to scrutiny and  evaluation in relation to Medium -Term Plan objectives, For this reason we recommend strongly  that the Director-General's proposal to prepare future Medium-Term Plans in advance of the  biennial Programme and Budget, should be adopted.
8.7 Turning briefly, Mr, President, to the Draft Programme and Budget for 1977-1978, docu-  ment 19 C/5, we wish to express our general agreement with it, The Medium-Term Plan and the  biennial Programme and Budget, should, as we have mentioned before, share common goals. We  believe that, in general, document 19 C/5 conforms to the basic guidelines and objectives estab-  lished in document 19 C/4. Perhaps some attention should be directed, however, towards the  number of small and rather isolated projects contained in document 19 C/5, Is a proliferation of  minor projects, and the attendant piecemeal approach to problem-solving which they entail, the  most effective way of achieving the objectives of the Medium-Term Plan? Might not more concen-  tration on major programmes, designed to deal directly with the "world problems" enumerated in  document 19 C/4, yield better results? With the exception of this minor reservation, we regard  document 19 C/5 as well thought out and planned.
8.8 Mr, President and distinguished delegates, we feel that high praise must go to the Director -  General for the reorganization of the Secretariat and the biennial Programme and Budget to fit  the Medium -Term Plan objectives. One of the fruits of this reorganization has been the creation  of a sector devoted to the social sciences. The elevation of the social sciences to an independent  sector is a step which should lead to rapid recognition of the crucial role the social sciences should  play in the activities of Unesco, Development problems occupy the central area of concern within  the Draft Medium -Term Plan and the Draft Programme and Budget for 1977-1978. It is therefore  natural that the strengthening of the social sciences should be seen primarily in the light of the  impact they can have upon the development process.
8.9 The application of the social sciences to development problems can be seen in three ways.  Firstly, the strengthening of the social sciences can be seen as redressing the long overdue bal-  ance between the natural and the social sciences, The function that the social sciences perform  in better integrating natural sciences with human goals, and in ensuring that the results of scien-  tific research are employed for the benefit of the human race, cannot afford to be overlooked.  Secondly, an emphasis on the social sciences will speed development by giving societies the means  of studying their problems from the inside - by developing endogenous means and infrastructures  to deal with those problems, Thirdly, strengthened social sciences will provide an impetus for  multi-faceted and balanced development that will deflate the danger posed by the one-sided develop-  ment of some sectors of society at the expense of others, The Government of the Republic of  Korea, acutely aware of the important role the social sciences should and can play, has always  striven to promote support for this vital area,
8.10 The establishment of the Co-operation for Development and External Relations Sector, like  the establishment of a sector for the social sciences, deserves our applause and support, This  new sector should ensure that Unesco's co-operation with Member States, with various interna.  tional non-governmental organizations and with other Specialized Agencies of the United Nations  is conducted on the basis of harmony and with full regard for distinctive characteristics at regional  or national levels,
8.11 Not least in importance is the emphasis this sector places on the need for strengthening the  National Commissions and for decentralization of Unesco's activities. Decentralization, designed  to give Member States a more active role in Unesco's work, does not mean the abrogation by the  Secretariat of responsibilities which are its alone nor a lessening of the Organization's world-wide  character, A decentralization policy should contribute to a heightened feeling of responsibility  towards Unesco on the part of Member States, as well as leading towards stronger and increasingly  active National Commissions. In the United Nations system, only Unesco possesses National  Commissions - bodies which offer unique opportunities for the strengthening of Unesco's activities,  Owing to their awareness of the potentials and the needs of their countries, as well as those of  Unesco, National Commissions find themselves in a position to carry out, for example, accurate  evaluations and assessments of Unesco programmes. The strengthening of the National Commissions  will ensure the growth of Unesco. Participation and involvement in the various phases of pro-  gramme formulation, execution and evaluation by Member States and their National Commissions  will ensure that Unesco's activities are truly global in their approach and scope. However, in  order to achieve such a global approach, not only must Unesco delegate more authority to the  National Commissions, but individual governments must grant an increased amount of respons-  ibility to their respective Commissions.
8.12 Mr. President, permit me to digress for a moment to illustrate, if I may, one way in which  National Commissions might become more involved with the activities of Unesco. The Korean  National Commission for Unesco has always attached the greatest importance to the issue of com-  munity development, In 1973 our Commission organized an International Seminar on the Compara-  tive Study of Community Development which participants from thirteen nations throughout the world  attended, The seminar participants agreed that community development was of vital importance  for the maintenance of international peace and harmony by helping to reduce the gap between  developed and developing countries. As a follow up to this seminar, the Korean National Com-  mission is supporting, through a funds-in-trust arrangement with Unesco Headquarters, an empiri-  cal research study of four model community development projects in four different countries:  India, Mexico, Tanzania and Korea. Most recently, a Training Seminar on Evaluation in Develop-  ment, attended by representatives from ten Asian nations, was held in Seoul, capital city of the  Republic of Korea, from 27 September to 9 October of this year, The seminar reaffirmed the  need for continued support for community development studies, as well as the need for constant  and effective evaluation of development processes,

In the natural science area, a Regional Laboratory Workshop on General Chemistry  Teaching in Universities (in South East Asia) and a Regional Meeting on Innovations in the Educa-  tion and Training of Engineers and Higher Technicians were held, The social science field has  seen the recent completion of the Training Seminar on Evaluation in Development. In June of this  year, the second session of the Executive Board of the Asian Federation of Unesco Clubs and  Associations (AFUCA) was held in Seoul. Domestically, the Korean National Commission for  Unesco has actively pursued a policy designed to disseminate the ideals of Unesco to the general  public and especially to the young, Local Unesco associations, at the provincial and city level,  have continued to contribute to public interest in Unesco's activities. The Unesco Youth Centre,  occupying 70 acres of land and providing sleeping accommodation for 400 young people, will be  opened some time early next year. The Centre will be used not only for youth activities in Korea,  but will also be made available for regional and international seminars, workshops, and confer-  ences, and for the training of youth leaders from all over the world,

12,2 Mr. President, we meet in the midst of an ever increasing crisis affecting world develop-  ment and peace, The crisis of development is one of poverty of the masses of the Third World,  whose most basic needs such as food, health, and habitat remain unmet. Such a crisis emanates  from the unbalanced economic relations of the developed and developing countries, or to put it  more plainly, that of the few affluent countries and the majority of the poor. We have all eagerly  awaited the taking of effective and concrete action by the world community, towards remedying the  existing economic disparities and inequalities, but despite numerous efforts made in this direction,  the situation remains unchanged, owing largely to the failure of a majority of the developed coun-  tries to respond to the call for co-operation in the concerted effort of the nations of the world to  create conditions conducive to development and peace. Indeed, the existing state of affairs indic-  ates that we are far from attaining our expressed goal of establishing a new international economic  and social order and creating conditions of peaceful well-being and a standard of living compatible  with the human dignity of all mankind. On the contrary, the gap between the developed and develop-  ing countries is ever widening and greater numbers of the world's population continue to live in  miserable conditions in which poverty, hunger and disease are rife, Such conditions can lead to  disharmony and instability, Unsatisfied needs and frustrated aspirations, if allowed to gain momen-  tum, can prove as great a threat to world peace and security as the continual amassing of arma-  ments, The time has come when all nations, be they developed or developing, must show greater  willingness to accept the cardinal principle of joint responsibility for world development and peace  and recognize the fact that all nations are economically interdependent, The privileged or tech-  nologically advanced nations cannot long remain immune and impassive to the plight of the less  advantaged and must take more effective steps to improve the economic position of the less fortun-  ate States by sacrificing narrow interests for what we regard to be the greater good of humanity. It  is only with the total commitment of all nations to this greater principle that strategies for develop-  ment and efforts towards world peace can be translated into meaningful realities, Here, Mr. Pre:  sident, is where we believe that organizations such as Unesco have played and should continue to :  play a vital role in drawing the attention of the world community to the manifold problems confront  ing us and offer solutions within their terms of reference,

I sincerely hope that fruitful results will be produced in this session under your eminent  and impartial leadership, I think it is also a matter for congratulation that this session of the  General Conference should be held in Africa for the first time in the history of Unesco, It seems  particularly timely for us to gather in Africa, the continent with such great potentialities for the  future, in order to discuss new prospects for the future of the Organization, In this connection,  I wish to express our deep appreciation for the arduous efforts made by the Government and people  of the Republic of Kenya, and the city of Nairobi, to hold this session of the General Conference  here. We are also glad to welcome the delegations of Grenada, Mozambique, Papua New Guinea,  Seychelles and Surinam, which have newly joined our Organization, At the same time I should like  to pay my respects to the Director-General, Mr, M'Bow, and express appreciation to him for all  the efforts he has made in preparation for this session under various difficult conditions,
16.2 Mr, President, this year marks a memorable stage in the life of Unesco, namely the thirtieth  anniversary of its founding, and it marks a significant anniversary for Japan, too, since it is now  twenty-five years since it was admitted to membership of Unesco, A quarter of a century ago,  Japan was war -devastated and under occupation, her people living very austere lives. It was in  such a state of destitution that Japan joined this Organization, with the sincere hope of having a  door open towards the international community and entrusting her aspirations for peace to Unesco.  During the twenty-five years that have since gone by, Japan has gone through a multitude of changes  in many respects, while the international situation has also undergone remarkable changes. Since  scores of nations have become independent one after another, the international community has ac-  quired a much greater diversity, Science and technology have registered remarkable advances un-  paralleled in any other period of history, and the expansion of international exchange in the fields  of economics, culture and political relations has resulted in growing interdependence among nations,  so that today no nation in the world can survive without some association with the international com -  munity, This international society of today is confronted with many difficult problems, such as  those relating to population, food, natural resources, energy and environment as well as the basic  problems of maintaining peace and protecting human rights. All of these call for joint efforts from  all the peoples of the world in order to solve them,
16.3 Mr, President, for many years Unesco has had a number of important responsibilities to  discharge, such as the furtherance of intellectual éxchange, co-operation for development among  its Member States and its general contribution towards the resolving of various global problems,  and it appears that its responsibilities are increasing still further, In view of the momentous na-  ture of the period on which Unesco is now embarking, I feel that there is a strong need to review  and rearrange the various basic principles underlying the Organization's activities. The Director,  General states, in his Introduction to the Draft Programme and Budget for 1977-1978, that "Unesco  has gone through many difficult periods since its foundation, but it is no exaggeration to say that  the difficulties it has had to face since the last session of the General Conference have been of un-  precedented gravity and that this is a crucial stage in Unesco's history . Sharing the view that  Unesco stands today at a critical point, I consider it very important that fresh thought be given to  the fundamental ideas governing the various Unesco operations.
16.4 Mr. President, allow me to refer to three viewpoints in this regard, The first is the conce ;  of international cultural exchange, the second is the concept of development, and the third ig the p  relationship between international cultural exchange and political questions. At the outset, I shoulq  like to make it clear that I use the term "culture here in its broad sense, and when I speak of  "international cultural interchange'' I refer to international exchanges in the fields of education,  science and culture,
16,5 I am afraid that the term "international cultural exchange'' has been used so far, not in the  literal sense of mutual intercourse, but rather to signify the one-way flow of culture from a limi-  ted number of nations to the majority of other nations, based on the idea that the cultural standapq  of many nations may be enhanced only through the introduction of cultures from a small number of  nations. I am doubtful of the propriety of such an idea, We should give their due importance tothe  cultural, religious, geographical and historical values indigenous to every country and people, Only  through such a pluralistic and comprehensive concept can the significance of the irreplaceable ang  independent dignity of each nation be perceived. It would then become possible to carry out interna.  tional cultural exchange on the basis of reciprocity, equality, independence as well as of solidarity  and co-operation, We may have to reconsider the over-simplified concept of international cultura]  interchange.
16,6 My second point, which is related to the concept of development, has a close bearing on the  first. The term '"development' has been used as a watchword for all of us during the last twenty  : years, Although the term lacks a precise definition, it seems that economic growth and technical  innovations have been identified as its central theme, while countries in the north are categorized  as developed countries, and those in the south as developing ones. Thus the term "development!  seemed to mean the process of catching up with the former by the latter. I should like to say a  few words on this matter in view of Japan's experiences. Japan has been engaged in the endeavour  to develop for over a hundred years, and a close study of this process highlights some points  worthy of attention, First, the development of Japan was helped to a great extent by the introduc-  tion of advanced knowledge and technologies from overseas. In other words, international cultural  exchange played a vital role in Japan's development. Secondly, however, Japan's development did  not take place just by copying overseas civilization, but was soundly based on her inherent cultural  traditions. The Japanese leaders who endeavoured to introduce and digest overseas civilization,  were imbued with a strong sense of respect for traditional values and for the principle of self-help,  What they always kept in mind was that development could not be attained overnight just be relying  on what was given by others, but that it could only be attained through the long process of work  performed by themselves. Mr. President, in view of such experiences as Japan has accumulated  during the last hundred years, I would like to put forward the following suggestions with respect  to the process of ''development'" which Unesco is called upon to deal with, The first is that the  earlier concept of development, in which priority was given to economic growth and technical in-  novations, should be so enlarged as to incorporate social and cultural aspects as well, The second  is that the idea of a universal model of development being displayed by a limited number of indus-  trialized nations should be replaced by the recognition of the need for each nation to formulate and  implement an individual development plan suited to its own cultural tradition. In other words, it  is necessary for us to recognize that the development of any nation is essentially of such an endo-  genous nature that it cannot be accomplished without the participation of the whole of its people and  their efforts for self-help. The third is that the principle should be confirmed that it is not  "assistance but ""co-operation' which each nation and international agency should offer in order  to enable such countries to continue their efforts of self-help for growth of their endogenous develop-  ment capabilities. From this viewpoint, the contents and methods of the "technical assistance!" that  has been afforded so far should be re-examined, and the more effective formula of "co-operation"  should be recognized and put into execution,
16.7 Mr. President, I should like now to refer to the third point, namely, the relationship between  international cultural exchange and political questions. So long as culture is taken to mean the acti-  vity of human beings, it is not only difficult but may be also impossible to isolate it from other acti-  vities such as economic, political or military affairs and the like, Culture, however, has its own  raison d'être. It should be independent of economic, political and military affairs in the world of  day-to-day realities. Accordingly, the agencies responsible for the development of culture in a  broad sense - of which Unesco is a representative example - should endeavour to keep a critical  eye on real interests involved in fields other than culture in order to prevent themselves from being  swayed by those interests. In this context, I cannot help but express my concern about the recent  trend of various political and economic conflicts and the arguments emanating therefrom making  themselves felt in certain meetings of Unesco. I sincerely trust that this session of the General  Conference will be so managed as to be worthy of the name of an agency designed exclusively for  the advancement and enhancement of culture, based on the principle of mutual understanding and  respect and on the spirit of mutual conciliation and co-operation, as called for in the Constitution  of Unesco.
16.8 Mr. President, may I express my views, in the light of three basic concepts I have just out-  lined, on the various objectives and programme actions set out in Unesco's Draft Medium-Term  Plan, which are before the present session for adoption for the first time, First of all, I fully sr  port the major emphasis which the Draft Medium-Term Plan places on the promotion of internation  exchange in the fields of education, science and culture, on the principle of understanding and res  pect for each nation's independence, and on international co-operation to encourage and assist the
424   self-help efforts made by each Member State to increase its endogenous development capabilities  I also support the programmes contained in the Draft Medium-Term Plan which aim at A ;  and expanding education based on the concept of lifelong education. The education of its people ei  foster the capacity of any nation for endogenous development, In this regard, lifelong a -  which is a concept embracing the whole process of human education both in and out of school - is  considered as a highly appropriate and pivotal concept that would enable each nation to provide its  people with the opportunity of undergoing education in the way best suited to its local conditions,
16.9 Joint research programmes on a world-wide scale dealing with oceanography, hydrology  "Man and the Biosphere'' and so forth, are regarded by my delegation as very appropriate types  of work to be undertaken by Unesco, since their results are closely related to the improvement of  the welfare of individual peoples. Furthermore, in view of the growing need in developing nations  for measures to reinforce the basic structures for the development of science and technology, and  also of communication, I wish to express support for Unesco's policy of attaching importance to  co-operative programmes in this field. ; This being so, I sincerely hope that special priority will  be given in the Medium-Term Plan to such programmes as those which may be expected to provide  effective guidelines for the formulation and implementation of future Unesco programmes and  budgets.
16.10 Mr. President, in the course of rearranging the conventional ideas and defining a general  framework for future programmes along these lines, we would regard it as important to define the  steps to be taken in practical terms on the basis of an objective evaluation of realities. While it  goes without saying that the Japanese Government is ready to participate and co-operate in all the  activities of Unesco, Japan, as one of the Asian countries, would particularly like to extend its  co-operation in joint programmes with other Asian countries, that is, programmes requiring parti-  cipation on an equal footing by all the nations concerned, such as the regional programme on educa-  tional innovation for development, the regional co-operation programme in basic sciences, the  cultural co-operation programme undertaken by the Asian Cultural Centre for Unesco, and so on,
16,11 At the same time, Mr. President, while taking an active part in programmes concerning  the Asian region, Japan also keenly realizes the necessity for serious consideration regarding the  attitude it should adopt towards matters affecting the whole of mankind, viewed in a broad perspec-  tive, In this connection, I would express the particular interest manifested by my delegation for  the United Nations University whose headquarters is located in Japan, I understand that this Uni-  versity is now in the stage of drawing up research programmes in three priority areas, namely,  "World Hunger'', "Human and Social Development and "Use and Management of Natural Resources",  all of which embrace urgent and important problems faced by mankind today. Some of these pro-  grammes have already been set in motion, I would like to request that Unesco endeavour to develop  a closer partnership with the United Nations University, to promote complementary relationships  in their respective functions and to make the fullest possible use of the results of studies carried  out by the University. Japan contributes her due share to the Endowment Fund of this University.  I express my appreciation, Mr. President, to those countries which have also made their respec-  tive contributions to the Fund and I should like at the same time to ask other countries to make a  similar appropriate response.
16.12 Mr, President, I feel that I must express myself with considerable severity with regard to  the drawing up and execution of Unesco's budget. The scale of Unesco's budget has to be deter-  mined by taking into account the financial situation of Member States and international economic  trends. Otherwise, the balance between the revenue and expenditure of the Organization will inevit-  ably be compromised, I would therefore strongly urge that, in drawing up the Budget for 1979-1980,  due consideration be given to the exercise of rigid restraints upon both proposed income and expen-  ditures, and that programmes of high priority be the object of particular concentration,
16.13 Mr, President, I have stated my basic views on Unesco's management as well as on the  Draft Medium -Term Plan and the Draft Programme and Budget. I have emphasized, in particular,  the importance of mutual respect for cultural independence. The delegations of one hundred and  forty Member States and two Associate Members representing divergent types of culture have  assembled here from various parts of the world, Observing the expressions of these delegates,  I cannot but be struck by the feeling that Unesco is really a kind of half-way house, between east  and west and between north and south, an intermediate point where varying types of culture meet  and their paths converge towards the lofty goal of mankind's fulfilment, or to that new world to  which we all aspire, At the same, time I am inclined to compare Japan itself to a kind of half-way  house as well, Japan, geographically situated at the point of contact between the oriental and  occidental cultures, has followed the path of modernization absorbing various types of culture and  yet maintaining a harmony with her own age-old historic culture. I earnestly hope that my country,  with her long experience and cultural characteristics will be enabled, through Unesco, to make her  own contribution to the security and prosperity of the international community, Thank you.

(22.3) I should also like to take this opportunity of thanking the Director-General of Unesco, Mr. Amadou-  Mahtar M'Bow, for kindly inviting representatives of the Secretariat of the Council for Mutual Economic  Assistance to attend the General Conference session as observers. The CMEA Secretariat regards this  invitation as an extension and development of contacts between thé Secretariat of CMEA and Unesco, and  expresses the hope that such contacts will be broadened and strengthened.
(22.4) Mr. President, ladies and gentlemen, in view of the close connection between certain aspects of  the activity of CMEA and that of Unesco in the field of scientific and technological co-operation, allow me  to deal briefly with certain aspects of this co-operation, which is carried out by the CMEA member  countries within the framework of our Organization. Scientific and technological co-operation occupies an  important place among the basic policies and forms of economic relations of the CMEA member countries.  It is an integral part of the overall co-operation of CMEA member countries and is aimed at helping them  to attain the highest possible level in key sectors of science and technology, to find effective solutions to  scientific and technological problems singled out for co-operation and to make prompt and extensive use  in industry of the findings of scientific research.
(22.5) In the organization and carrying out of scientific and technological co-operation between CMEA  member countries, particular importance is attached to mutual consultations on basic questions of scien-  tific and technological policy of common interest, and to co-ordination of the national economic plans of  CMEA member countries on scientific and technological problems selected for long-term co-operation.  On the basis of such consultations conducted during the 1973-1974 period within the framework of the  CMEA Committee for Scientific and Technological Co-operation, a policy document was prepared entitled  "Basic problem areas and most important problems in science and technology of mutual interest for co-  operation between CMEA member countries during the 1976-1980 period and in the longer term'', which  contains 13 problem areas, embracing 112 scientific and technological problems, including 17 fundamental  problems. The selection of problems has been made with a view to solving, through co-operation, import-  ant questions affecting the power industries of CMEA member countries, considerably raising labour pro-  ductivity in extractive industries, working out scientific bases for the effective utilization of material re-  sources, devising new means of protecting plants taking account of their impact on the environment, de-  vising highly productive automated metal-working equipment, and so forth.
(22.6) The "basic problem areas' document I have just referred to has also been used by CMEA member  countries and bodies in working out agreed plans for scientific and technological co-operation in the 1976-
1980 period, including a consolidated plan for scientific and technological co-operation between CMEA  member countries in the 1976-1980 period, covering over 260 problems made up of more than 850 topics.  The basic concern of scientific and technological co-operation between CMEA member countries is the  organization of joint development work on scientific and technological problems of mutual interest. Co-  operation in this field takes various forms the organizational, methodological and legal bases of which are  being steadily extended and improved.
(22.7) Under the comprehensive programme adopted at CMEA's 25th session in 1971 in Bucharest, the  joint efforts of CMEA member countries are primarily concerned with major fundamental and research-  oriented scientific problems, together with problems of an applied character. The utmost attention is  given to co-operation in the scientific and technological fields, determining the technological level and  efficiency of industry in particular countries, such as the creation of new and improvement of existing  means of generating and transmitting electricity, the development of new substances and materials, the  elaboration of new and highly effective technological processes, the mechanization and automation of pro-  duction, and so on.
(22.8) The results of this co-operation are becoming increasingly tangible. For instance, research in the  realm of solid-state physics has made a definite contribution to the theory of superconductivity. A number  of important findings have been made in the field of research on the properties of electrons in metals and,  in particular, a new quantum effect discovered, namely magnetic surface electron conditions occurring in  thin films of metals under the influence of a weak magnetic field. Great attention has been given to ques-  tions of cryotechnology.
(22.9) In the field of biological physics, a general.mathematical model of cell energy has been made, ex-  plaining some fundamental properties of the cell. Very important results have been obtained in research  on the structures of nucleic acids and proteins, etc. With regard to the selection and creation of high-  yielding varieties and hybrids of agricultural crops, research has been conducted to create new se  varieties of wheat and barely, as a result of which over 290 new varieties of agricultural crops have been  distributed and submitted for government testing in the co-operating countries.  research has been carried out on air-sea interaction in the  the findings of which are used in assessing the self-  nd improving hydro-

(22.12) With regard to the conservation of nature, over 500 items of work have been completed, cover;  such matters as new methods of cleaning industrial fumes of harmful exhaust substances, highly ete  ways of rehabilitating landscapes marred by industry, etc., many of which have been put into practice >  various branches of the national economies of CMEA member countries.
(22.13) The CMEA member countries are also co-operating on other matters of mutual interest under the  comprehensive programme, such as scientific and technical information, the training of scientific and  technical personnel, the provision for research purposes of apparatus, equipment and materials, and so  forth.
(22.14) As emphasized in the comprehensive programme, one feature of the further extension and improve  ment of co-operation and of the development of socialist economic integration is the fact that this process .  takes place as part of an objective process of a gradual growing together and evening out of the levels of  economic development of CMEA member countries. The main ways of achieving this objective are maxi-  mum mobilization and effective use of each country's own efforts and resources, together with utilization  of the advantages of the international socialist division of labour.
(22.15) Noting the successes achieved in mutual economic, scientific and technological co-operation be-  tween the CMEA member countries, the 30th session of the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, held  this year in Berlin, considered that the time was now ripe to take still further advantage of the opportun-  ities offered by the comprehensive programme for a joint solution to a number of vitally important econ-  omic problems of CMEA member countries. These purposes should be served by the preparation of long-  term target-oriented programmes of co-operation in the most important branches of production over a  period of 10 to 15 years, which will also include measures regarding the scientific and technical co-operation  of CMEA member countries in furtherance of the objectives laid down.
