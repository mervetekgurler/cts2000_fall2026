Tr [EN  NINTH MEETING  Tuesday, 13 June 1950, at 10.30 a.m.  Chairman:  Draft Resolution on Biennial Sessions of the  General Conference, introduced by the United  States of America [document 5C/16 (Rev.)].  [1] Mrs. BRUNAUER (United States of America)  gave a short explanation of the substance of the  United States revised resolution. She drew  attention to the growing work of the Specialized  Agencies and to the consequent burden placed on  the Secretariat, as well as the high costs involved  in annual meetings. The adoption of this amend-  ment might involve changing the terms of office  of members of the Executive Board.  [2] The DIRECTOR-GENERAL was not opposed  to biennial sessions, which meant that more time  and money would be available for programme  activities. He was aware that FAO and other  Specialized Agencies were taking steps to alter  their rules concerning annual conferences. How-  ever, there were not only legal problems to con-  sider, such as the implications of two-yearly meet-  ings for the structure of the Organization. The  draft resolution under discussion requested the  Director-General and the Executive Board to work  out an amendment to the Constitution without,  however, indicating the nature and extent of this  amendment. The proposal should have been pre-  ceded by a thorough study of its effects on the  functions of the Organization. The General Con-  ference was the only body representing Member  States. Under the Constitution, it was for the  General Conference to determine the Organization's  general policy and main lines of work, and to  advise the United Nations Organization on the  educational, scientific and cultural aspects of  questions of concern to the latter. To renounce  the principle of compulsory annual meetings  would be to increase the responsibility of those  bodies not representative of Member States. The  question of a new distribution of responsibility  would arise, as would the question of modifying  the structure of the Executive Board. It might  become necessary to envisage a Board composed  not of individuals, but of representatives of Gov-  ernments.  Following the tradition of the Institute for  Intellectual Co-operation, the Conference might

[3] Mr. de CLEMENTI (Italy) introduced a draft  amendment to the United States resolution [docu-  ment 5C/112].  He felt that annual meetings of representatives  of National Commissions, as suggested in the  Italian draft amendment, would result in a  valuable exchange of ideas, as the questions  discussed would be more specific in character  than those coming up at a General Conference.  Delegates would have consultative and advisory  powers, and their discussions would be more real-  istic.  |4] The CHAIRMAN felt that the proposal sub-  mitted by the Italian delegation was more in the  nature of an addition than an amendment to the  United States proposal. He suggested that the  Committee discuss the latter first, and the Italian  draft amendment afterwards. The fundamental  question of biennial conferences and their con-  sequences on the whole of Unesco’s policy would  have to be discussed.  [5] Mr. MURTINHO (Brazil) supported the  United States proposal, as this would result in  considerable time-saving for delegates. It was,  however, necessary to appreciate its effect on the  Organization as a whole, and the Committee  should carefully study the full implications. He  wished to propose an amendment to the United  States proposal, on the following lines:  THE GENERAL CONFERENCE  INvrres Member States to bring to the notice of the  Director-General before 15 September 1950 any sug-  gestions or proposals which would seem useful to  them with reference to the implications of having  meetings every two years, and the modifications which  would have to be made in this respect;  INsTRUCTS the Director-General to undertake a  detailed study of the question and to prepare any  necessary draft amendment to the Constitution in the  light of the suggestions he may receive from Member  States.  [6] Mr. MAHMASSANI (Lebanon), while approv-  aX          ing in principle the United States proposal, felt  that it raised a procedural question which  required the application of Article XIII of the  Constitution. Consequently, they could not legal-  ly discuss this amendment unless it were intro-  duced according to the special procedure of that  Article.  [7] Dr. WALKER (Australia) felt doubts about  discussing an amendment to the Constitution be-  fore the requisite six months notice had been  given to Member States. He was in favour of  reserving a decision on the second paragraph of  the United States proposal for the time being, as  he could not give a final commitment. However,  he saw distinct advantages both from the point  of view of the budget and of efficiency in holding  biennial meetings. Steps should be taken to make  the Executive Board more representative of gov-  ernments, as many fundamental policy decisions  were bound to be required. He recalled that the  Board, as at present constituted, was composed  of private individuals and not of the spokesmen  of the various governments. The Australian dele-  gation's attitude would therefore depend upon a  complete scheme of alterations in the present  structural composition.  With regard to the Italian proposal [document
5C/112], while he was not opposed to periodic  meetings of representatives of National Commis-  sions, such meetings could not be regarded as  substitutes for directives which could be issued  only by Member Governments. He would  approve the adoption of the United States pro-  posal as amended by the Brazilian delegation, but  would prefer to postpone consideration of para-  graph 2 of the resolution, as due notice had not  been given.  [87.5 Dr. BARON CASTRO (Salvador) in principle  supported the United States proposal which he  felt would be better, not only from the point of  view of Member States, but also from the point of  view of the Secretariat. He suggested, however,  that the implications of the various problems in-  volved be worked out and fully discussed at a  later date.  [9] Mr. JAUME (France) regretted that a Spe-  cialized Agency such as Unesco should make  decisions based on financial considerations.  Nevertheless, he appreciated the argument put  forward by the United States delegation, and  regarded the draft resolution with complete  objectivity. The Committee had heard the case  both for and against it, and he merely wished to  record the potential danger of weakening the link  between Unesco and the National Commissions.  There were obvious difficulties in stating precise-  ly the new role of the Executive Board, and he

The United States proposal involved procedure  under Article XIII of the Constitution, and this  seemed to him to be the decisive point. It seemed  more logical that an exhaustive study be made  first on the whole matter, and that a decision be  taken on the basis of that study.  [10] The DIRECTOR-GENERAL explained that  the underlying idea expressed in the letter he had  received from the President of the United States  delegation did not call for a decision at the pre-  sent time, but only agreement on the draft  amendment which would be circulated to Mem-  ber States for their comments and suggestions.  As suggested in the amendment proposed by the  Brazilian delegation, these comments should be  submitted to the Secretariat before 15 September.  [11] Mr. de VISSCHER (Belgium) agreed with  Mr. Jaume’s remarks, although it seemed to him  that budgetary considerations were most impor-  tant. He was of the opinion that biennial meet-  ings would give small States an opportunity to be  put on a par with big States.  The composition of the Executive Board would  require alteration. There could either be two  committees—governmental and intellectual— or a  joint committee composed of intellectuals and  State representatives. He feared that the Executive  Board and the Committee of Intellectuals might  have overlapping powers. He therefore proposed  amending paragraph 2 of the United States pro-  posal to read:  Takes nole of the proposal with reference to having  biennial meetings . . .  followed by the text proposed by the Brazilian  delegation.  He hoped that the procedure involved would  mean that the Director-General would send a  questionnaire to Member States.  [12] Dr. BLANCO Y SANCHEZ (Cuba) was in  agreement with the Director-General and Mr. de  Visscher. It seemed to him that, for the time  being, the United States proposal called only for  agreement in principle.  [13] The DIRECTOR-GENERAL explained that  the text of paragraph 2, as proposed by the United  States delegation, did not commit governments.  It had been the Secretariat's idea to draw up a  questionnaire, requesting an answer before 15  September, and this was in conformity with the  suggestion made by the Brazilian delegation.  At present, the Executive Board was composed  of representatives of governments, who acted as  individuals and reviewed problems impartially.                                             There were two different kinds of patriotism—the  national and the international—which Unesco was  endeavouring to develop. Members of the Ex-  ecutive Board, many of whom were civil servants,  were torn between these two patriotisms, these  two duties. They faced this dual duty with a rare  courage to which he himself paid tribute, but  their position was a very delicate one. The dif-  ficulty would only increase if the Board had wider  responsibilities. On the other hand, there was  an urgent need for moral authority, and it was  this which underlay his own suggestion for the  constitution of a committee of intellectuals. It  was his responsibility to work out a draft pro-  gramme, but he must be advised by great minds.  The General Conference would be there to impose  restrictions on too bold a policy.  [14] Dr. KADHIM (Iraq) favoured the adoption  of the United States resolution, together with the  Brazilian amendment which he felt would have  far-reaching implications.  [15] Professor KUSEJ (Yugoslavia) considered  that the present Conference was not competent to  take a decision on the draft resolution until after  its implications had been fully studied. It seemed  to him that the resolution would increase the  powers of the Executive Board. He had felt that  annual meetings were very necessary in view of  rapidly changing conditions in the world today.  Professor KuSej was not opposed on principle to  the United States resolution, but wanted the Com-  mittee to recommend undertaking a study of all  the problems involved in its adoption.
