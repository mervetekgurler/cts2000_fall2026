1. The PRESIDENT:  give the floor to our first speaker this afternoon, Mr Thomas Hammarberg, Observer  for Amnesty International.
2.1 Mr HAMMARBERG (Amnesty International):  Conference on behalf of Amnesty International. My intervention will deal with  Unesco's work in the area of human rights; not the philosophical and theoretical  aspects but the very concrete problem: what can be done against the widespread  occurrence of human rights violations throughout the world of today?
2.2 The message will be that Unesco has an absolutely crucial role to play in  this area. Of course, this is in line with its own Constitution, which underlines  the importance of the protection and promotion of human rights. More important,  however, is that there exists a vacuum in this field which only Unesco can fill--  in co-operation with governments and other organizations.
2.3 Take, for instance, the example of torture. Torture is a most serious  problem even today. It is global; Amnesty International has received during the  past two or three years well-substantiated reports of torture and cruel, inhuman  and degrading treatment of prisoners from more than sixty countries. These re-  ports have come from various geographical areas and countries with different  political systems.
2.4 Mr President, of course torture is illegal. There are clear prohibitions  against torture in international law. The International Covenant on Civil and  Political Rights--which is in force and ratified by more than half of the countries-  rules that the right not to be tortured is an absblute right, a right which can  never be derogated from--not even in times of national crises or war. Still,  torture is practised in more than one-third of the countries of the world today.  One almost gets the impression that the interrogations at the police centres and  the discussions in the United Nations and Unesco take place on two different  planets. And herein lies the challenge to Unesco.
2.5 The agreed standards are not implemented, they are not even made known  within individual countries. Many of the prisoners who are dragged into inter-  rogation centres do not themselves know what rights they have or should have.  Their families do not know either--and they often are at a loss on how to complain.  Many of the policemen, soldiers and officers who are in charge of detention and  interrogation are not informed on what they are allowed to do and what methods they  cannot use. For instance, they are usually not given the International Code of  Conduct for Law Enforcement Personnel. Many of them do not know that they have a  right--and indeed the duty--to refuse orders to torture. Many of the doctors  involved in these situations seem to be unaware of the ethical code for medical  personnel which, of course, forbids any involvement of health personnel in torture.  And, more generally, the public at large in not a few countries are, in practice,  kept in the dark about these internationally recognized principles and rights.  This last point is significant; the public opinion of a country can be decisive  in the defence of human rights. In fact, constructive interaction between the  authorities on the one side and non-governmental organizations and individuals  on the other has, in our experience, been one of the best guarantees for a con-  tinuous protection of human rights. That interaction is, o€ course, undermined if  the basic information is withheld. In that sense, the right to know one's own  rights is fundamental to other rights.
2.6 Mr President, in the situation 1 have described, what should be done?
2.7 Firstly, Unesco ought to launch a dynamic programme of dissemination of  the basic international standards for human rights, including information about  complaints machineries. The texts of the basic agreements should be made avail-  able in all major languages. The same should go for explanatory booklets like  the excellent little handbook published in Unesco's name: Human Rights: Questions  and Answers, which has still not been published in more than a handful of langua-  ges--and not, for instance, in Arabic, Russian, Chinese or Portuguese. More such  publications should be issued. There seems also to be a need for closer co-  ordination with the United Nations Secretariat--and its Department of Public Infor-  mation--to continue to avoid duplication and, above all, to concentrate the limited  resources on the most urgent needs. 2.8 Secondly there is a need to build regional human rights documentation  centres which could service governments, organizations, researchers, schools and  other educational institutions. Such centres could bridge the distance between  Paris and the real world in more than one sense. Such a network for human rights  education and information material would of course be greatly helped if the pro-  posed international clearing house for such material was established.
2.9 Thirdly, a system of reporting on the progress of human rights education  in Unesco's Member States should be developed. Unesco can do much in this area  but in the end the Organization depends on support from governments and the non-  governmental organizations. The latter are reporting their activities, for in-  stance, via the joint Unesco/NGO Working Group on Human Rights Education. What is  needed now is more systematic reporting by governments on what they actually have  done to disseminate international standards, to incorporate human rights in school  curricula, to educate law enforcement personnel, etc. which reports should of course  by analysed and made publicly available.
2.10 Fourthly, there is a need to support individuals and organizations that are  repressed because of their attempts to teach human rights. Teachers, journalists,  trade unionists and others have been imprisoned, and in some cases even killed,  on these grounds. And that certainly underlines the importance of Unesco's  complaints machinery, a machinery which needs to be further strengthened to deal  effectively with complaints from individuals. Let me, at this point, take the  opportunity of thanking the Director-General for the personal interest he has  shown in this aspect of Unesco's work.
2.11 Mr President, some of the suggestions I have mentioned are already part  of Unesco's programme, as elements in the seven-year Plan for the Development of  Human Rights Teaching which was adopted by the General Conference in 1980 in  Belgrade. But a plan will have little impact if it is not backed up with suffi-  cient resources, and the budget proposals which are before the present Conference  in this regard seem to us conservative, even if indirect contributions are taken  into account. Moreover, the implementation of the 1980 plan also seems to be  delayed. For instance, it is now suggested that the meeting for a review of the  progress in implementing the plan be postponed until 1986, leaving only one year  before the seven-year period is over. There may be good reasons for such a post-  ponement; meetings are not necessarily the answer to urgent needs. What is  important, however, is that this area is given the priority it deserves. That  will not be very costly; Unesco's role is, of course, to catalyse, to stimulate and  assist in human rights education rather than to take over the teaching itself.  A good programme in this field can therefore be cost-effective, particularly if  it is focused more on the active dissemination of basic information, rather than on  reflections and studies. Thank you, Mr President.
