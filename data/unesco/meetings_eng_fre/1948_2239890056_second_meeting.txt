Mr. HOLCROFT (New Zealand) regretted that  the Organization's activities in the domain of  education should be hindered by lack of staff.  He thought that a suggestion should be made to  the Programme and Budget Commission that the  funds allocated to education should be increased  at the expense of other less realistic undertakings.  Professor KABIR (India) did not agree; it  seemed to him difficult to establish degrees of  importance for the various sections of Unesco.  He wished to draw the Sub-Commission's atten-  tion to the two following matters : (1) Unesco's  programme should be sufficiently elastic to be  adaptable to the human and social problems  arising from education in each country; (2) the  financial and social position of teachers was  known to be inadequate and Unesco should launch  a campaign for its improvement.  The CHAIRMAN assured the representative of  India that the Sub-Commission and the Secretariat  would bear in mind his remarks on the first  point. The second point was closely connected  with the draft Teachers’ Charter which was  included on the agenda.  Mr. THERON (Union of South Africa) agreed  with Dr. Stoddard and Mr. Gould in thinking that  Unesco should work not only in close co-operation  with Member States and national organizations,  but should also suggest to the latter that they  should make experiments and organize research  having a bearing on their own needs, thus light-  ening the task of the Secretariat.  Mr. LITTLE (Australia) was glad to note that  Unesco’s programme as regarded education was  limited to well-defined activities; he hoped,  however, that the programme would stress to a  greater extent the essential purposes of the Organi-  zation. It was necessary in each country to gain  the co-operation of the Administration, the teach-  ing profession and the public; in fact, however,  the effect of a multiplicity of projects was to  disquiet the Administration and public and to
310  alienate the teaching profession. It should not  be forgotten, moreover, that scientific discoveries  which concerned education could only be assimi-  lated slowly. He thought that it  was not sufficient to ask “the opinion of respon-  sible officials” when missions were sent by Unesco  to various countries; enquiries should also be  made concerning the legislative and financial  conditions which influenced the existing situation,  and the opinion of educational organizations and  members of the teaching profession should also  be taken into account.  During the ensuing discussion, Dr. SALIBA  (Syria) suggested the addition to the activities  provided for in the document under consideration  of an enquiry concerning each country's con-  ception of the aims of education.  Mr. LITTLE (Australia) proposed the deletion  of paragraph 4 (c), as it seemed to him that  requests from governments should be spontaneous  and not solicited.  There was no objection to Mr. Little's proposal. M. ABRAHAM (France) thought it very regret-  table that the Secretariat should arrest the deve-  lopment of the seminars. In his view, they were  not only meetings for work on specific points,
311  but were especially valuable for the personal link  they established between educationists in different  countries. During the seminars, those educa-  tionists not only became aware of the differences  in their methods of work, but learned to know  and thus understand each other. Exchanges of  documents were valuable, but human exchanges  were still more valuable, and nothing could take  their place.  M. NOVRUP (Denmark) seconded the French  delegation’s resolution.  Dr. TUDE-DE-SOUZA (Brazil) cited his own  experience of the Sèvres Seminar and likewise  supported the resolution.  Mr. GOULD (United Kingdom) thought the  French proposal premature. The results of the  current years seminars had not yet been  published. Several countries had not been able  to attend, twenty-two being represented at Ash-  ridge, twenty-one at Lake Success, and fifteen at  Podebrady. That was due, no doubt, to over-  hurried preparation. Although he was sure that  the seminars had done useful work, Mr. Gould  was of the opinion that international gatherings  of that kind needed much preparation. On that  account, he proposed the following amend-  ment :  That the proposed seminar tentatively arranged  for 1949 be postponed to 1950;  That the Executive Board prepare a report for the  Fourth Session of the General Conference of Unesco,  stating the Board’s views on the proper pattern and  purpose of international seminars arranged by Unesco.  Mr. LITTLE (Australia) agreed with Mr. Gould.  So far as Australia, a distant country, was con-  cerned, the representatives had been chosen too  quickly and had arrived at the seminar ill-  prepared.
