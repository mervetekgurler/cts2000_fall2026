2.2 To conclude, I would like to inform the General Conference that I have  received from the head of the delegation of the Socialist Republic of Vietnam a  letter which reads as follows:  Dear Sir,  In letter no. 196 BTK/CG85 of 8 October 1985, the Delegation of the  Socialist Republic of Vietnam informed you of its reservation regarding  the presence of the so-called 'Delegation of Democratic Kampuchea' at  the twenty-third session of the General Conference of Unesco.  In explanation of the reasons for this reservation, I should like  to outline briefly some of the essential facts concerning the true situ-  ation of Kampuchea over the last ten years:
1. After the liberation of Kampuchea from American aggression in  April 1975, 'Democratic Kampuchea' was not more than a dicta-  torial militaristic regime, of a ferocity unparalleled in his-  tory, which during the three years, eight months and 20 days  of its existence made genocide a form of government, condemn-  ing three million human beings to death and pillaging the land  of Angkor, in addition to massacring thousands of Vietnamese  citizens and creating innumerable Lidices when trespassing on  Vietnamese territory.  Permit me to quote a passage from the Declaration of the  National Union Front for the Safety of Kampuchea, dated 2nd  December 1978: Claiming to be carrying out a 'radical, social  revolution at all levels', Pol Pot and Ieng Sary have wiped  out towns and forced millions of town dwellers to abandon  their houses and possessions to live in destitution in the  country, condemning them to forced labour and physical depri-  vation. They have destroyed all the sacred ties of feeling,  whether between fathers and sons, husbands and wives, brothers  and sisters, or between relatives and neighbours. They have  eliminated the villages, which have been the focus of our  peoples' loyalties and way of life for thousands of years.  They preached 'collectivization', 'communal meals and hous-  ing', the abolition of money and markets ... They have  trampled underfoot all the traditions, morality and customs of  our people, laid waste our age-old culture. They prohibit  freedom of conscience, organize compulsory collective  marriages, break up families, and sap our moral fibre. They  have desroyed the pagodas and temples of Buddhism, which has  long been the State religion of Kampuchea, and compelled the  monks to return to secular life. They have even done away  with Hinduism, and in so doing exterminated the Cham people.  They have abolished nearly all higher educational establish-  ments. They have forced all 13 and 14 year old children to
12  give up their studies in order to join the army and serve  their selfish interests...'
2. Under the guidance of the National Union Front for the Safety  of Kampuchea, supported by the Vietnamese volunteer forces  that the Front appealed to for support, popular uprisings  succeeded in overthrowing Pol Pot's genocidal regime and  founding the People's Republic of Kampuchea on 7 January 1979.  When they emerged from this gruesome apocalypse, the
4.5 million Kampucheans who survived were little more than  walking skeletons, condemned by famine and disease soon to  die, in a country whose social fabric had been torn apart.  The burning problem facing the Government of the People's  Republic of Kampuchea, sole authentic representative of the  Kampuchean people, was to put an immediate end to famine and  galloping disease and to restore economic, social and cultural  activities. Since then, over six years have elapsed and the  People's Republic of Kampuchea has made considerable progress  towards effecting a miraculous recovery. Returning from the  land of.the dead, the Kampuchean people is now busily engaged  in building a new social structure with a new State, insti-  tutions and economic system and a medical service, culture and  education designed to serve the people.  By some freak of nature, the 'Democratic Kampuchea'  monster although slain, managed to survive in ghostly form  after its actual life had come to an end. But it makes no  difference whether it signs the Universal Declaration of Human  Rights or sports a label 'Coalition government of Democratic  Kampuchea', such gestures will never cancel out its horrendous  crimes or remove its stench from the nostrils of world public  opinion.
3. The major successes achieved by the People's Republic of  Kampuchea in the struggle for the defence and reconstruction  of the nation, in particular the crushing victories gained by  the Kampuchean people since the beginning of this year, show  that the situation is irreversible. In the light of the pro-  gress made by the Kampuchean people in all fields during the  last six years, the People's Republic of Kampuchea and the  Socialist Republic of Viet Nam have decided that the volunteer  forces of Viet Nam will continue their progressive annual  withdrawals from Kampuchea until complete withdrawal is  achieved in 1990. Should these withdrawals be exploited to  undermine the peace and security of Kampuchea, the Governments  of the People's Republic of Kampuchea and the Socialist  Republic of Viet Nam will hold consultations with a view to  taking appropriate measures.  For its part, the People's Republic of Kampuchea has  stated that it is ready to enter into talks with the various  Khmer opposition groups or individuals in order to discuss the  implementation of national reconciliation on the basis of the  elimination of the Pol Pot clique, which is guilty of geno-  cide, and the holding of general elections following the com-  plete withdrawal of the Vietnamese volunteer forces from  Kampuchea.  As I stated in my address to the second plenary meeting,  I wish to request you to reproduce the contents of this letter  verbatim in the records of the second plenary meeting and in  the report of the twenty-third session of the General Confer-  ence,  Yours faithfully  (signed) CU HUY CAN  Head of the Delegation of the Socialist  Republic of Viet Nam

3. The Chairman, followed by the representative of the Director-General intro-  duced item 5.1 of the agenda and the related documents (23 CI24 and 23 Cl24  Add.). The General Conference had, at its twenty-second session, in resol-  ution 27, requested the Executive Board to study a proposal from Australia and  New Zealand which involved the amendment of Article V, paragraph 1, of the Con-  stitution. Its purpose was to raise the number of members of the Executive Board  from 51 to 52 and the extra seat thus created would have been attributed to  electoral group IV. Australia and New Zealand also wished to be transferred from  electoral group I to electoral group IV.  In its study the Executive Board had analysed the current situation, par-  ticularly in the light of Recommendation B.4 of the Temporary Committee, which  the Executive Board had endorsed and which recommended that 'the number of  members of the Executive Board should not be increased for the next ten years'.  In this context, the Permanent Delegates of Australia and New Zealand had  informed the Executive Board at its 121st session that they were prepared to  withdraw their proposed amendment to the Constitution. The Executive Board had  then adopted 121 EXIDecision 5.6.1, in which it recommended to the General Con-  ference that it:  modify the allocation of seats for the purposes of election to the  Executive Board so as to reduce the number of seats in electoral  group I from ten to nine and to increase the number of seats in elec-  toral group IV from eight to nine;  comply with the requests of Australia and New Zealand that these two  States shall belong to electoral group IV instead of electoral group I;  decide to implement the decision set out under paragraphs (a) and (b)  above at its twenty-fourth session.  delegate of Australia told the Committee that he considered the Exec- -  utive Board's recommendation clearer, simpler and fairer than the proposal sub-  mitted to the twenty-second session of the General Conference. At that time, how-  ever, no one had any idea that certain Member States of group I would be with-  drawing from Unesco. Conversely, several States in the South Pacific, already  members of the United Nations, were soon to join group IV in Unesco. Furthermore,  in all the United Nations Specialized Agencies, Australia and New Zealand  belonged to the group that corresponded to their geographical location. Even in  Unesco they were placed in Asia and the Pacific in 1974 when it came to regional
439    activities. At the time, they had been left in electoral group I because they  were the only independent States in the South Pacific. Since then several other  States of that region had become members of Unesco and had been placed in elec-  toral group IV. Since the Temporary Committee had recently recommended that the  number of seats on the Executive Board should remain unchanged for ten years,  Australia and New Zealand had withdrawn their proposed amendment to the Consti-  tution. They were grateful to the Executive Board for its recommendation to the  General Conference that one seat be transferred from group I to group IV and that  Australia and New Zealand should also be transferred from group I to group IV, it  being understood that, in order to obtain a consensus, it had been agreed not to  apply those measures until the twenty-fourth session of the General Conference.  This new distribution of seats among electoral groups was much fairer and should  serve for several years, even if it was to be expected that more States from the  South Pacific and Africa would become members of Unesco in the near future. In  conclusion, the delegate. of Australia hoped that the Legal Committee would  endorse the recommendation of the Executive Board.
5. One member of the Committee said that the reasons given by Australia and New  Zealand to justify their transfer seemed to him perfectly acceptable. Since the  proposal to increase the number of seats on the Executive Board had been with-  drawn by its sponsors and the transfer of both countries could be carried without  any change in the Constitution, the attention of the General Conference should be  drawn to the fact that it could decide by a simple majority. The Legal Committee  should, however, recommend to the General Conference that it consider a redistri-  bution of seats, in view of the fact that, given the number of members in elec-  toral group III, that group was the least well represented on the Executive  Board. In support of this request, which was seconded by another member of the  Committee, he cited the representation rate of group III on the Executive Board  in 1980 and in 1983 and showed that not only was it the lowest of any group, but  that it had deteriorated considerably.
6. One member expressed his appreciation of Australia's and New Zealand's wish  to join their own region, and warmly supported their request. He congratulated  group I for having made a solution possible, and said that he supported the  recommendation of the Executive Board in that connection.
7. One member said that his country had had doubts about the proposal submitted  by Australia and New Zealand at the twenty-second session of the General Confer-  ence. These doubts were due to the fact that the proposal submitted by the two  countries raised serious difficulties and could lead to the destabilization and  disruption of the delicate balance in the distribution of seats on the Executive  Board. However, since the idea of increasing the number of seats on the Executive  Board had been withdrawn and a consensus had been reached, the proposal could be  supported. On the other hand, the new distribution of seats might give rise to  political problems in the future. If, for instance, the membership of group I  continued to decline and that of other groups to increase, a new imbalance in the  distribution of seats would emerge. It had to be borne in mind that the measures  set out in the Executive Board's recommendation were not due to be applied for  two years, and that in two years' time the situation might be quite different and  the measures that had been proposed might prove obsolete. It also had to be borne  in mind that it would be wrong to adopt a purely arithmetical approach to the  question of the distribution of seats on the Executive Board, for it was a  political question. Each group should have the appropriate representation on the  Executive Board.
