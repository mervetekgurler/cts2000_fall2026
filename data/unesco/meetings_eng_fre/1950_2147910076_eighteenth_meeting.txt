[29] Dr. LIU (Head, Statistical Service) recalled  that Unesco’s statistical responsibilities had been  specified in Article VIII of the Constitution. The  Statistical Commission of the United Nations had  approached Unesco about education statistics in  preparing the Statistical Yearbook; but, as Unesco  had not been able to collect the information  required at that date, the Yearbook did not  include a chapter on education.  [30] The call for education statistics, however,  was not confined to the United Nations and  outside bodies. Unesco itself needed the informa-  tion, and there were also special questions on  which statistics could throw considerable light,  such as educational opportunities for women in  the various countries.  The Director-General proposed.
(1) To collect and compile statistics;
(2) To publish statistics in conjunction with  plans worked out with the United Nations;
(3) To improve international comparability of  statistics (in 1951, the most elementary statistical  series such as numbers of schools, teachers and  students, would be collected).  [31] The CHAIRMAN having asked why obliga-           tions for consultants were estimated at $1,000 in
1950, but at only $700 for 1951, Dr. LIU (Head,  Statistical Service) explained that the sum of  $1,000 had been used for a cost of living survey,  while the figure of $700 represented the first obli-  gations for consultant experts called in by the  Statistical Service.  [32] Miss LAISE (United States of America) noted  that the Statistical Service scheduled new appoint-  ments throughout the year, and questioned  whether a 25 per cent lapse rate was not too great.  [33] Mr. WEITZ (Head, Bureau of Adminis-  trative Management and Budget) replied that the  Director-General avoided making appointments  to the Service in order to leave Dr. Liu free in the  matter of choice of staff.  [34] Mr. WEEDEN (Australia) wondered whether  the fee system would not be more suitable for the  payment of consultants during the first year.  [35] Dr. LIU (Head, Statistical Service), replied  that many problems were too intricate and too  closely bound up with other problems peculiar to  Unesco for the fee system to be used. As the  results of the questionnaire on school statistics  were coming in, sufficient data would be available  in 1951 to justify the employment of consultants. [43] Miss LAISE (United States of America)  thought that six counsellors were too many, unless  specific requests had been received.
