Professor SEVERI (Italy) was satisfied by the  declaration of the Chairman of the Executive  Board and would not press his proposal for a  change of wording.  [60] Mr. MONTEL (France) agreed with the  Chairman of the Executive Board, but suggested  the substitution of the word “mathematical” for  “computation”, as it was not a question of a con-  ference on applied mathematics.  Professor SEVERI (Italy) had no objection to  the deletion of the word “computation”, as he  considered that “mathematical” covered both pure  and applied mathematics.  Professor AUGER (Director, Natural Sciences  Department) pointed out that resolution 2.21  referred to work which had been initiated by the  Economic and Social Council and which was  being carried out by Unesco in collaboration with  the United Nations. The word “computation”  should be retained, as it appeared in all United  Nations documents.  [66] In that case, Mr. MONTEL (France)  wondered whether it was necessary to await the  decisions of the Cambridge Congress, since the  latter would be concerned exclusively with pure  mathematics.  Professor AUGER (Director, Natural Sciences  Department) explained that the International  Mathematical Union was to be formed in October
1950, while resolution 2.21 provided for the con-  tinuation of the study of the project in 1951; the  Union set up in 1950 would of course be consulted  in 1951, when the study of the project was con-  tinued.  [67] Mr. CUMMINS (Australia) suggested that  the word “international” raised difficulties with  regard to the expense and the means of financing  the centre. As more than one centre would  ultimately be required, he considered that the  countries benefiting directly from the provision  of the centre should contribute towards its up-  keep. He moved:  That the Director-General is authorized to investigate  the possibility of obtaining the collaboration of Euro-  pean Member States and of appropriate national and  international organizations in the establishment of a  regional computation centre in Europe.  In that way, the creation of the centre would  carry a minimum of budgetary implications.  The DIRECTOR-GENERAL suggested that the  representative of the United Nations should be  asked to state his Organization's views on a  matter in which Unesco had always worked in  close collaboration with the United Nations.  [68] Mr. DURAN (United Nations) announced  that the Committee of Experts set up by the  Economic and Social Council had prepared a  report on the subject, which would be considered  at the Council's next session. The report con-  tained a recommendation for the establishment of  a Computation Centre, a Brain Centre and a Social  Sciences Institute; it gave second priority to four  other laboratories, on the strength of reports sub-  mitted to it by Unesco.  The details of the financing of the institutes  and laboratories had not yet been settled, as the  Economic and Social Council intended to con-  vene, in 1951, a conference on the basic sciences  at which those questions would be considered.  Referring to the United States draft amendment  in document 5C/64, Mr. Durdn emphasized that  that Committee had informed the Economic and  Social Council that it considered questions relating  to the Computation Centre and to a certain num-  ber of other laboratories quite obviously to be with-  in Unesco’s competence and that, consequently, it  was for that Organization to take the main re-  sponsibility for the continuation of the work.  Mr. Durän therefore feared that the Economic  and Social Council might be somewhat discon-  certed if Unesco’s General Conference decided to  refer the matter back to it.  [69] Professor KRUYT (Netherlands) agreed in  part with Mr. Cummins and in part with Dr. Rabi.  He proposed that Mr. Cummins’s text should  make specific mention of a centre for Western  Europe, and proposed the addition of the words                                       “and to appoint a group of experts to advise the  Economic and Social Council at its next meeting”.  [70] Professor NIELSEN (Denmark) thought  that the proposed name of the centre in English  was misleading. Routine calculation was only an  accessory part of its work, which was to consist  of applying mathematics to other sciences  throughout the world. He suggested that the  centre be called an “International Centre for the  Application of Mathematics”. Without the word  “application” its scope would be too wide.  With regard to the Italian proposal [document
5C/34], Professor Nielsen agreed that the confer-  ence envisaged should not be held before the  establishment of the New International Mathe-  matical Union. He felt that Unesco should not  hand over the initiative in the matter of the centre  either to the Economic and Social Council or to  the new Union.  [71] Professor von MURALT (Switzerland) felt  some uneasiness about the projects for scientific  institutes of Unesco, which he had not experienced  with regard to the proposal made by Dr. Rabi,  because that project was coupled with a man’s  name. He felt that the first step in setting up  an institute was to find a scientist to take the  lead; otherwise probably nothing would be done.  He therefore recommended that the Director-  General should be asked, as an initial step in the  execution of these projects, to seek the advice of  scientists with a view to finding suitable persons  to direct the institutes.  [72] ANAS Khan (Afghanistan) did not think  that Unesco could be considered merely as a  scientific institute, as its principal aim was not  only to advance science, but to diffuse scientific  knowledge to countries in need of assistance. He  thought that the setting up of a computation  centre in India would enable contact to be  established with scientists far away from any  centre of civilization. He hoped that Unesco  would try to discover what its real duties were,  and would not be satisfied with carrying out the  programme of a scientific institute.  [73] Dr. MAJUMDAR (India), supported by  Dr. RABI (United States of America), thought  that the Netherlands and Australian amendments  were already covered by the United States pro-  posal. If, however, it was proposed to set up a  centre subsidized by Unesco in a specific geo-  graphical area, he would oppose it because it  would hamper Unesco’s freedom of decision.  [74] Professor SEVERI (Italy) observed that the  International Computation Centre would only  survive if it were set up in a place where there  Was a mathematical tradition. The idea of an
367  international centre should be retained: he did  not believe that regional centres would produce  satisfactory results.  In view of the stage reached in mathematical  sciences, it should be possible to find not one,  but several, persons capable of directing such an  institution; it had to be remembered that similar  centres existed, and were already run on an inter-  national basis, in England, France and Italy.  In conclusion, Professor Severi strongly recom-  mended the adoption of item 2.21.  [75] The CHAIRMAN ruled that item 2.21 should  be taken with the Australian draft resolution.  [76] Professor CARNEIRO (Brazil) raised a  point of order, requesting that item 2.21 (provid-  ing for the setting up, in collaboration with the  United Nations, of an international computation  centre) should be dissociated from the United  States proposal (for the creation of a regional  centre). The two projects were entirely separate  and had nothing in common.  Mr. CUMMINS (Australia) stated that he con-  sidered his draft resolution as an amendment to  item 2.21.
