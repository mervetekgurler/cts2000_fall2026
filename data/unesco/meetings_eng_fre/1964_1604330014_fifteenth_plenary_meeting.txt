In other words, having  determined the budget ceiling, let us now devote our efforts to using the money to further  the great interests which have brought us here in the name of the United Nations Educational,  Scientific and Cultural Organization.

16.11 Now regarding the priority which Unesco is currently according to education, 1 was  struck by the statement in the Director -General's remarks to us, that "to attempt to give  priority to everything is to give priority to nothing". In a programme as broad as that of  Unesco, not all aspects can be said to be of equal importance, and, difficult as the task  may be, priorities must be developed. We are glad that this fact has been generally recog-  nized by Unesco in recent ycars; and Canada wholeheartedly supports the decision of the  last two General Conferences that education 1s to receive a first priority. This we think is  as it should be, because, in the view of my delegation, education is in the "sine qua non" to  satisfying the needs and aspirations of the world's peoples. I know it has been suggested by  some that the priority currently being given to education should be only of a temporary  character, but it is difficult for me to imagine circumstances in the foresecable future that  will justify diminishing Unesco's present emphasis on education.
16.12 I am aware of the enhanced priority recently given to science and we all recognize  the importance of Unesco's rôle in this sector, particularly in the developing countries,  However, there can be no scientific progress unless there is a sound educational system.  The priority of science in relation to education seems to us only to recognize the essential  elements in man's progress in controlling his material environment, The basic needs of  education and those of scientific knowledge and development are easily recognized by all  men in every part of the world and the improvement of education and scientific endeavour  hag an urgency which cannot be denied, Accordingly, we have given these two aspects of  Unesco's aims the highest priority.
16.13 Nevertheless, we must not forget the importance of the human and social sciences  and the influence they have on the destiny of mankind. It may very well be that mankind  would be better served in the generation ahead if more emphasis could be given to the dis-  semination of existing knowledge and the application of known scientific truths to the human  situation than by ever-increasing emphasis on the discovery of new knowledge, particularly  in the fields of pure science. I know that many will regard this statement as a heresy inthe  modern scientific and technological age, and I am quite aware that this is not really a ques-  tion of choosing either one or the other. Indeed, the discovery of new knowledge must pro-  ceed hand in hand with the dissemination and application of existing knowledge. The fact  remains, however, that we do have at our disposal today the scientific and technical know-  ledge necessary to substantially improve the lot of men in every part of the world. We must  make use of this knowledge and apply these techniques for the benefit of mankind, and Unesco  certainly ought to continue its efforts in this respect. Surely, we are concerned primarily  with people and only with material things as they affect the lives of individual human beings,  As the Director-General said in his opening address, we must concern ourselves with en-  suring universal respect for justice, law, for human rights and fundamental liberties for all,

16,16 Mr. President , I have spoken mostly about the present programmes and aims of  Unesco and the attitude of the people of my own country toward it, but I should like to end  on a different note. The exciting and complicated times in which we are living have made  it abundantly clear to many thoughtful men that the independence of nations and individuals  can only have meaning if, at the same time, we recognize the growing interdependence  among men and nations. Indeed, unless we recognize the interdependence of all mankind  and the necessity of involving ourselves in the solution of the problems that confront our  neighbours, then the great human aims of peace among men, justice, liberty, freedom and  even material security, may have little meaning. The great Russian novelist, Dostoevsky,  writing in an age when such concepts were not so readily understandable as they are today,  said that "each of us is responsible for everything and to every human being". Undoubtedly,  Dostoevsky's statement goes farther than many of us are willing to concede today, but,  nevertheless, the very existence of the United Nations system is a recognition of the respon-  sibility which men have for their fellow men, a kind of responsibility that truly transcends  national boundaries and distinctions of race or creed or colour. The translation of these  lofty concepts into actions today, this year, and in the future, is indeed a high task and one,  in vnich Unesco should, and must, play an important part.
