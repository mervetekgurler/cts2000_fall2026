6.1 Mr HAEMMERLI (United Nations Development Programme):  Mr President, Mr Chairman of the Executive Board, Mr Director-General,  Your Excellencies and distinguished delegates to this historic session of the  General Conference of Unesco, on behalf of the United Nations Development  Programme (UNDP) and its Administrator, Mr William H. Draper III, allow me first  to extend best wishes for a very successful conference.
6.2 Having the privilege of following your debate, and having noted its  high intellectual content and its focus on some of the most pressing issues  facing the world, and particularly the developing countries, and given the  excellence of the documentation before you, 1 am confident that this Conference  Will chart a new course for the next six years that Will position Unesco at the  forefront of some of the key problems of the 1990s. IJNDP looks forward to  working hand in hand with Unesco to meet some of these challenges.
6.3 The task, while formidable, is made easier by virtue of the fact that  the analyses of world trends contained in the Draft Medium-Term Plan before you  converge with the position expressed by my own organisation in defining its role  in the 1990s. Moreover, there is a great promise of effective joint UNDP-Unesco  action because the collaboration between our two organisations in the field of  development co-operation is marked by a new sense of purpose, due in great part  to the dynamic leadership of the Director-General.
6.4 UNDP particularly welcomes the Draft Plan@s focus on human development  to stem the tide of decline initiated by the economic policies of the 1980s.  Mounting external debt, unfavourable terms of trade, recession and painful  structural adjustment have produced a significant decline in human development.  Since the early 198Os, the share of government expenditures for education,  health and other areas of human development has declined by over 40 per cent in  most low-income countries. A tragic result of this decline is that, despite the  drastic economic reforms and the related reductions in government outlays,  inevitable negative consequences on further social progress and economic growth  Will remain. In the meantime, the debt problem is still pressing, having reached  $1.3 trillion, and growing.
6.5 A recently issued report of the Secretary-General of the United Nations  on the external debt crisis and development vividly illustrates the impact of  the policies of the 1980s on education. The report compares low-income countries  with low debt in the 1980s to low-income countries with high debts. It points  out that while low-income countries as a whole had strong primary-school  enrolment rates in the 197Os, those with high debts in the 1980s have completely  stagnated at the level of 75 per cent enrolment. What is even more alarming is  that projections of the current trends reveal that these countries may not even  reach 80 per cent enrolment by the year 2000. In other words, even by the end of  this Century, more than one Child in five may not benefit from primary school in  low-income countries that have undergone a debt-servicing crisis. The same  unfortunate scenarios unfold for secondary and tertiary education.
6.6 As was eloquently stated by the Minister of Education of Zimbabwe last  Saturday, and by several speakers before her, there is a serious risk that, as a  result of these trends, the manpower gap between the rich and the poor countries  Will widen if no strong remedial action is taken with the help of the  international community. Some of the poorer countries already lack a sufficient  pool of educated and trained people to manage their own future. It is UNDP's  mandate to create self-reliant capacity by training peoele, transferring  knowledge and know-how and strengthening institutions. We are, therefore, keenly  aware of the imperative need to prevent a further gap of human capabilities.
6.7 Demographic and employment factors also affect our future task.  Increased poverty levels, caused by the relatively low levels of growth in the
198Os, are compounded by rapid population growth in most developing countries.  Average population growth is currently 2.1 per cent. (If this trend were to  continue the population in developing countries (excluding China) would double  over the next 30 years.) In addition, there is a significant age structure  difference between developed and developing countries. This means that from 1985  to the year 2000 a11 the net growth in the world population in the 20 to 40  age-group is projected to take place in the developing countries. This means, in  turn, that developing countries must create 700 million new jobs, and the skills  that go with them, by the year 2000, just to accommodate the new entrants into  the labour force.
6.8 The potential for strife and ferment is real. It is essential,  therefore, to redefine the objectives and policies of development programmes.  They must achieve better economic growth while promoting human development and  pursuing employment and equity-oriented policies.
6.9 UNDP is strongly committed to join Unesco in translating the broad  objectives you Will adopt into effective development projects, in accordance  with the preferences of each country.
6.10 UNDP is now preparing a new development cycle which Will caver the  period 1991-1996. The Administrator has called for a significant increase in  levels of funding. The Governing Council of UNDP Will decide on resource  allocations and resource targets at its next session in June 1990. Country  programmes are now in the process of being prepared in many of the countries  that UNDP serves. In this process, UNDP is prepared to bring to the attention of  the planning and financial authorities the importance of devoting more resources  to human development. Unesco, for its part, must be in a position to help  countries develop and implement effective strategies which also attract external  resources.

6.12 As has been noted by many speakers in this general policy debate, UNDP  has joined Unesco, UNICEF and the World Bank in sponsoring and preparing the  World Conference on Education for All, scheduled to be held in Thailand next  March. Among the important features of this Conference, as cari already be seen  from the mandates of the four sponsoring organizations, is the fact that it Will  bring together educators, planning and financial authorities, and governmental  and non-governmental organizations from both developed and developing countries.  The goal is to forge a broad new alliance that Will set in motion and work  towards realistic and attainable goals in the field of education. In addition to  participating in a11 the preparatory phases, including a range of regional and  subregional meetings, UNDP is also providing financial support to Unesco for the  preparation of studies of primary-school effectiveness in selected countries. We  are working together on two round tables for the Conference, one on  environmental education, the other on the impact of technological change on  basic learning needs. Moreover, we are exploring other initiatives to follow up  the Conference.

In liaison with the World Bank, the African  Development Bank and bilateral donors, a pilot project is currently being  carried out for the region in one country, the objective being to present  fundable proposais to a Round-Table Conference.
