[1] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] The meeting is open. 1 call on the  delegate of Uruguay.  [2] Dr. Cyro GIAMBRUNO (Uruguay) — [Trans-  lation from the Spanish] Mr. President, gent-  lemen, Uruguay comes to this Conference with  renewed faith that humanity’s urgent problems  can be adequately tackled within the terms of  the commitments prescribed for Unesco by the  Constitution of 1945, which defines our object as  the advancement of international peace and the  general prosperity of mankind through the co-  operation of all nations in the fields of education,  science and culture. We ourselves have nothing  important to add to the views Uruguay propounded  at the Third Session of the General Conference in  Beirut, save to express our gratification that the  ideas set forth by our delegation on that occasion  have been taken into consideration and adopted  by the Director-General of the Organization, who  has agreed on the need for combining with the per-  manent long-range plans of Unesco a concrete  programme of immediate action designed to  diminish international tensions and to apply within  the Organization’s sphere whatever measures are  required by the circumstances of the moment.  Thus our ideals and our practical work can now  exert their maximum force at the exact points  where collision between ideologies and ways of life  provides occasions for resentment which threaten  to bring to nought the noblest aspirations of the  human mind and the generous impulse of the  nations that founded Unesco.
13] We are persuaded that Unesco’s work over  the whole of its field can contribute effectively to  the intellectual and moral solidarity of mankind  and thus serve the cause of peace; but we also  feel that there is room for an emergency pro-  gramme of measures designed to exercise a direct  and immediate influence on the factors governing  the peace of the world. It is for that reason that  we regard the resolution passed by the Twentieth  Session of the Executive Board as most timely, and  earnestly trust that the present Conference will  consider the possibility of including in its pro-  gramme adequate measures to avert the threats  lowering over the world today and bring to the  United Nations’ work for peace the swifter aid  necessitated by the perils of the cold war by which  we are now surrounded.  [4] The Charter of the United Nations is the  bridge by which all peace-loving nations may  attain the goal of living in harmony as good neigh-  bours. In this difficult period Unesco's task can  only be to give to culture and science the where-  withal to find the common denominator for  agreement, to their mutual profit, between the  two worlds now at odds.  [6] We have just heard distinguished speeches by  the Chairman of the Executive Board and by the  Director-General, clothing the idea I have out-  lined in vigorous words and showing plainly the  need for all countries belonging to Unesco to put  forth their strength in so applying our Organiza-  tion’s programme as to achieve through education,  science and culture the lofty goals of moral unity  between the nations and social progress for the  peoples.  [6] The General Conference of Unesco has chosen  lovely Florence as the seat of its Fifth Session as a  symbol of universal peace. Florence draws its life  from the spiritual ferment of its great geniuses, its  unrivalled masters: Dante, Cimabue, Giotto, Orca-  gna, Donatello, Masaccio, Brunelleschi, Michel-  angelo, Rafael, who left us the treasures all around  us of lofty culture and art which might fittingly  be called divine. Churches, palaces, museums and  libraries all show clearly what can be done by men    creating and building in peace. These stupendous  works of human genius plead to the world for  peace that they may live on for the generations yet  to come. Peace is the burden of the pealing bells  in Giotto’s tower, and all the geniuses who have  brought light to the world add their prayers for  peace. Florence, the mother-city of Humanism  and the Renaissance, 1s a symbol of universal  peace. Would that all men of good will might  come to Florence, that they might become imbued  with the spirit of Good Government symbolized in  Ambrogio Lorenzetti's wonderful frescoes in the  Hall of Peace of the City Palace of Siena.  [7] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] 1 call on the delegate of Iraq.  [8] Dr. Mohamed Fadhel JAMALI (Iraq) —  Mr. President, fellow delegates, my delegation  takes much pleasure in joining other delegations  in appreciating the hospitality of the Italian  Government and the beautiful, historical and  cultural city of Florence. We also join in the  praise of the most worthy Director-General of the  Organization, for his noble spirit and high leader-  ship of the Organization. We subscribe to all that  has been said in this connexion.  [9] Permit me to be honest and frank and state  here bluntly before this august assembly the atti-  tude of my people towards this Organization.  Essentially, my people have lost faith in all inter-  national organizations, Unesco included. The  reasons for this attitude are numerous. To begin  with, the decision of the United Nations on the  partition of Palestine, which led to the homeless-  ness of one million Arab people, shook the faith of  my people in the justice and conscience of inter-  national organizations as a whole. In the second  place, my people feel that they are hardly repre-  sented in the administration of these organizations,  while certain other States get the lion's share. In  the third place, they feel that while the benefits of  these organizations are scattered all over the globe,  they hardly reach Iraq. In other words, the  popular thought about international organizations,  including Unesco, is that they suffer from discri-  mination, partisanship and power politics. What-  ever truth exists in these accusations, I hope that  they may be only temporary and that the inter-  national situation will improve in such a way as  to make my people feel the beneficial effect of  international co-operation in general and Unesco  in particular.  [10] There is a brighter side to the situation,  however. This year the Iraqi Government passed  legislation for the formation of a National Com-  mission, and as a sequel to the most fruitful visit  of Dr. Raadi, the Unesco representative to the  Middle East, the Iraqi National Commission was
90  formed, and we hope to proceed with the work of  Unesco vigorously.  [11] We are here to exchange views and share  experiences as a family of nations. Each of us has  many small or big problems. Yet many of these  problems are common to us all and no matter  how small or local a problem may be, directly or  indirectly, it affects us all. We in Iraq have many  experiences to share with the rest of the world.  To begin with we have experienced the univer-  sality of science, culture and learning. Baghdad,  the “home of peace” as it was called in the Middle  Ages, was a great world centre of learning and  culture in its days of glory. The Islamic culture  which flourished in Baghdad under the great  Caliphs is itself a synthesis of the cultures of East  and West. The Arabs were the first to bring  together elements of Indian and Chinese and Per-  sian cultures, on the one hand, and Greek, Roman  and Syriac learning and philosophy, on the other.  This is exactly what Unesco desires to do. We,  the Arabs, through the lessons of history, have  come to learn that science and culture, no matter  what their national quality or locality, are the pro-  perty of humanity at large. No geographical or  political boundaries can isolate ideas and truths  indefinitely.  [12] Iraq has another experience to share with  many members of Unesco. It suffered from wars  and destruction several times in history, and after  each catastrophe we began a new life—successive  civilizations from the Sumerian, 5,000 B.C., to the  great Islamic civilization in the middle of this era.  [13] Iraq may be truly described as the paradise  of archaeologists. Its mines of antiquities are ever  rich. This year we had three sites under excava-  tion and a good deal of new treasure has been  added to the knowledge of mankind, especially in  the field of mathematics. These excavations, by  the way, represent an excellent example of inter-  national co-operation. British and American  scholars co-operate with Iraqi scholars in some  excavations. I wish, in this connexion, to express  our gratitude to Unesco for providing a year's  scholarship for an Iraqi archaeologist.  [14] Each war and destruction was followed by a  new uprise and a new civilization. The last  devastating destruction of Baghdad was in 1258,  when all the centres of learning and the libraries  of Baghdad were destroyed by Hulaku. From that  war Iraq has never recovered. Thus Iraq today  represents a typical country where post-war re-  construction is overdue and where Unesco can  find a fertile ground for activity and co-operation.  [15] The new Iraq, which is not yet thirty years  old, under the leadership of His Majesty the late  King Feisal the First, began to develop its new  educational system on all levels. On the ele-  mentary level, schools were opened, even in the                               remotest villages, and mobile schools for the  nomadic tribes were provided. Evening schools  for adults and classes for combating illiteracy were  encouraged. On the university level, besides  opening colleges for the various professions, Iraq  began sending students to European and American  universities, as well as to the American University  of Beirut and the universities of Egypt and Turkey.  Today over a thousand students study at the Iraq  Government's expense outside Iraq. This does not  include a number of students who travel abroad to  study at their own expense.  [16] In its educational work, Iraq faces, like  many other countries, some very grave problems.  | shall deal only with one problem, and that is  the one which touches the world situation today.  In a divided world like the one we live in, subver-  sive movements are bound to spring up and be  very virulent in any country which is under-  developed, and no matter what precautions are  taken, the effects of these subversive movements  on students’ ideology and discipline cannot be  ignored.  [17] We believe that faith in the spiritual values  of man and respect for the dignity of the indi-  vidual, along with effective social and economic  reforms, provide the only means to remedy the  situation, and Unesco must impress this fact on  the educational systems of the world. Viewing the  world situation realistically, education has to appre-  ciate that the path to freedom, peace, justice and  tolerance is not an easy or a simple one and, while  education should cultivate respect for freedom,  [reedom should not be granted to those who go  out to destroy it. Those within a given country  who cry for freedom in order to destroy it, those  who cry for democracy in order to destroy demo-  cracy, and those who speak for the masses in order  to exploit and enslave the masses, should not be  given the chance to do so.  [18] It is not fair nor right to teach peace and  preach peace while the world today is arming  heavily. In matters of freedom, peace and  tolerance, we must have universal unified defini-  tions, standards and measures. Let us hope that  the Human Rights Convention will provide these  definitions, standards and measures. Until that is  done, the younger generation has to understand  that the path for human survival and human pro-  gress 1S not a straight one, nor a smooth one.  [19] A philosophy of education must combine a  high ideology with complete realism. Unesco  must work to formulate and to disseminate such a  philosophy of education. This, in our view, 1s  much more important than any technical or scien-  tific projects which Unesco might undertake. This  universal conflict of ideologies today presents  Unesco with the greatest challenge. No one can
91  remain indifferent or neutral in this world of ours.  The question is also raised, whether students should  or could be taught that differing ideologies can  peacefully coexist side by side, and whether the  principle of live and let live could be applied.  That would be quite possible and desirable if the  conflicting ideologies had a truce, just as world  religions, which warred with one another in ages  past, have come to have a truce and to live side by  side. But, so long as one side uses methods of  virulence and penetration, self-protection and self-  defence become unavoidable, if not a necessity, for  national and cultural survival.
120] An essential condition of self-defence, how-  ever, 1s continuous self-criticism and self-examina-  tion. Complacency, self-deceit and self-conceit,  and power politics might easily lead to loss of  ground before the opposing ideology. The young  generation must be so brought up as to search for  the truth and the whole truth, and not to be  swayed by a press or radio that is one-sided, a  press or radio which presents only one point of  view of a certain vested interest or certain pressure  groups. There is nothing so inimical to demo-  cracy and good international relationships as a  one-sided press or propaganda machine. Education  for a democracy must be such as to prevent super-  ficial judgments or one-sided knowledge about  situations. We accuse dictatorships of controlling  information, but in democratic countries in the  name of freedom there is freedom of misinforma-  tion and freedom of withholding information and  truth. Education must provide the prophylactic  the world needs in this respect.  [21] A tragic example of the one-sided control of  world propaganda by a certain partisan group 1s  what the world has absorbed about Palestine and  the Arab world during the last thirty vears. The  peace-loving Arabs who, since World War I, have  been striving for freedom, unity and progress, have  been pictured by an uncouth partisan press as  savage, reactionary and bloodthirsty. This propa-  ganda has influenced many people in the world, to  the extent of creating in the Middle East a situation  so tragic and so inimical to peace. Unesco should  impress on its members that the next generation  will not accept one-sided propaganda as final truth  and must insist that they hear the other side.  Otherwise democracy would be a fake and peace  would be endangered. One of the facts that stand  in the way of genuine international co-operation in  Iraq in particular, and in the Arab world in gen-  eral, is the fact that the young generation find the  western world so inconsiderate and so unappreci-  ative of the rights and position of the Arab world.  [22] The tragedy of the million homeless Arab  refugees of Palestine is eloquent proof that the  western world has inflicted great injustice and     R E   " a        cruelty on the peace-loving Arabs of Palestine,  without their having committed any crime to  deserve this state of affairs. The school-age chil-  dren of these refugees, who owe their misery pri-  marily to the United Nations, certainly deserve  greater consideration in the 1951 Budget. Arab  refugee students at the university level should be  given free access to and free opportunities in the  universities of Europe and America. We have just  read that a number of Palestinian Arab students  were about to be turned out of a certain American  university because they are refugees and cannot  afford to continue to pay for their education.  Unesco should see to it that this is not done. It  is very detrimental to international spirit and  morality. Those nations who are primarily re-  sponsible for the human tragedy of Palestine must  see to it that the situation is partially alleviated,  and Unesco is the agency to bring this about. It  is the intention of my delegation to appeal to this  august assembly while the Budget 1s being  discussed, to see that the Arab refugee relief 1s  given even greater consideration. It is a sad thing  for education that the realities of the world situa-  tion have gone so much counter to ideals. Educa-  tion, while pointing out the ideas of freedom,  truth and justice to the young generation, must  always critically analyse and present the ills of  humanity and provide corrective and prophylactic  measures. Unesco must see to it that the edu-  cational systems of the world adopt such a philo-  sophy. It is not an easy task, but it is because it  is not easy that we have met here together. May  our endeavours in this Conference be crowned with  success.  [23] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] 1 call on the delegate of Lebanon.  [24] Mgr. MAROUN (Lebanon) — [Translation  from the French] Mr. President, fellow delegates,  the head of the Lebanese delegation has done me  the honour of requesting me, as permanent dele-  gate to Unesco, to speak here for my country.  Accordingly, it is my pleasant duty to greet, on  behalf of Lebanon, the country which is receiving  us today, and this city,so highly qualified to serve  as the setting for an international conference of  culture, science and education.  [25] The contribution this land has made to  civilization is incalculable. In the earliest days of  culture, Italy was the inexhaustible source of all  the highest products of the human mind. At the  dawn of modern history, the science, philosophy  and literature of Florence, no less than her  painting, sculpture and architecture, were the ins-  piration of her northern and western neighbours,  the countries of the Danube and the countries of the  Rhine. The reverses of fortune and even the injuries
92  Florence has suffered endear her to us. We love  her today as the symbol of a glorious past. Leba-  non, the youngest of States but one of the oldest  countries of the world, associates itself with all  the Members of Unesco in paying tribute to Flor-  ence and in thanking the Government and people  of Italy for the welcome they have so generously  extended to us, enabling the Fifth Session of the  General Conference of Unesco to assemble amid  these reminders of Florentine humanism.  [26] After four years’ service with Unesco, 1  should now like to present, on behalf of the  Lebanese delegation, not any theoretical consider-  ations that may be prompted by the present pro-  gramme, but simple reflections that have been  borne in on me over these past years, as  Unesco was gaining strength. 1 should be the  first to give our Organization credit for express-  ing the sincere desire of the peoples of the  world to forge the instruments of peace through  their efforts in understanding and mutual assis-  tance. The time for Unesco's action has come;  she is the epitome of the world’s struggle against  the grim spectre of war.  [27] To measure the progress we have made, we  need only consider the number of Unesco’s Member  States and the development of its programme.  Judging by figures, Unesco is clearly gaining  ground; in four years, twelve new States have  joined the Organization—a conclusive proof of the  growing faith it has inspired. Yet surer proofs  are to be found in the collaboration of over a  hundred international non-governmental organiza-  tions, each in its special field, the steadily  increasing number of National Commissions carry-  ing out the most varied activities in forty-three  countries and, lastly, the interest evidenced by  newspaper articles and broadcasts dealing with the  programme and work of Unesco. To this must be  added the prestige Unesco has gained at its General  Conferences, in North and South America as a  result of the Mexico Conference, then in the Middle  East following the Beirut Conference, and now in  western Europe during the present session in Flor-  ence; this prestige is a fact of practical importance  and will in time spread to all parts of the world.  The growing faith of the peoples in Unesco 1s  justified by the development of its programme and  the distinguished leadership of its Director-  General.  [28] Instead of the haphazard mass of projects  that must needs characterize the formative stage of  any organization of such scope as ours, we have  before us today a clear and realistic programme,  reflecting Unesco’s resolve to strive by the most  effective means for the building of a better world.  The clearly-defined aim is to work out what might  be termed “a new scientific humanism’, which,               we trust, may enable the men of today to cope  more effectively with the present scientific revolu-  tion and which will be the means of establishing a  new world in which everyone will realize that he  has a part to play. Unesco’s endeavours, as set  forth in the present programme, are truly in  keeping with its responsibilities. They will draw  fresh strength from the firm, clear-sighted and  systematic methods that the Director-General is  applying throughout the Organization. The  Lebanese delegation has pleasure in associating  itself with the tributes that Mr. Torres Bodet has  received from the intellectual leaders of all  countries.  [29] And yet, despite these expressions of faith,  there is no denying that Unesco is now surrounded  by a rising tide of scepticism. What can be the  reason for this disillusionment, and how can it be  checked?  [30] In his stirring speech yesterday, the  Director-General described most forcibly the con-  ditions the States impose on Unesco. The Lebanese  delegation feels that in 1945 Unesco was given  a structure which took no account of the spiritual  and cultural problems of Member States. A Secre-  tariat was established, to work as though it were  a centre from which instructions were issued and  projects framed for the entire world; but, from  both the geographic and the cultural points of  view, the countries concerned are at varying dis-  tances from the centre. This accounts for the  various “blind spots” and the lack of wunder-  standing. It is idle to say that the National  Commissions can make these resolutions truly  effective by adjusting them to the special require-  ments of their countries. The National Commis-  sions are handicapped by these same blind spots  and cannot, unaided, make Unesco’s ideals the  driving force they should be.  [31] To make up for this structural weakness,  the Lebanese delegation considers that Unesco  must make its presence more directly felt if it is to  sway the minds of men. The Director-General has  already thought of such a solution, the value of  which is proved by the establishment of a Regional  Office for the Western Hemisphere. We ourselves  would have been glad to have an office for the  Middle East, as suggested in 1947 by Mr. Jacques  Maritain and approved by the General Conference  in Mexico City. Sooner or later, it will be neces-  sary to revert to this project and it would be best  to do so forthwith.  [32] Mention should also be made of the condi-  tions Unesco has created for itself and which, to a  large extent, account for the distrust and indif-  ference with which it is surrounded. Unesco exists  and works through its Secretariat alone as the  heart and nerve-centre of the Organization. The  men forming it must therefore stand for the
93  highest principles, for it is surely futile to try to  act well before thinking rightly. The Secretariat  must be imbued with a desire for truth, upright-  ness and impartiality, equal to the high inter-  national mission to which it has pledged itself.  [33] To illustrate my argument, may 1 mention  the following point? The resolutions adopted by  the General Conference are inspired by the ideals  of Unesco: peace, understanding, brotherhood  and educational reconstruction. These resolutions  are entrusted to the relevant Departments that have  to consider the best methods of implementation.  They then begin to be interpreted. The modern  world is far too inclined to corrupt the most sacred  truths. The same words mean utterly different  things in different countries: the interpretation  placed upon freedom, peace and human dignity  depends entirely on political ideologies. Unesco  should stand as the very symbol of rectitude and  thus win for itself the most enduring renown. It  should be the means whereby man may realize his  aspirations and voice his rights to freedom and  respect for the individual. But because Unesco has  held aloof from the everyday life of the peoples,  its prestige is seriously impaired today.  [34] When he so eloquently presented his report  to the General Conference yesterday morning,  Mr. Torres Bodet asked whether Unesco’s work in
1950 should be planned along the same lines as  in 1945. He stated that it was for the General  Conference to define Unesco’s attitude in the  present world situation. Only by grasping what  this situation 1s, shall we be able to determine  what contribution we can make towards peace.  [35] I should like to reply to the Director-  General’s question by referring to the allegory of  Botticelli’s famous painting of Pallas subduing the  centaur. It was discovered not long ago in an  attic of the Pitti Palace and, with its lofty inspira-  tion drawn from mythology, symbolizes not merely  the trimphant power of thought, but also the  supremacy of gentleness which conquers by com-  passion rather then by threats. The goddess is  probably relying to some extent on her resplendent  armour; but, garlanded with flowers, she turns  with calm confidence towards the monster, on  which she places her unarmed right hand. And  the monster, as though inwardly subdued, offers  no resistance. His spirit is tamed because he is  forced to recognize a truth that is stronger than  force, a grace that is fairer than beauty.  [36] Amid the growing threats of a new world  war, we must realize that peace between the  nations is not an unattainable aspiration. Hence  the necessity, for peoples and international institu-  tions alike, to take stock of the underlying condi-  tions for peace. In this hour of moral and spi-  ritual, rather than material and political, crises,  palliatives are becoming worse than the disease       and noble intentions more dangerous than outright  surrender. The drama of mankind turns upon a  conflict of mutually incompatible  materialism and idealism. Peace may be won only  if we, in all humility, grasp the true destiny of  mankind. If the Herculean efforts of this Organ-
1zation are not to remain futile, if we are not one  day to admit, as peoples and as responsible indivi-  duals, “ergo erravimus’’, it is absolutely essential  that we should, when preparing our programme,  draw inspiration from the springs of true peace  —that peace which was conferred upon mankind  by the “Prince of Peace’, as the true expression  of his spirit and token of his kingdom. [38] Sir Sarvepalli RADHAKRISHNAN (India) —  We have listened with interest to the speeches of  the Chairman of the Executive Board and the  Director-General. We have also read the Director-  General's Report. May I offer my cordial congra-  tulations to the Director-General and his staff on  their earnest and enthusiastic efforts to make  Unesco an organ of the new civilization, of the  new society free, full and rich, a society of which  we dream though we no longer dare hope for its  early realization. The Deputy Director-General,  Dr. Laves, will be leaving us at the end of the  Conference. He has done a great deal for the  clarification of our financial problems. We wish  him good luck and express our hope that we may  meet him again in the years to come as a member  of the American delegation, even as we welcome  today Dr. Julian Huxley as a member of the British  delegation.  [39] The activities of Unesco are getting better  known in my country than hitherto. The estab-  lishment of the National Commission last year  which includes the leading workers in arts and  sciences, in education and culture, the Field  Science Co-operation Centre for South Asia  operating from New Delhi, the recent Seminar on  Rural Adult Education at Mysore, the book coupon  scheme, which we have utilised to the extent of
75,000 dollars, the study of social tensions under-  taken by the Department of Anthropology and  some Universities, and several other matters have  brought the work of Unesco to the attention of our  intellectuals, artists and University men. One  evidence of this increasing interest is that crit-  icisms are often heard of what we do or fail to do  in the General Conference and the Executive Board.  The delegate of Iraq referred this morning to the  complaints which small countries—small in size,  small in industrial development—are making.  They are not made to feel that they are also  ideologies/  organic, integral elements of this great organiza-  tion. The authorities should note this fact.  [40] Mr. President, it is a real joy to meet in  this world-famous centre of European civilization,  where we are reminded at every turn that man  does not live by bread alone or by politics alone.  There are other values for which our organization  stands, the values of beauty and truth. It is noi  industrial prosperity or political achievements,  important as they are, that preserve a people in the  memory of mankind. How much do we know  today of the proceedings of the Athenian Assembly,  of the Roman Senate, of the Government of Byzan-  tium, of the petty conflicts of the Italian States  in the Middle Ages? But though many centuries  have passed, we still regard with devout gratitude  the marvellous works of the Greek poets and  artists, the great literature of Rome, the lovely  creations of Byzantium, the Divine Comedy of  Dante and the works of Michelangelo, to name a  few. These are the permanent possessions of the  human race. The great achievements of art and  religion, like those of science and philosophy are  the results of what a man does with his solitari-  ness, to use Whitehead’s words. If we are never  solitary, we are never truly human. In those  moments of stillness, we transcend the contingent  distinctions of race and class, nation and religion  and touch the eternal human in man. What is  most unique 1s the most universal. In great works  of art, we recreate our most inward experiences  by clothing them with universal significance.  What lifts a work of art from commonplaceness is  intensity of experience and universality of spirit.  [41] These are just the things we miss in the  modern world. The dignity of man requires that  his individuality be recognised, that he be not lost  in an anonymous crowd. On all sides we see the  perfecting of organization and the withering of  man. Our thinking is done for us, our amuse-  ments are made for us, our standards of beauty  and goodness are fixed for us. We have no per-  sonal opinions, no reasoned judgments. The  eclipse of human rights by state compulsions, the  debasing of ethical standards by demagogy in all  spheres, politics and art, trade and international  relations, the indoctrination by the press, radio  and cinema, the bureaucratic control of education,  information and publicity are dehumanising men  and producing an anthill society. We are anxious  to spread literacy, to raise the school-leaving age.  We should see to it that regulation of thought and  control of opinion are kept within limits and are  not used for corrupting people’s minds. In the  last resort man lives by that which cannot be  regulated. Again, international understanding is  one of our central aims and yet in reality we are  being educated in the wrong way. We are given  to the contemplation of our own virtues and the              condemnation of other peoples’ weaknesses. This  nation is immoral, that other is decadent. This  nation consists of prototypes of slave mentality,  that consists of barbarous subhuman gangsters.  This sort of idiotic chatter, vile nationalist propa-  ganda goes on in the drawing rooms even of  those who consider themselves to be cultivated. It  is no answer to say that the other people indulge  in abuse and wilful misunderstanding of us. If we  do as they do, there is nothing more to be said.  “] love my country because I love all countries’,  Mazzini said. Every country is someone's country.  We must be taught to ponder over the defects of  ourselves and the qualities of others, for no nation  has a monopoly of either virtue or of vice. We are  all human, much too human.  [42] Owing to the neglect of responsible thinking  and the violation of the basic unity of mankind by  nationalist vanity, we have suffered in recent  decades. The demands of group loyalty, nation,  race, class, religions, community dominated us.  We snarled at one another, determined not to  give in to the other. We have seen marvels of  man’s intellectual penetration, but we have also  seen horrors of the savagery of the human heart. [72] Dr. Mahmoud HASAN (Pakistan) —  Mr. President, ladies and gentlemen, two nights  ago when I went to the Opera as a guest of the  Government of Italy and enjoyed its magnificent  hospitality, I saw bouquets of flowers in the  theatre. To my mind those flowers symbolized  one aspect of this Conference, the beauty and grace  of this lovely city of Florence, where we are the  guests of the people and Government of Italy, and  also the bouquets of well-deserved praise which  have been presented to the worthy Director-  General of Unesco for the splendid work which  he has done in this great Organization. 1 am  adding small blossoms of congratulation and  praise to that bouquet which has been presented  to him. I listened with very great interest and  pleasure to the speeches of the Director-General  and my respected friend, the Chairman of the  Executive Board, Sir John Maud, and I admired  their eloquence, the beauty of phrase and concept;  but I am not here only to praise those things. I  am paying my tribute to their passionate sincerity  and burning desire to arrest the downward flight  of this world of ours along the path of destruc-  tion. Reference has been made to the terrible  clouds that are on the horizon and which threaten  to destroy the shattered and broken lives of this  world of ours. We who have seen the beauty of  [taly and the art of Florence, have also seen the  terrible scars and wounds of war in the destroyed  buildings, which warn us that if ever a third war  comes perhaps we shall be reduced to that stage  which was represented in a cartoon of Punch,  when two monkeys, a male and a female, sat on  a tree and said “Let us start over again the whole  work of creation’.  [73] The East, ladies and gentlemen, has a great  and glorious history because it was the torch-  bearer of culture and civilization and humanity  through the ages. During more modern times  it is the West which has been the leader of pro-  gress, civilization and culture. But what we  people of the East have seen in the last war has  really shaken our belief in that culture and  civilization which we thought was permanent and  unalterable. We have some indication of that  terrible carnage that took place. When I accuse  the West, I am fully conscious that the hands of  the people of the East are also not clean. Probably  because we people of the East are not as thorough  as the people of the West are, probably because  we do not possess the wonderful but terrible  atomic weapons which can create havoc and  destruction, the destruction which we have  created 1s comparatively small. If progress and  civilization mean that, the creation of more and  more terrible engines of destruction, then I would  rather be uncivilized, I would rather go back to  the times when warfare was as it is represented  by these beautiful paintings round us, where  people met and fought and the destruction was  relatively small. The clash of man and man in  the great battles of the past was not nearly as  destructive as in the great battles of today. This  is the feeling which is uppermost in our minds  when we think of the work and the ideals of  Unesco. 1 listened with rapt attention to those  beautiful and wise words of the Director-General  when he spoke about the purpose of Unesco and  the means of achieving it. His words find an  echo in our hearts—I am speaking now as a  Mussulman and a Pakistani, because we have  always believed in peace.  [74] Only three months after the establishment  of our country, a conference was called by my  Government, of all scientists, men of letters and  scholars, who decided that the educational 1deo-  logy of Pakistan should be based on universal  brotherhood, universal tolerance and social justice.  These are the ideals which we have been following  for the last 1,400 years and, if I may say so with  all respect and humility, in this regard we have  nothing to learn from Unesco, but a great deal  to learn about the methods, the practical ways  in which these ideals can be realized; and it 1s in  this respect that we look forward to the great  work which Unesco is doing and in which we  want to be very humble sharers.  [75] Sir John Maud asked some questions in his  address. Are the National Commissions and the  countries really doing what they ought to do in  order to carry out the ideals of Unesco? 1 feel  that the answer to this question from most people  in those countries will be—if we are honest and  sincere—very unsatisfactory. Let us admit that.  But the defect and default lies not only with these  nations and these countries but also with Unesco,  because Unesco has not made itself a living spirit,  a vivid reality to the people of most countries. I  am speaking only as one who comes from the  East. Therefore we have to find out how best  Unesco can achieve those great and noble ideals  in which all of us have faith and with which we  all agree. Unesco has created a vast organ-  ization, a very large and effective machine. But  I just now referred to the mechanical progress of  the West, and I am afraid that the western  mechanical mind is apt to worship the machine,  much more so than the purpose for which the    machine was created, and the machine goes on  multiplying itself and complicating itself and,  therefore, when we look at Unesco’s work and  see that it is attempting to do everything, I am  afraid we are filled with some fear. And here I  am afraid I do not agree with that worthy and  great professor, Professor Piaget, when he said  that Unesco was attempting everything and having  some success in everything. It reminds me of a  story from my country. There was a couple, a  middle-class labouring couple, who had a son  about twenty vears old. The wife said to the  husband: “Go and find a bride for our son. The  bride should be twenty years old.” The husband  thought a little and said: ‘If I cannot get a bride  of twenty, may I bring four girls of five years  each?” A little success in everything, to my mind,  is not as desirable as a great deal of success in  a few things. And I think Unesco is at the  present time rightly blamed for burying itself  under an avalanche of reports, memoranda,  pamphlets and directives. I am not blaming the  Secretariat; nothing is further from my mind than  that: I think the Secretariat has been doing  splendid work and its difficulties must be  enormous. Even though I have not had the  privilege of being inside Unesco House and being  associated with its work, as Secretary of Educa-  tion to the Government in my country I see the  vast amount of literature which is brought into  my room every month or every week, and which,  I must confess, I have not the time to read. So  that it is really a question of the machine mul-  tiplying itself. If I may be permitted to change  my metaphor, it is like a vast building such as  the very vast building of the Pitti Palace, where  we cannot find our way at all—I think the meta-  phor is appropriate—a vast building which goes  on growing and growing and which reminds me  of two lines of the English poet, who in speaking  about such a building says: “Windows that  exclude the light and passages that lead to  nothing.” I am not suggesting that the work of  the Secretariat of Unesco is of that type; I am  merely submitting that if Unesco were to con-  fine itself to that very great and noble task, that  of rousing the people of the world and compelling  them to do things in order to achieve our ideals  and objectives, perhaps that would be better than  Unesco trying to do everything itself.  [76] In my own country, for example, we have  very great difficulties. We are a backward people  in a certain sense, as the great leader of the Indian  delegation said, but we also know what are our  difficulties and what the remedies for those dif-  ficulties are. 1 do not think that people who do  not know our own problems intimately can tell  us much about them or the means of overcoming
102  our difficulties. What I would, therefore, sug-  gest is that the Director-General and his worthy  colleagues should come out into the world, as it  were, and meet the peoples of the world, the  National Commissions, the governments, the  unofficial organizations of different countries, and  discuss with them their difficulties and their  problems, and arouse and inspire them. These  are the few words which I emphasize. I am fully  conscious that we have occasional visits from the  Adviser or liaison officer of Unesco, who comes  to our country—I suppose that is true of other  countries also. He says, “How do you do?” to  the Education Secretary, has an audience with the  Education Minister, distributes a few pamphlets,  and arranges his plane journey for the next coun-  try on his list. This is not really going to arouse  the conscience of the people of those backward  countries. I would like, for example, that people  in my country, those honest workers in the paths  of truth, those who want to achieve the ideals  which Unesco has before it, should have the  opportunity to meet the great personalities of  Unesco, so that mind will be rubbed against mind,  and there would come a torch, a flame, to set  alight the world. It is no use sending out what  we call in our language “the horses of paper ”.  These do not really achieve anything. I would  go even further, even at the risk of being regarded  as Utopian; I would suggest that Unesco should  charter a large ship and put in it four or five  hundred honest workers from every country who  would go round and visit as many countries as  possible and meet, very informally, large numbers  of people and workers, so that they could make  that intimate personal contact, and arouse that  enthusiasm which alone can achieve something  in this world. These are, to my mind, far more  important things to do than having the poor  harassed Director-General and his associates sitting  in rooms and poring over reports and directives  and things of that type. I have been an educa-  tionist, an active educationist all my life, and for  the last two years I have been a Secretary to the  Government, sitting in a room and doing that  kind of thing, and I realize that one week of  coming into the field, as it were, and working  with the people, is far more valuable than any  amount of mere talk and conferences. l am a  great believer in conferences; | think these inter-  national conferences have very great value, but  they are merely a means to an end. To quote a  Persian saying—with the permission of the Per-  sian delegation—“they sat down, they talked,  they dispersed”. I am afraid this is a description  which could apply to many of our conferences.  Therefore, I would, in all humility, urge that  Unesco should change its outlook, that Unesco              should realize that it can only achieve its purpose  if it arouses the conscience of the world and  compels us, as it were, to carry out these  objectives.  [77] We have heard of wars, we have heard of  crusades. I believe that Unesco must launch a  new crusade, a new crusade of peace. Sir John  Maud spoke about the wandering scholars of the
20th century. 1 would like Unesco to send out  the wandering missionaries of peace, the roving  ambassadors of love and affection and mutual  co-operation among all the nations of the world.  We have to work in the spirit of the great Savo-  narola, who, in this very city, five hundred years  ago, spoke fearlessly against tyranny, against  all kinds of wrong, when the Medicis were at the  height of their power, and who, called to the bed-  side of the dying Lorenzo, refused to give absolu-  tion because Lorenzo refused to free the country,  which alone would earn him absolution for his  sins. I am a great believer in Unesco. Sir John  has said that Unesco changes the minds of people.  May 1 humbly suggest that we should change not  only the minds, but also try and change the hearts  of people, because we are afraid of the mind, the  reason, alone. 1 think that if we change the  hearts and the minds of people, if we really create  in them the consciousness that they must come  together if they are to survive, if they are to be  civilized, that they must come together and gird  their loins and work in the great and noble cause  of peace, I think Unesco will have achieved its  purpose. Whether a scheme here, a programme  there, or a project is achieved or not, to my mind,  that is a small thing, and insignificant. Our  national problems are different, they must be dif-  ferent. 1 do not believe in one world, 1 do not  believe that you can reduce all the nations to  one great uniform pattern. I hope not. We  must retain our individualities. We must have  our particular problems, our peculiar problems,  and we must solve them in our own way. What  we need, therefore, is not one uniform panacea  for the evils of the world which Unesco, or any  world organization, can put forward; what we  want is the rousing of the consciences, the minds,  the hearts, the souls of the people, so that all  can come together and say that each of us, in our  own way, in our particular manner, shall all con-  tribute to the building of that noble edifice of  peace which will withstand not only the blast of  atomic bombs and hydrogen bombs, but all the  savagery that the scientific mind of men can  create. If this be the ideal of Unesco, I hope that  we shall be able to come together, to meet to-  gether and not only discuss programmes and pro-  jects to be applied to the world at large, but to  deal with men.  [78] To become a master of words 1s a very  good thing, but mastery of words, I am afraid,  also sometimes degenerates into slavery of words,  and instead of deriving inspiration from life and  reality, we are apt to derive inspiration merely  from words. Let us try to avoid that danger.  Let not Unesco merely concern itself with words,  whether spoken or written, let Unesco come out  in the open field, let it go out and meet every one;  whether he belongs to those savage and half-  savage countries to which reference was made by  previous speakers, or to the highly developed and  civilized countries of the West, it does not matter.  If you search men you find a common humanity,  and it is for Unesco to try and bring out this com-  mon humanity so that all men of all countries,  of all stages of development, may combine together  and work for the achievement of the ideal of  peace and love in this world.  [79] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] 1 call on the delegate of Persia.  [80] Mr. Ali Asghar HEKMAT (Persia) —  [Translation from the French] Mr. President,  fellow delegates, ladies and gentlemen, I feel it  my duty to begin by expressing, on behalf of  the delegation of Persia, my sincerest gratitude  to the Italian Government which has extended  such hospitality to us and supplied all that is  required for a Conference such as ours. This  splendid city of art and beauty in which we are  being received cannot but leave an indelible  impression on the hearts and minds of all who  have the privilege of visiting her.  [81] On reading the interesting report of the  Director-General I noted a few points which may  perhaps be worth drawing to the attention of this  distinguished assembly. The first point which  struck me was the Director-General’s allusion to  the inadequate co-operation of certain Member  States in the carrying out of Unesco’s aims. In  my opinion, it is absolutely necessary for Mem-  ber States and Unesco to co-operate closely; it  is superfluous to mention here that Unesco’s suc-  cess depends to a large extent on the contribution  that Member States make to the work of this  international Organization, since it is they who,  in fact, carry out Unesco’s plans. The delegation  of Persia desires a tightening of all bonds uniting  Member States and Unesco.  [82] In the Director-General’s report, an im-  portant place was given to education, and there  can be no doubt that the work being carried out  in this field will achieve the success it deserves.  The establishment of the Clearing-House for Edu-  cation is one of the most promising developments;  it will be a means of centralizing all information       on education and of communicating it to the  countries wishing to keep abreast of the experience  acquired by other countries. Fundamental edu-  cation is another of the activities being encouraged  by Unesco and tried out in a number of countries.  In our opinion, this type of education should be  extended even further—a task which is the re-  sponsibility of Unesco, with the assistance of the  Member States concerned. I cannot refrain more-  over from mentioning the work that Unesco has  been doing over the past few months in connexion  with adult education; and may 1 say, gentlemen,  that at least as much importance should be attached  to adult as to fundamental education? The best  means of achieving practical results in adult edu-  cation is to apply the simplest methods. It  should here be emphasized that any work carried  out in countries belonging to the same language  group and using the same script should take  account of regional conditions; it 1s impossible  to adopt the same teaching methods in all coun-  tries. For instance, entirely different methods  must be used for teaching the Arabic script  and the Roman alphabet. Regional conferences  organized by governmental or non-governmental  organizations in the neighbouring countries would  be one of the most valuable means of promoting  adult education.  [83] While I'am on this subject, may I add that  these countries should collaborate closely in pre-  serving everything that relates to their common  culture—museums, historic documents, monu-  ments, language, etc. They should do all in their  power to prevent discord due to national pride  and aggressiveness, sparing no efforts to eradicate,  in a spirit of peace and brotherhood, all prejudices  traceable to such feelings; lastly, they should real-  ize that scientists, inventors and scientific  achievements cannot be considered as belonging  to any one country, since science is the property  of the whole world. Avicenna, the Persian philo-  sopher, Balzac, the French writer, Confucius,  the Chinese legislator, and Bach, the German  musician, have rendered signal services to their  several countries and thus enhanced their glory,  but, while each belongs to a particular people, he  cannot be regarded as a citizen of one country  only. We pay tribute to them here, because  mankind as a whole was enriched by their works  and thoughts.  [84] Just as there can be no question of intro-  ducing the same teaching methods in all coun-  tries, we cannot expect National Commissions in  all countries to adopt the same standard methods  for the performance of their duties. National  Commissions should be organized in accordance  with the educational conditions in each particular  country. The aims of all Commissions are
104  naturally the same, but that does not alter the  need for adopting different practical methods.  Similarly, we feel it would be desirable for  Unesco to divide the countries of the world into  special regions and groups for the implementation  of its programme of education, culture and  science, and for it to assign to each of these  groups the programme best suited to its geo-  graphic and historical development. Many are  the nations which would, but cannot, contribute  through education to improving the lot of the  mass of the people and to strengthening inter-  national understanding. It is Unesco’s duty to  give these people the necessary moral and material  assistance and the benefit of expert advice.  [85] I now have only two other matters to dis-  cuss: the book coupon scheme and the exchange  of persons. The book coupon scheme is un-  doubtedly one of the greatest contributions Unesco  has made towards the dissemination of thought,  and, consequently, to the improvement of inter-  national cultural relations. We must acknowledge  how highly we approve this scheme, but we  should be yet more pleased it all Member States  decided to participate in it. With regard to the  exchange of persons, while appreciating the sub-  stantial services rendered by Unesco, I may per-  haps be allowed to suggest that the Organization  should do its utmost to add to the number of  fellowships and seminars, arranging for several  fellowships to be granted to certain countries so  that more fellowship holders may have an  opportunity of studying matters of interest to their  country. Book coupons have already facilitated  the circulation of printed material, and many of  the obstacles due to the need to obtain foreign  currency have thus been removed, at least in the  case of countries that have joined the scheme.  We now have to seek means of solving the same  currency problems for all persons wishing to  undertake study tours. For there are countries  which, owing to hard-currency shortages, find  great difficulty in sending their nationals abroad.  The elimination of such obstacles is primarily the  responsibility of the great powers with centres  for study and scientific research. Moreover, it  should not be forgotten that the economic dif-  ficulties that afflict the world today, and partic-  ularly the smaller countries, are the direct con-  sequence of disputes between the great powers.  They therefore have the primary responsibility  and duty of remedying this situation and helping  the smaller countries to find a way out of this  ECONOMIC Crisis.  [86] Lastly, I feel it necessary, in all frankness,  to draw attention to one important point. Unesco  is above all an educational, scientific and cul-  tural organization and not a political institution.                    Unless we save it from the encroachment of polit-  ical issues, we may endanger the very life and  work of the Organization. Ladies and gentlemen,  [ attended the London Conference which drew up  the Constitution of Unesco in 1945, and now at  the present Conference it is some disappointment  to me to find not only that Unesco has been unable  to stand aside from politics over the past five  years but that it is becoming increasingly en-  tangled in those very chains that Unesco was  created to shatter.  [87] Another danger is that, perhaps through  the Organization’s failure to come to an under-  standing with men of good will, both the Secre-  tariat and future conferences are likely to be  deprived of the services of those persons who  have hitherto been inspired by the sincerest faith.  Mr. President, Mr. Director-General, fellow dele-  gates, let us not forget that Unesco is founded  above all on faith, and that its aim 1s to promote  international understanding. If that faith and  understanding were to be shattered even in the  Executive Board, the Secretariat or the General  Conference, how can we expect them to achieve  those results Unesco desires for the whole world?  Some claim that Unesco is a subject on which  the general public is ill-informed and on which  there are too few publications. I, on the con-  trary, am of the opinion that our Organization  should make itself known by its results and  achievements, for these would be its best adver-  tisement.  [88] I should have liked to make some mention  of the educational reforms still needed in the  country I represent, and of what I consider to be  weaknesses in the Organization itself. But, so  deeply have I been impressed by the beauty of  Florence, this centre of art, that all such matters  are forgotten. A few lines of our national poet,  Saadi, run: “I intended on your arrival to tell you  of my grievances; but what can I say when they  all melt away at your coming?” An interesting  comparison may be drawn between these words  and a passage from Dante, the father of Italian  poetry and a native of this very city. In the  Paradiso we read: ‘I have visted the most radiant  of heavens but the sight was such that on my  return I am left without the power of words .  and even of memory. For nearness to the  desired object so absorbs the mind that the  memory is paralysed.” 1 feel convinced that  Unesco, whose work has made such headway  since the Organization was established, will con-  tinue its onward march and that, thanks to the  efforts of its loyal supporters, the world may at  long last enjoy the blessings of a true and lasting
105  peace, based on the mutual understanding of all  peoples of the world.
