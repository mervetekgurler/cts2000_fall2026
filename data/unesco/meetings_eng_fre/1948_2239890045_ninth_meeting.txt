Mr. WEEDEN (Australia) (Rapporteur of the  Budget Sub-Commission) pointed out three typo-  graphical errors in document 3C/PRG-BUDGET/2.  In paragraph (7) Part II, at the bottom of page 4,  the sum $115,000 should read $105,000; the next  amount, of $25,000, was correct; the $95,000  should read $80,000 and the last words of the  paragraph (on page 5) should read “and the other  financial and administrative services” instead of  “and Budget and Administration”.  M. HUYSMANS (Belgium) congratulated the  Budget Sub-Commission on its excellent work.  He praised Unesco’s spirit of enterprise, which  was by no means damped by the scarcity of funds  at its disposal. The budget of Belgium was  $1,000,000,000 for ordinary expenses and of that  sum Unesco received $100,000, that is, the cost  of one new technical school or the amount of the  grants paid to the four Belgian Universities. It  seemed that to bring about a revolution in ideas  with such means was an almost super-human task  and that, if the Member States did not make  greater sacrifices, Unesco’s aims would never be  reached. That left far too small a margin as a reserve  for programme activities. Mr. HOLLAND (United States of America)  (Chairman of the Drafting Committee) read his  Report, calling attention to an amendment to  document 3C/98, contained in the Addendum to  document 3C/PRG/0.5, which recommended that,  in Part Ill, item 4 (Social Sciences and Huma-  nities) should be divided into two sections :  item 4 : “Social Sciences” and item 5 : ‘‘Philo-  sophy and Humanistic Studies”. ‘Cultural Acti-  vities would then become item 6, and “Com-  munication” item 7.  Mr. Holland proposed the adoption of the reso-  lutions contained in document 3C/98 and its  Addendum.  The CHAIRMAN asked whether there were any  general comments.  Sir John MAUD (United Kingdom) said that  the drafting committee, of which he had been a  member, had been faced with the difficult  question would their fellow delegates in the  plenary meeting be confused if the drafting com-  mittee attempted to change either the order of  the items inside each Chapter, or the order of  the Chapters in the Programme as a whole. He  himself was convinced that the Drafting Com-  mittee had taken the right decision when it  decided to try to improve on the programme  passed at the Mexico City Conference the previous  year, and which had come down from the Exe-  cutive Board almost unchanged in document 3C/5.  The Programme adopted at Mexico City was  a great deal better than any programme the  Conference had had to work on before, but it  had proved rather difficult to explain at home  some of the chapter titles (for instance “Cultural  Interchange” and “Human and Social Relations”)  and to justify them either as coherent parts of  Unesco's work, or as intelligible sub-headings of  the total Programme. The Programme presented  to the Commission differed, in respect of those  two Chapters, from the plan agreed at Mexico  City the previous year.  The second difficulty which had been found  in the previous year’s programme was the lack  of any very clear logic in the order of the six  Chapters into which the programme had been  divided; there again the drafting committee pro-  posed to the Commission an arrangement of the  Chapters which Sir John Maud thought was more  sensible.  Various delegates, and the Director-General in  his Report, had suggested that a phrase which  might sum up the whole work of Unesco was  “to help the world to become more civilized.”  As Sir John saw it, the programme presented for  the Commission's discussion showed, under seven  headings, the seven ways in which Unesco dared  to think that it could help to make the world  more civilized : firstly, by helping to reconstruct  those parts of the world devastated by the war  (therefore Reconstruction was the first Chapter);  secondly, by helping the world to become better  educated (the second Chapter was, therefore,  Education). The three succeeding Chapters dealt  with the “SS” of Unesco (natural sciences, social  sciences and humanistic studies). For the “C”  of Unesco, Chapter 6 was called “Cultural Acti-  vities , and dealt with the part of Unesco’s pro-  gramme which aimed at helping the world to  become a place in which the creative spirit of  man could work more freely, and the works of  that creative spirit could be more widely and gen-  erally enjoyed.
