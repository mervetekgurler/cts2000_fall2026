SIXTH MEETING  Tuesday, 6 June 1950, at 4.20 p.m.  Chairman:  Draft resolution concerning the Status of  Associate Memberships of Unesco [document
5C/20].  [1] In presenting the United Kingdom draft  resolution contained in document 5C/20,  Mr. WARD (United Kingdom) recalled the  history of the proposal which had originated in  the generous impulses of the United States delega-  tion at Beirut in 1948. At that time a resolution  on similar lines had been moved but had met  with opposition on many sides. He regretted that  the United Kingdom delegation had been among  those opposing the motion, which had subsequent-  ly been withdrawn. Study of the problem had  since led the Government of the United Kingdom  to the conclusion that associate membership  would be one effective means of associating non-  self-governing territories more directly with the  work of Unesco.  [2] He thought that there was substantial  agreement on the end in view. If, however, the  United Kingdom draft resolution, moved as a  means to that end, raised difficulties of procedure,  his delegation would welcome any alternative  suggestion which would contribute to the removal  of that difficulty. His delegation was well aware  that, even if the draft resolution were approved  in Plenary Session, no immediate steps could be  taken, since the Director-General would first have  to study the matter. It would be for the Sixth  General Conference to decide on the merits of the  proposal. However, any proposal which would  lead the Director-General to study the problem of  non-self-governing territories would receive United  Kingdom support.  [3] Mr. SEBASTIAN (Philippines), while agree-  ing with the aims of the United Kingdom proposal,  voiced some doubts as to the means proposed for  attaining them. He did not intend to oppose the  draft resolution; he merely hoped that the pro-  cedural difficulties would be noted by the  Director-General and the Executive Board when  they made the necessary adjustments to the Con-  stitution.  [4] He failed to see how the governments of  ad  Professor Jako NIerLsen (Denmark)  non-self-governing territories could consent to  participate in the manner suggested in the draft  resolution. They were to be given the right to  voice their opinions and to contribute financially  to the funds of the Organization without having  the right to take part in decisions on the use of  such funds. He hoped that non-self-governing  territories would be admitted either as observers  or as full members.  [5] Moreover, the United Kingdom draft resolu-  tion stated that the Director-General (or the Ex-  ecutive Board) should consult  with Member States responsible for territories  not responsible for the conduct of their own inter-  national relations, to formulate proposals for any  necessary amendments to the Constitution.  What was the procedure contemplated for sub-  mitting amendments to the proposals made by the  Director-General (or the Executive Board)?  [6] HAMED ZAKI Bey (Egypt) considered that  the issue was really one of procedure: it was a  question of amending Article II of Unesco’s Con-  stitution, but Article II made no mention of non-  self-governing territories. This statement in no  way implied that the Egyptian delegation was  opposed to the admission of such territories. On  the contrary, it wished to champion freedom, and  believed that, at least in the cultural field, such  areas should participate with full rights in United  Nations activities.  If Article II of the Constitution were to be  amended, it would clearly be necessary to have  recourse to the procedure set out in Article XIII.  In view of the procedure established in that  Article, however, it seemed difficult to reach any  solution.  [7] Mr. WAHAB (France) thought that common  ground might be found by asking the Director-  General to consult with those Member States that  were responsible for the administration and  foreign affairs of non-self-governing territories,  and to forward the results of their enquiries to all  the Member States. He therefore submitted the  following draft resolution:     ;  |  |  |  |  |                        THE GENERAL CONFERENCE,  ConsiDERING the Resolution whereby the Third General  Conference of Unesco requested Member States re-  sponsible for the administration of non-self-govern-  ing territories to ensure the co-operation of such ter-  ritories in the work of Unesco,  InsTRUCTS the Director-General:  To invite Member States responsible in one form or  another for the development and conduct of the affairs  of certain States or territories, to indicate in their  annual reports the points on which they have achieved  co-operation by such States or terrilories in the work  of Unesco;  To invite the same Member States, after consulting the  States or territories concerned, to send him any sug-  gestions on the forms which such co-operation might  take, including those which might involve amend-  ment of the Constitution;  To communicate to all Member States the  results of this enquiry by a date which will enable  the Sixth General Conference of Unesco to discuss any  amendment that may have been proposed in this  respect.  [8] Mr. SABA (Legal Adviser) wished to draw a  distinction between the two kinds of problems at  issue:  In the first place, the Committee had to solve  a question of substance: To what extent could  non-self-governing territories be associated in  Unesco’s work? How far could they have the  same rights and duties as self-governing States?  In the second place, a problem of constitutional  procedure had to be settled.  The General Committee had asked for legal  advice on these two points. In doing so, it had  had regard to the provision of Article XIII, 2, of  Unesco’s Constitution, concerning the procedure  to be followed in considering proposals for  amendments to the Constitution, and stating that  the General Conference shall have power to  adopt by a two-thirds majority rules of procedure for  carrying out the provisions of this Article.  Unfortunately, the special rule to regulate this  procedure for amendments did not yet exist. A  problem of constitutional procedure therefore  arose.  [9] According to Article XIII, the draft texts of  proposed amendments must be communicated by  the Director-General to Member States at least six  months in advance of their consideration by the  General Conference. It was not stated in that  Article who was empowered to draft such amend-  ments. If the procedure hitherto adopted as re-  cards amendments to the Constitution were ex-  amined, it would be found that the General Con-  ference had adopted the “first reading” of the pro-  posed amendment, and then instructed the  Director-General to transmit it to Member States,
673  so that it could be submitted to the following  Conference for adoption. But the delicate ques-  tion of procedure in the case of an amendment to  an amendment had not so far arisen.  [10] In the absence of such rules, Mr. Saba had  to lay a request before the Committee. If the  Committee decided to instruct the Executive  Board or the Director-General to formulate a draft  amendment, it was essential that the instructions  should be most precise and that the procedure  to be followed be laid down in specific terms.  [11] The CHAIRMAN thought there were two  distinct tasks involved, to provide for an im-  plementation such as would avoid undue delay to  Article XIII, 2 of the Constitution, and to give an  opinion on the produce to be followed.  He did not see any wide divergence between the  French resolution and that of the United King-  dom. He therefore suggested that they be com-  bined; the way would then be clear for sugges-  ing a procedure for implementing Article XII,
9 of the Constitution in a manner which would  allow the Sixth General Conference to take a  decision. As Mr. Saba had indicated that the  Committee should not make a precise proposal  regarding the implementation of that Article, he  suggested that the preparation of a draft rule of  procedure for submitting amendments to the Con-  stitution should be left to the Director-General  and the Secretariat. The Committee could, how-  ever, give useful suggestions to guide them in  their work.  [12] Dr. WALKER (Australia) supported the  Chairman’s suggestion and expressed the hope that  the United Kingdom and France would be able to  come to an agreement.  In raising the matter of Article XIII, 2, in the  Sub-Committee, he had had in mind not only the  question of amendments to the Constitution, but  also the doubful soundness of the practice of intro-  ducing an amendment to the Constitution and  discussing it in principle at the same Conference  without observing the statutory six months  notice.  [13] During the six months which should  precede the discussion of principle, valuable pre-  liminary discussions and study of technical prob-  lems could take place. Delegations also had time  to get full instructions from their governments  which they could not procure in the course of a  single conference. If six months’ notice were not  given, many delegations would be compelled to  abstain from the vote, regardless of their convic-  tions on the merits of the proposed amendment.  Moreover, the delegation proposing the amend-  ment should be responsible for draîting the text,        and not the Director-General, whose part it would  be to provide technical assistance to the delega-  tion concerned and to arrange for consultations.  [14] He suggested the following procedure:  The delegation submitting an amendment to the  Constitution should give notice of its intention  and explain the substance of the amendment at  one Conference; the Director-General should then  be authorized to arrange consultations and to pro-  vide assistance to the delegation sponsoring the  amendment (a procedural decision not requiring  a two-thirds majority), and the following General  Conference should take the final decision. That  procedure would give delegates who were ready  to express an opinion the opportunity to do so at  the time when notice was given.  [15] The CHAIRMAN explained that he had  mentioned the matter of amendments in order to  show that the provisions of the Constitution were  not sufficiently detailed.
116] Mrs. BRUNAUER (United States) wondered  whether the French resolution was designed to  make 1t possible for the Sixth General Conference  to adopt the solution of associate membership by  a two-thirds majority if it so desired. If so, she  thought that procedure in order. Everything  should be done to prevent delay in the adoption  of a solution.  However, she reminded the meeting that, when  the Constitution was drafted, it had been intended  that important amendments should be brought up  at one Conference and adopted at the next. The  more flexible wording had been included because  a large number of minor adjustments had been  expected.  [17] The CHAIRMAN emphasized the task of the  Committee, which was to give advice on the way  in which a final decision could be prepared. He  suggested that the problem of preparing rules of  procedure for submitting amendments to the  Constitution gave place to the more immediate  task of embodying the opinion of the meeting re-  garding procedure to be applied in the considera-  tion of document 5C/20 in a definite form.  [18] Mr. OLLENNU (United Kingdom) agreed  that, as a matter of general principle, the existing  rules might prove adequate for the treatment of  amendments of lesser importance. Dr. Walker's  suggestion would be preferable for dealing with  very far-reaching amendments. He understood  that, when six months’ notice had been given, an  amendment would be put on the agenda of the  Conference in the normal way. Sub-amendments  would then be dealt with in the normal course of  Conference procedure. As to the problem of  associate membership, the misgivings which had  been voiced were, in his opinion, details which  could be dealt with by the General Conference.  Since the matter had been raised at Beirut in 1948,  he felt that Member States might be ready to deal  with it under a procedure involving as short a  lapse of time as possible.  Mr. Ollennu thought that any slight differences  between the United Kingdom and French delega-  tions in the matter of a text for submission to the  General Conference could be overcome. The dif-  ficulty arose as regards discussion of the report  before an amendment to the Constitution was pro-  posed, but he felt that agreement was possible on  this point.  [19] Mr. WAHAB (France) pointed out that the  French delegation’s draft resolution did not  broach the basic question, which would have to  be settled by a future Conference. The resolution  merely asked Member States to set an enquiry on  foot, so as to obtain suggestions which might  serve as a basis for precise proposals, and to sug-  gest such procedural methods as seemed indicated  in this respect.  [20] On the CHAIRMAN'’s assurance that the  question of reports would not prejudice the pos-  sibility of associate membership being discussed  at the Sixth General Conference, Mr. OLLENNU  (United Kingdom) withdrew the United King-  dom draft resolution in favour of the French pro-  posal.  [21] Mr. SABA (Legal Adviser) asked that cer-  tain points be clarified. Firstly, was the object  of this resolution to instruct the Director-General  to ask Member States, six months before the Sixth  Session of the General Conference, to forward to  him all the suggestions which he might consider  it proper to submit, in the form of an amendment  or in some other form, on the question of the  participation of non-self-governing territories in  Unesco's work? Secondly, did this projected  enquiry concern only those Member States that  were responsible for the administration of the  territories of other States?  [22] Mr. WAHAB (France) replied to these two  questions in the affirmative.  The draft resolution proposed by the French  delegation was adopted. Amendment proposed by the New Zealand delegation  to Rules of Procedure 11 and 14.  PARAGRAPH (d).  [1] At the suggestion of Mr. COWELL (United  Kingdom), it was agreed to insert the phrase “in  accordance with Rule 36 (3)” at the end of the  first sentence of paragraph (d) of the amendment.  [2] Mr. COWELL (United Kingdom) further  proposed changing the last sentence of paragraph  (d) to the effect that no new item might be con-  sidered until seven days had elapsed after it was  placed on the agenda, unless the General Confer-  ence decided otherwise by a two-thirds majority  of the members present and voting.  [3] Mrs. BRUNAUER (United States of America)  pointed out that such a change would mean that  a two-thirds majority could demand immediate  consideration of a new item, whereas the original  text presented by the New Zealand delegation re-  quired the unanimous consent of all members. If  new items were of major importance, it was pre-  ferable to require unanimous consent.  [4] Mr. MURTINHO (Brazil) remarked that in  the original amendment, the questions to be in-  cluded in the agenda were qualified as “impor-  tant”. It was therefore essential that they be  considered, and Mr. Murtinho was consequently  in favour of the adoption of the United Nations  formula permitting delegations to ask their Gov-  ernments for instructions. He also supported the  Professor Jakos NieLsen (Denmark)  suggestion made by Mr. Cowell, which was  designed to enable such discussions to be ad-  journed.  The CHAIRMAN having seconded Mrs. Brun-  auer’s point of view, Mr. COWELL (United  Kingdom) withdrew his suggestion.  PARAGRAPH (9).  [6] The CHAIRMAN felt that paragraph (g) of  the amendment might be omitted as, according  to Rule of Procedure 48, the sections under con-  sideration applied to “commissions, committees  and subsidiary bodies of the Conference’. He  proposed substituting this wording for that pro-  posed in the New Zealand amendment.  [6] Dr. BEAGLEHOLE (New Zealand) thought  the inclusion of the sub-committees, which were  of a more informal character, was inadvisable, as  they should not be governed by elaborate pro-  cedure. A generic term should be used, or else all  bodies enumerated.  Mr. de VISSCHER (Belgium) agreed with  Dr. Beaglehole. As the enumeration in the pre-  sent case was not long, he supported the New  Zealand amendment and moved its adoption.  [7] Mr. SEBASTIAN (Philippines) proposed the  wording: “commissions, committees and sub-  sidiary bodies of the Conference’.  Mr. JAUME (France) pointed out that, in  sections XII, XIII and XIV, and especially in  Rules 61, 65 and 69, this was in fact the formula  used. He therefore proposed, in order to preserve  uniform terminology, that Mr. Sebastian's pro-  posal be adopted.  The proposal of the delegate for the Philippines  was adopted.
