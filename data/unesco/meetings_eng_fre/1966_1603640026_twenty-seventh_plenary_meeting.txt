3.1 Mr. KIRPAL (India): .  Mr. President and fellow delegates, in opening the debate on Item 11 of the Agenda  concerning Unesco' 8 contribution to the promotion of the aims and objectives of the United  Nations Development Decade, 1 am fully conscious of the supreme importance of this sub-  ject, not only for Unesco but for all the bodies of the United Nations, and indeed for all  Member States whether they are materially poor or affluent. Development, like peace, is  the concern of all mankind and it must be a process in which all participate as a single  family of mankind, without any division or even any basic distinction between the rich and  the poor, the developed and the developing, the so-called "haves" and "have-nots" -terms  of usage which, indeed, should disappear from our terminology. The concept of develop-  ment should be regarded in this way only and this should be the starting point of the great-  est venture in history, the task of development in which humanity is now engaged with  increasing awareness of its importance and urgency.
3.2 Development and peace are the two faces of Unesco and without the one the other is  meaningless, and the two together must be pursued in the same spirit of realism and dedi-  cation. There can be no peace on earth without development and the task of development  to which the United Nations Decade is dedicated cannot proceed in discord and conflict.
3.3 When the Government of India requested the inclusion of the item now under discussion  in the Agenda of this session of the General Conference, it was moved by three considera-  tions apart from the importance and urgency of the matter itself. In the fivst place we  thought that in the middle of the Development Decade there should be an opportunity for re-  viewing the progress made in the fields of Unesco! s activities, an appraisal of the results  achieved in the context of the needs of the situation. Secondly, arising from such a review,  the Conference might consider the problems and difficulties encountered in the process of  development with a view to smoother and more effective operation. Thirdly, on the basis  of such a serious survey of the past we might look into the future and review our policies  and programmes for adequate action in the years to come.
3.4 I hope the discussion of this important item by the distinguished delegates will  strengthen Unesco both in terms of implementing projects and programmes and in genera-  ting that spirit of determination without which we shall not come anywhere near our goals.  For a review of the progress made during the first half of the Development Decade we are  indebted to the Director-General whose excellent report contained in document 14 C/10 is  packed with much substance and many ideas and presented in significant form. 1 must  thank the Director-General for this report. We know that he and his Secretariat are fully  aware of Unesco's responsibilities in this field and Mr. René Maheu personally is an in-  spired exponent of the challenge of development in both its material und its moral aspects.
3.5 From an examination of the Director-General! 8 report on the Development Decade,  two conclusions emerge very clearly. On the one hand, one is impressed by the improve-  ment of the methods and instruments of action, the perfecting of an organizational frame-  work within which Unesco can pursue its activities for the achievement of the five major  objectives set out in paragraph 58 of the report. On the other hand, one is depressed,
766   Sesiones plenortas / aen puse sacedanua XXVII  almost dismayed, by the inadequacy of the results achieved so far, by the widening of the  gap between the standards attained by the affluent countries and the situation existing in the  poor countries, in the fields of education, science and communication. This is borne out  by the rate of progress indicated in all fields. As an example, I shall refer briefly to  the field of literacy. Between 1950 and 1960 the number of adult illiterates in developing  countries increased from 425 million to 445 million, and in spite of impressive increases  in school enrolment at the primary stage, the number of illiterate adults has continued to  Increase during the Development Decade to reach a figure of 460 million, which represents  almost 60% of the population available for productive work in the developing countries.
3.6 If the programme for eradicating illiteracy continues on the same scale as in the first  half of the Decade, supported by increased primary enrolment ratios, the number of adult  illiterates at the end of the Development Decade in 1970 will amount to 440 million, or  about 15 million more than the corresponding total for 1950. Document 14 C/10 informsus  that the total eradication of illiteracy would be possible by 1980 if present efforts, as from
1666, were quadrupled.
3.7 Here, Mr. President and fellow delegates, lies the chullenge of development, and what  is true of the problem of eradicating illiteracy, applies in the same measure to the other  four priority areas, namely educational planning, improvement of educationaladministration  and analysis of the costing and financing of education; teacher training, adaptation of  curricula and new methods and techniques; implantation of science and its application to  development; and the development of communication services and social sciences to support  thesc high priority programmes.
3.8 By tha quadrupling of efforts and resources during the next five years in all fields of  developmerit we shall be-able to narrow the gap appreciably. Otherwise, at the same rate  of development, the gap will continue to widen, dividing humanity into two groups, with all  the tragic potentialities of such a division at a time in history when, morally and technolo-  gically, the whole of mankind must be a single, harmonious family, enjoying together the  fruits of that wonderful knowledge which can make our times the most glorious and signifi-  cant chapter of human history.
3.8 The situation becomes all the more poignant when, again taking the eradication of illit-  eracy as an example, we examine the methods and means of action. We are now ready for  the planning and formulation of literacy programmes integrated with overall social and  economic development, and for the implementation of work-oriented literacy projects  geared to the development of the agricultural and industrial sectors. The experimental  programme has made a good start and the method of functional literacy is well developed  and understood. All that fe wanting is the availability of resources. Asa great English  poet of the present day has said: " Between the idea and the reality ... falls the Shadow" .  Indeed the shadow between the plan and its implementation deepens, casting a sombre spell  on the condition of mankind. With increased effort, which is not at all utopian, we cancast  off this dark shadow. The quadrupling of effort in the field of literacy, as in other fields  of education, science and communication, is entirely within our capacity. Indeed, it would  represent only a very small fraction of the present-day outlay on armaments or even on  such objectives as might be considered quite inessential.
3.10 Coming to the difficulties encountered in the first half of the Development Decade, we  should like to see greater co-ordination between Unesco, the United Nations Development  Programme and other United Nations Agencies in the implementation of activities within  Unesco! s field, with Unesco having greater authority and scope for swifter action. This  would reduce the delays which now take place and introduce that essential element of flexi-  bility without which we can neither achieve the necessary measures of efficiency nor meet  the requirements of urgency. We would also like to see less bureaucratic procedure and  more programme involvement on the part of those responsible for selecting operational  projects. The process of aid-giving should not be a long-drawn-out procedural drill; what  is required is swift action by those who can appreciate the urgency of the situation.

3.11 It is also necessary to improve our methods of finding the best know-how for develop-  ment and ensuring that it will be effective in operation. This requires careful selection of  experts, to ensure that they have not only the necessary specialized knowledge and experi-  ence in their field but also that degree of motivation and attunement without which their  work cannot make the desired impact. We hope that greater efficiency and flexibility can  be achieved in the choice of the right experts and their full functioning withina given cultural  and administrative situation. The facilities for training for concrete and urgent tasks in a  developing society and for increasing training facilities at the national level also need to be  examined and improved upon.
3.12 At the national level the relevance of education, science and communication for overall  economic and social development is now fully admitted, fairly well understood but not yet  translated into the necessary action in terms of priorities and programmes and the impor-  tance of the authorities entrusted with these responsibilities. National plans for education  are often retarded by the inadequacy of implementation at the local level. These difficulties  need to be sorted out by the developing countries themselves, with guidance and stimulus  from the world Organization.
3.13 Looking to the future, the task is clear but not easy, and we have to do everything  possible to step up the level and accelerate the pace of development, and forthis, Mr. Presi-  dent, the key is the material resources available. We hope that in the years to come,  from this point in the middle of the Decade, we can move forward together, developing  countries and developed countries, and concentrate our efforts in erucial areas and priovi-  ties which are now well-defined. In this task of development we fully realize that action at  the national level is crucial. Material resources and technical know-how from outside will  only yield the desired results if there is great determination and the necessary organization  at the national level. We feel that the time has now come to join together both these ele-  ments. The needs at the national level are great and keenly felt. If the resources for these  needs are available, to the extent which is required, it will be possible for very substantial  results to be achieved by the end of the Development Decade.
3.14 Finally, I would like to refer to the problem, not only of the resources for develop-  ment, but of the need for humanizing development. And o° this we have heard a great deal  from our Director-General in his speeches here, and from many of the delegates who spoke  on the problem of peace and the general aims and objectives of Unesco. We hope that in  the pursuit of development, all countries will be mindful of the quality of living, and it is  in this connexion that we have presented in the Programme Commission - and the matter  is under discussion - a project for humanizing development, for reflection on the standards  of living for the developing countries, on the problem of aims and goals and not merely of  material means and material results.
3.15 Mr. President, we have, along with 51 other countries, presented a draft resolution,  document 14 C/DR.119, for the consideration of the General Conference. You will see from  this text, which I am afraid delegates will not have had time to study carefully because it  has just been distributed, that the first few paragraphs refer to considerations and factors  which have been well stated and well discussed elsewhere. Since the problem of develop-  ment is an overall problem, and Unesco! 8 action in the fields of education science and  communication is only a part of the larger programme, we thought it necessary to state  these considerations. But the Conference need not spend too much time on the discussion  of some of them, which lie outside the strict fields of Unesco. The operative part of our  resolution comes at the end. There are three paragraphs addressed to Member Statés and  then the last féw paragraphs invite the Director-General to do certain things. I shall not  take up your time by reading the text, but I should like to say that we shall be very glad to  offer any explanation of the terms of this resolution if such explanations are required during  the course of the debate.
3.16 Mr. President, thc delegation of India would also whole-heartedly support the draft  resolution in document 14 C/DR.113 Rev., submitted by Algeria, the Central African Re-  public, Chad, Madagascar, Niger, Rwanda and Senegal. This refers to a problem to which

8.8 Ce n'est plus à l'Unesco qu'il faut désormais faire remarquer que l'aide et la coopé-  ration internationales ne sont que l'expression de la prise de conscience des problèmes  que pose l'existence d'un Tiers Monde défavorisé, En dehors des impératifs économiques  et financiers, comme la nécessité d'équilibrer les échanges commerciaux par l'établisse-  ment d'une parité équitable des prix des matières premières et des prix des produits ma-  nufacturés, ce n'est plus ici, & l'Unesco, que nous avons à soutenir qu'une règle nouvelle  de la vie internationale nous eat désormais imposée, qui est celle de la solidarité oblige-  toire entre tous lea peuples : solidarité des hommes, certes, mais aussi et surtout, soli-  darité des problèmes, Nous savons que nous ne sommes pas du tout en avance sur l'Unesco  quand nous affirmons la nécessité de dégager la notion d'aide de ses ambiguftés, L'aide  n'est pas une forme de charité, même généreuse, L'aide n'est pas, non plus, l'octroi de  subsides gratuits et permanents, puisque, d'une part, elle repose sur la conviotion sincè-  rement acceptée que la fin de la raisdre chez les uns conditionne la prospérité des autres  et assure, en dernière analyse, la paix de tous, et que, d'autre part, comme l'a souligné  le Directeur général dans sa réponse aux délégations qui étaient intervenues dans le débat  général, elle est fondée sur les efforts incessants et acharnés que nous autres, pays ac-  tuellement pauvres, devons déployer pour accélérer notre développement. L'aide est ainsi  un moyen d'assurer la période de transition - tant il est vrai, Monsieur le Président, que  la finalité de l'aide, après tout, c'est la fin de l'aide,
5,9 Ces principes, affirmés et réaffirmés par les assises de notre Organisation, et pro-  clamés par son Acte constitutif, nous assurent de la légitimité, à la fois morale et juridique,  de notre démarche,
5,10 Quel est l'objet et quelle est la portée du Projet 14 C/DR,113 Rev., qui est soumis  à la Conférence générale ?
5,11 Il est question, en tout premier lieu, d'agir dans le prolongement logique de tous les  objectifs que se sont assignés, par leurs résolutions et leurs projets, l'Assemblée générale  des Nations Unies, le Conseil économique et social, la Conférence des Nations Unies sur  le commerce et le développement et, bien entendu, les différentes assises de notre Orga-  nisation, Deux idées-forces se dégagent irrésisiiblement de ces activités : d'abord, la hâte,  pour ne pas dire l'impatience fiévreuse, d'attcindre "les objectifs d'ensemble assignés à  la Décennie des Nations Unies pour le développement", "d'accélérer le progrès vers la  croissance auto-entretenue de l'économie des divers nays et leur progrès social”, et de  faire porter "l'effort majeur [de l'Unesco/ essentiellement Sur les programmes à long  terme" ; ensuite, et parallèlement À cette impatience, une conscience aiguë des obstacles  qui s'opposent aux efforts déployés, du fait que "l'écart entre les niveaux de vie des pays  développés et des pays en voie de développernent s'est accentué au lieu de diminuer et qu'un  ensemble d'indicateurs économiques montre la lenteur des progrès accomplis vers les ob-  jectifs fixés pour la Décennie",
5.12 Dès lors, Monsieur le Président, notre projet de résolution s'inscrit dans une triple  perspective : il se veut, avant tout, une modeste contribution à une meilleure organisation  de l'aide internationale et à son utilisation avec le maximum d'efficacité ; il vise, ensuite,  à faire une simple conatatation, qui est la suivante : tels qu'ils fonctionnent aujourd'hui,  les mécanismes d'attribution de l'aide obéissent, d'une manière générale, à des critères  habituels de rentabilité bancaire qui, nous semble-t-il, ne sont pas applicables à des situa-  tions précisément inhabituelles ; il se propose, enfin - et c'est son objet essentiel - d'in-  sister sur l'idée qu'il faudra procéder à un aménagement des contreparties nationales, en  en assouplissant les délais de palement,
5.13 Que recouvre exactement cette notion d'assouplissement ? Il s'agit - je dirai, tout  naturellement - de prendre en considération la durée, qui est une dimension essentielle  de toute stratégie de l'aide. Intégrer la durée, c'est mesurer clairement le temps néces-  saire aux différentes étapes du développement, C'est aussi harmoniser la nature, lé volume ’  et la durée de paiement des contreparties exigées des Etats bénéficiaires de l'aide, avec  les possibilités immédiates de ces Etats,
5,14 Dans la majorité des cas, en effet, un projet d'aide appelle, de la part de l'Etat bé-  néficiatre, l'engagement de prendre en charge, pendant la durée de ce projet, des dépenses,  notamment de fonctionnement, relatives par exemple À la rémunération des experts, à l'uti-  lisation et à l'amortissement du matériel, etc, Le règlement, en espèces, de ces dépenses  constitue parfois pour noua, pays en voie de développement, une charge trop lourde, et  nous accule à un dilemme dramatique : ou bien nous renonçons à l'exécution d'un projet,  malgré une rentabilité certaine, prévisible à moyen et à long terme, ou blen nous devons  sacrifier une partie importante du budget national pour assurer l'exécution de ce projet,
5,15 Nous préconisons donc que l'on opère une distinction dans la catégorie des dépenses  dites de contrepartie et que l'on admette que des apports en nature, en investissements-  travail puissent être fournis par les pays en voie de développement pour régler ces dépenses,
5,16 Le deuxième aspect de cette notion d'assouplissement résulte de la reconnaissance  de Ja situation spécifique des pays en voie de développement, C'est la raison pour laquelle  une voie nouvelle doit être trouvée pour assurer leur "'décollage''. Quelles sont les inci-  dences de cette conception sur le problème qui nous préoccupe ?
5.17 Il ne nous semble pas téméraire d'affirmer, Monsieur le Président, que l'expansion  auto-entretenue d'un pays sous-développé ne peut être réalisée qu'à partir d'un certain  stade de développement, En conséquence, il n'est pas difficile de faire admettre que, dans  les premières années de l'exécution d'un projet, un pays sous-équipé en hommes et sous-  équipé économiquement ne peut fournir une contribution financière importante, Dès lors,  il nous semble que la conclusion suivante s'impose : au début de l'exécution d'un projet,  il est, à la limite, cruel d'exiger d'un Etat bénéficiaire d'une aide une contribution finan-  cière qui excède ses possibilités, Le versement d'une telle contribution n'est possible et  ne peut devenir obligatoire qu'à l'issue de la phase de démarrage de l'opération, au mo-  ment où l'on espère que celle-ci produira ses premiers fruits et se traduira concrètement  par une augmentation des ressources nationales,
5,18 Avant de terminer, je voudrais dissiper, s'il en était encore besoin, l'équivoque pos-  sible que pourrait suggérer notre projet, Il ne s'agit nullement de transformer l'aide inter-  nationale en subsides gratuits et permanents, J'espère avoir prouvé le contraire en essayant  de cerner les ambiguftés de la notion d'aide, en insistant sur le fait qu'elle ne peut et ne  doit être que transitoire. J'ajouterai que le projet de résolution 14 C/DR, 188, présenté  par l'Inde et dont la délégation malgache est cosignataire, rejoint exactement nos préoccu-  pations, Les deux projets - le 14 C/DR, 113 Rev, et le 14 C/DR,199 - sont complémen-  taires et nous apportons au second tout notre appui.

16.1 Mr. FRANKEL (United States of America):  Mr. President and distinguished delegates, we are discussing this afternoon a subject  of central importance not only to the great theme of peace, but to what future historians  will undoubtedly call the meaning and destiny of the Twentieth century. No subject better  captures the energies and the promises of this century than the theme of development. It  is not a theme of interest only to the underdeveloped countries, it is a theme of interest to  all countries rich and poor. It {8 a matter of concern to the rich countries because it is in  their natural interest, and in their national interest, that peace be sustained in the world.  It is a theme of concern to the developed countries because, over the long run, their pros-  perity and their own self-respect depend on the degree to which they can work with their  fellows in other countries to advance the great cause of human development.
15.2 My government supports the concept of development for all countries and it strongly  supports the objectives and the programme of the United Nations Development Decade. I  believe that not only in words but through deads our support for these objectives is clearly  fn the record. I would also add that in my own country's assistance programmes we follow

So you will see, I hope, that the remarks that Iam about  to make do not have to do with ends and purposes but with finding the most effective ways  and means to achieve these purposes. And with your permission Mr. President, I should  like to make certain general remarks by way of background before commenting specifically  on the draft resolutions before us.
15.3 ‘To begin with, I think everyone can agree that the concept of the Development Decade  has been useful in a number of ways. In the first place, it has provided a moral focus for  the international comity of nations. It has assisted in the stimulation of progress and in  clearer definition of goals. It has served to bring about a recognition of collective inter-  national responsibility among those countries which provide resources of funds and trained  manpower and the countries whichutilize these resources. Ithas led toa better co-ordination  of United Nations programmes. Finally, I believe the Development Decade has achieved  real progress in providing an impetus and climate for acceleration of development.
15.4 Against that background, it is worth nsking what the present and the future immedi-  ately ahead of us are. Developed and developing nations alike, I believe, have realized  that progress is now within the reach of all. We have learned better to weigh world needs  and better to plan ahead. The flow of financial resources for the support of development  has steadily increased. In 1060 the total flow of development assistance amounted to seven  thousand, seven hundred million dollars. The corresponding figure for 1954 is nine thou-  sand, nine hundred million dollars. In 1065 it rose to eleven thousand million dollars.  What should we expect for the balance of the Decade? I believe we can look forward to a  continued, but probably not spectacular, increase in this assistance.
15.5 Now in this connexion, I think it i8 important to recognize that if we look at develop-  ment as an economic, a social, a scientific problem there are built-indimitations to the  rate of development. The net flow of assistance to the developing countries must, of course,  continue to increase. Moreover, no one can argue that enough - or even nearly enough -  is now being done. Yet there are two essential limitations to what canbe done within limited  periods of time.
15.6 First of all, we must alwaya ask what provisions have been made by the intended  recipient countries to utilize the flow of resources to them, without disrupting their own  economies. Comprehensive and effective measures to assure economic stabilityare always  esgential, otherwise the additional in-put of resources will be nullified by concurrent un-  controllable inflation. This is one limitation.
15.7 A second, and I think an even more serious limitation, is that sufficient technical and  human competence must be readily available to utilize an increased flow of funds. As one  carefully reviews technical assistance activities, one recognizes that a limiting factor in  educational and scientific programmes has been a lack of teachers, scientists and engineers  to carry out many of the projects. These limitations do not mean that development is a  romantic ideal. They do not mean that we cannot push ahead, and push ahead faster. But  they do mean that development is not merely a problem of wanting development, it is a  problem of balanced growth in many different sectors of society; andthere is a time factor -  principally the time factor involved in the training of human beings - always present in it.
15.8 Now, against this background, let me turn briefly to the resolutions before us: draft  resolutions 113 and 199. We have just received draft resolution 199, and must therefore  reserve a final judgement until we have had an opportunity to give it the detailed considera-  tion it deserves. But on the basis of our first reading, my delegation does appreciate the  spirit and aims of this resolution. However, both these resolutions in their present form  scem to us doubtfully appropriate in this Unesco forum. Whether the present requirements  of the programme of the United Nations Development Decade-other than that of counterpart  ‘180   Nesiones plenarias / renapuue 3ace0anns XXVII  contributions - are too restrictive, is a question which should be discussed in the Govern-  ing Council of the United Mations Development Decade. Furthermore, the question of how  much counterpart the recipient country must provide is also a problem which I do not  belleve can be considered seriously and carefully in this Conference. It is an Issue requir-  ing detailed economic analysis, no general formula applies to it, it must be studied country  by country and in terms of the specific conditions pertaining to each country. I can say,  however, that the requirement that a recipient country provide some funds or some facilities  or some personnel for each of its development projects, is a wise provision, in that it vre~  vents the country from coramitting itself excessively to a programme too great for it to  carry out. lFurthermore, this requirement provides a guarantee of the real interest of the  country, the genuine commitment of the country in carrying out the project.
15.9 Having said this much, however, having introduced these caveats, I still wish to make  it clear that my government supports the general spirit of draft resolution 198, a call for  the acceleration of the rate of development. Our principal point fs that we believe it inap-  propriate for Unesco or any other Specialized Agency to attempt to specify, in forums out-  side the United Nations Development Decade Programme itself, what the nature and extent  of the United Nations Development Decade should be. We believe that proposals of the  nature of those contained in draft resolution 113 and draft resolution 119 are properly  brought up in the Governing Council of the United Nations Development Decade.
15.10 To be sure, a general discussion here has value, an exchange of views anda sharing  of experiences will educate us all and will be helpful to our brother organizations in the  United Nations family. Indeed the debate here helps remind us of the several calls already  made on behalf of development, including the basic resolution which established the evel -  opment Decade and, in addition, more recent proclamations in UNTAB and other United  Nations bodies. Further calls, however, at this point, seem to me to be gilding the lily.  We must distinguish between the usefulness of discussion and the appropriateness of a  resolution. “he United States delegation believes that the proper place to call formally for  any reconsideration of the United Nations Development Decade objectives, scope, size,  criteria or terms is in the United Nations Development Decade forum itself,
15.11 Mr. President, in taking this position, we believe that we are taking a position that  is not only helpful to the proper co-ordination of development planning within the United  Nations family, but is also helpful to the growth, stability and the reputation of Unesco it-  self. It is tempting when men and women are strongly in favour of something, as we all  are in the case of development, to pass resolutions. It is tempting to imagine that in pas-  sing resolutions one has accomplished something, but there is always a danger in passing  resolutions when actions are far from what the resolutions call for. There is always a  danger in passing resolutions that lie beyond the authority or the power of the body that  passes these resolutions. When this becomes a habit the authority, the seriousness, the  sophistication of the organization that passes such resolutions can be called into question.  And when such questions are raised, the faith of others in this organization declines, the  faith of its own workers is likely to decline. For this reason we would urge seriously,  while this discussion is useful and desirable and necessary, that this body abstain from  passing resolutions on subjects for which other forums in the United Nations are more  appropriate.
