Benner (Netherlands) Question of the Annual Budget Ceiling [1] Mr. BRULL (Cuba) asked whether it would not be advisable to study some of the problems raised by the Director-General. Those problems were of fundamental importance, especially that relating to the Budget figure, and the question whether or not there was to be a budget ceiling. The question could not be side-stepped. Without knowing the Budget figure, it was impossible, in considering the detailed items, to know whether they could be adjusted to the total sumavailable. Mr. Brull re-emphasized the need for taking up the fundamental problem of the budget ceiling. [2] The CHAIRMAN pointed out that he had already raised the question whether or not the Budget Committee should discuss the organizational principles outlined in the Director-General’s report, and that it had been decided to limit discussion, for the time being, to a general survey of the Budget Estimates. In any case, the Budget Committee had no authority to fix the annual budget ceiling of the Organization. According to the Constitution, that could be voted only by a plenary meeting of the General Conference. [3] Mr. BRULL (Cuba) expressed the view that the Committee could at least recommend a total figure. He thought the Committee was competent to suggest such a figure, on the basis of an analysis of the various items in the Budget. It was difficult to pronounce in favour of certain items, or to suggest new ones, in the absence of any idea of the total sum available. [4] Mr. EL DIWANY (Egypt) drew attention to document 6C/BUR/1, page 9, where it was said that: “The General Conference must receive the Committee's first report before taking a provisional decision as to the total figure of the Budget—i.e., not later than the afternoon of 20 June.” [6] Although the wording was not very explicit, it did provide an indication. While it was not stated that the Committee should discuss the figure, the Committee could, it seemed, take an interest in it. [6] The CHAIRMAN, referring to his previous experience in the Budget Committee, pointed out that its first task was to ensure that the estimates presented by the Secretariat were reasonable and accurate. After that, the plenary meeting of the General Conference would vote the budget ceiling for the year, and the Committee would then consider any reductions necessary to bring the total within that ceiling figure or to allow for the costing of new resolutions. [7] Mr. RAADI (Iran) was of the same opinion. While the Committee was not called upon to deal with the budget ceiling immediately, it would have occasion to express its opinions later on. The Con- ference would take no decision on the point until after the Committee had examined the figures; such an examination would be primarily a verification, and would not bind the Committee, which might subsequently have to reconsider the total appropria- tions. [8] The Committee agreed to the Chairman's ruling regarding the procedure for its discussion on the question of the annual budget ceiling. [12] Mr. TAYLOR (Deputy Director-General) felt that the suggestion to transfer certain Unesco activities to technical assistance funds was a matter of programme policy and outside the authority of the Budget Committee. The Committee could only recommend certain courses of action as being finan- cially desirable. [13] Sir Ronald ADAM (Representative of the Executive Board) pointed out that certain fixed rules governed the use of technical assistance funds. In each case the request for assistance had to come from the individual Member State, which undertook to match the aid thus given with a contribution from its own resources. The rigidity of the various rules governing the Technical Assistance Programme precluded the transfer to it of many Unesco activities. The Executive Board had already studied the problem and realized its difficulties. [14] Miss LAISE (United States of America) agreed with the remarks of the Deputy Director-General and the representative of the Executive Board. It was, indeed, a fact that technical assistance was an expansion of Unesco’s regular programme. It appeared from Dr. Elvin’s statement that, in addition | to the economic developmentcriteria, the other major, 4 point which seemed to determine whether a pro-,; gramme was carried out under the regular budget’ or technical assistance funds was the requirement, in the latter case, that the receiving government should provide funds for local costs. The opinion of the Indian delegate would be of interest, since his country had received both help from Unesco and technical assistance. [15] Mr. KIRPAL (India) said that his Government had requested technical assistance, but had not yet received any. Certain difficulties, however, had already been noted. Firstly, there was a predominant tendency to send visiting experts, whereas the provi- sion of badly needed equipment would be of more value to under-developed countries. Secondly, the per diem, office space, and other local expenses of a technical assistance expert were proving so high that many countries hesitated to seek that type of aid. [16] Mr. ELVIN (Director, Department of Educa- tion), replying to the CHAIRMAN, said that the complete breakdown of the amount of $286,100 requested for Assistance to Member States was being prepared and would be distributed very soon. [17] With regard to the transfer of Unesco Funda- mental Education activities to technical assistance funds, he pointed out that, although that solution мм тт тв VIII-3 was very tempting to Member States and Secretariat alike, there were certain difficulties. As Sir Ronald Adam had said, fixed rules existed for the use of technical assistance funds, and one of those was that such funds should contribute to the improvement of the basic economic conditions in the country concerned. Although better educational facilities did, in the long run, lead to the raising of economic standards, the problem was often far more cultural and intellectual than a question of basic economy. [18] In the second place, Unesco's share of the technical assistance funds was only 14 per cent, and it should be remembered that other Programme Departments, as well as education, had a right to share in that allocation. [19] Referring to a point made by Miss Laise, Mr. Elvin said that receiving countries did, in fact, contribute to local Unesco projects, but not under suchstrict rules as those enforced by the Technical Assistance Programme. [20] Mr. Elvin assured the Committee that very close liaison was maintained between Unesco and the Technical Assistance Programme in order to avoid overlapping and to determine which authority should be responsible for a particular project. He gave an example showing how a project started by Unesco could later be transferred to the Technical Assistance administration, if that seemed the more appropriate course. [21] Mr. Elvin stressed the fact that, whereas the programmes of Unesco and the Technical Assistance Board could be closely linked and co-ordinated, they were different in principle, and that, in particular, if Unesco's Fundamental Education activities were to be taken over by TAB their nature would immediately change. They would be administered as part of the problem of basic national economy, and not, as was Unesco's practice, from the point of view of educa- tional research and development. She sought infor- mation as to whether it had been possible to co- ordinate the salaries and allowances of Unesco experts employed under the regular programme and the Technical Assistance Programme. TAB had been unwilling to adopt the current Unesco rates, and Unesco was therefore trying to revise its system of fees and allowances to agree with those of TAB. That did not affect the Budget for 1952 and, in any case, it was a programme rather than a budget matter.
