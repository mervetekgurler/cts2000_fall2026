[1] Mr. BOURGEOIS (Switzerland), Rapporteur  of the special Sub-Committee, recalled the words of  Heine when, after a tiresome encounter with the  Customs officials, he exclaimed: “Thoughts are  duty-free!” Once thoughts were expressed in books  or printed documents of any kind, however, they  came up against not only Customs regulations,  which were a minor difficulty, but also endless  administrative and budgetary complications.  Educational, scientific and cultural documents  were the chief instruments of Unesco’s work for  the advancement of tolerance and truth. How  could it carry out that work if it met with  intolerable obstacles at every frontier?  [2] That was why, at Beirut, the General Confer-
457  ence had instructed Unesco to prepare the  necessary agreements for arranging for the free  circulation of such material. The Conference held  at Geneva the previous March had prepared a text  which, though not ideal, represented the greatest  measure of freedom that could be aimed at in  present conditions. It was obvious that, from the  cultural point of view, complete freedom would  have been desirable, and that books could not be  considered as merchandise. The Sub-Committee  considered that the cultural nature of books should  be all the more emphasized as, commercially, they  represented no more than 1 per cent or 2 per cent  of the total volume of trade. Unfortunately, it  must be admitted that certain material consider-  ations militated against the complete acceptance of  that essential character of books. In any case, it  did not seem that the Draft Agreement prepared by  the Geneva conference (which was attended  mostly by customs and finance experts) could give           rise to serious objections in the 25 countries which  had attended the Conference. The amendments  submitted to the Sub-Committee set up by the  Programme and Budget Commission related to  details of form and sprang from a desire to bring  out yet more clearly the fundamental idea on  which such an Agreement should be based.  [3] Some countries might think that the Draft  Agreement in its final form was too limited in  scope; but it was obvious that an international  agreement could only be achieved at the cost of  various sacrifices. Exemption from import duties  had been proposed exclusively for cultural objects  imported for non-profit-making purposes. On that  point the French representative had raised the  question of possible dumping. It was obvious that  such an Agreement could not justify dishonest  practices of that kind. The Canadian, Mexican  and Swiss delegations had thought that the pro-  visions of the Agreement might have been more  liberal, and that no restriction (customs, admin-  istrative of currency) should hinder the free  movement of books, manuscripts and documents  of an educational and scientific nature. The Sub-  Committee's view was that the results achieved  were the best that could be expected.  [4] The only important addition it had made to  the draft agreement in document 5C/PRG/11 had  been a Protocol, which provided that the United  States of America would have the option of ratify-  ing the Agreement or of accepting it with the  inclusion of a reservation, which formed part of  the Protocol.  The Sub-Committee had agreed to add the  following clause to the end of paragraph 2 of the  Protocol [document 5C/PRG/20, page 7]:  provided that no measure taken in virtue of this  reservation shall be of a discriminative nature.
