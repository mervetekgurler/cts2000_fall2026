Unesco can help give them this training. It can help to make the new generation of scientists international-minded. No doubt the grants that are given for international meetings will help to promote scientific discovery but, much more important from our point of view, they will also help to promote understanding between scientists of different nations, and that is precisely what Unesco has to do. NEEDHAM (Secretariat) : I may perhaps be allowed to answer the questions of the delegate of New Zealand in the inverse order. Firstly, with regard to the support of the unions, of which there are now about ten in all the principal branches of the Natural Sciences, these were the spontaneous creation of scientists themselves and first took form in 1919 at the end of World War I, when the International Research Council was formed. Since 1931 this has been known as the International Council of Scientific Unions, which is represented by an observer at this General Conference. The fundamental principle on which I would say that Unesco supports the activities of the international scientific unions and societies federated in the Inter- national Council, is that it supports thereby inter- national co-operation in science, and not scientific research itself, as has been said by the delegate of the United Kingdom. The pooling of information among scientists, leading to an improvement in the standard ofliving and the welfare of the peoples, is a process which could not go on if there were no international scientific unions; the international co-operation and fertilization of minds which result from interna- tional meetings is the kind of thing in which Unesco cannot but be interested. As regards the principles — the more precise principles about which the delegate of New Zealand asked — Unesco has not given, except in excep- tional circumstances, any grants for administrative expenses, because the international societies and unions all receive from their adhering national bodies very small contributions, which, nevertheless, are considered to be enough to provide for the ad- ministrative expenses of the international bodies. Secondly; Unesco has not given, and I think it will not suggest giving, funds for congresses of what we call the «free for all» category — that is to say, where every scientist who is qualified may go and make a contribution and give a paper. These — 457 —        congresses, for a hundred years past, have been the- undertaking of the host country and, if Unesco were to finance such a congress, probably no host country would ever again resist the temptation of suggesting that Unesco should aid it. Such congresses must be distinguished from symposia, meetings where you have only ten to twelve scientists particularly eminent in a particular field meeting together. Then it is legitimate to give transportation grants. Thirdly, another general principle is that Unesco has not given, nor would it give, per diem or subsis- tence allowances for men to attend such meetings. If, then,it is asked what are the main categories of help which have been given, they may be de- scribed as follows (I may say that all this may be found in document 2C/6) : Firstly, there is the transportation grant, which permits scientific men to attend selected interna- tional meetings. This is a very important feature, because formerly it was only possible for older men, perhaps retired with private means of their own, to attend these meetings. Now it means that the best men can be chosen, even if they are relatively young. There was recently held a very successful meeting at Prague on optics, and another at Cracow on cosmic rays, and meetings in numerous othercities on other subjects. Secondly, in addition, a very few travel grants have been given — that is to say, subsistence allow- ance for a matter of a few months. In this way a Polish physicist has been sent to Edinburgh to the laboratory of Max Bourne to renew contact after isolation during the war years; and a Chilean who is setting up a marine biological laboratory in his own country has been sent to Naples for a few months. Thirdly, publications. I will mention only ‚one among the manythatexist (there are at least sixty) — the monthly bulletin of the International Union of Scientific Radio, which is studying the problem of the isospheres. Fourthly, international co-operation between all these international unions. I would mention the one which is dealing with details of seismographie movements, relating to human welfare with regard to. earthquakes, and renders this information avail- able in its reduced form to all concerned. Some unions also operate their own small inter- national laboratories; and there the impetus of Unesco has been in 1947 to set on foot the produc- tion of a world catalogue of type-cultures. Finally, there is aid to stockrooms, to which the Chairman referred a little while ago under II. D. 5, such as that at Brussels, where standard samples of substances of a very high degree of purity necessary for technical work, are maintained under the auspices of the International Union of Chemistry. All these things, really are of the essence of inter- national scientific co-operation, not in the interest ultimately of science, but in the interest of the welfare of the peoples and the raising of the standards of living and of international intercourse. EDWARDS (Australia) : Mr. Chairman, there is no indication here in this motion of the type of competition proposed, but in the Working Party it was referred to as an essay competition, and the Australian delegation will continue to oppose this proposal to conduct essay competitions under the auspices of Unesco. We submit that it is not the function of Unesco to undertake a search for literary merit or to reward literary gladiators, whether or not they prepare their own essays. It is the function of Unesco, as part of its Fundamental Education Programme, to discover where illiteracy exists and to endeavour to remove it. Thatis the proper and main function of Funda- mental Education. We believe it is a good thing for young people to learn about people of other lands, to know of the ideals and principles for which Unesco stands and to endeavour to apply them; and they can do this with- out writing under competitive conditions essays about Unesco, particularly as Unesco stands for collaboration rather than competition. Apart from the expenditure involved in this enterprise in rewar-d ing winners of the competition — and the expendi- ture is considerable — the Australian delegation is quite opposed to the principle of holding essay com- petitions as part of Unesco’s activities. We believe the task of stimulating local interest in Unesco can safely be left to National Commissions or Co-operat- ing Bodies in Member States, without embarking on essay competitions. That is the view of the Austra- lian delegation. The CHAIRMAN : Does anyone further wish to speak to this motion? If not, may I put it to the meeting? The motion is carried.
