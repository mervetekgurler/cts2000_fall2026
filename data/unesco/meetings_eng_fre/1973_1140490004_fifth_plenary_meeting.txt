4.1 Mr. SEDDOH (Ghana):  Mr. President, as Ghana has fully participated in the examination of the propos-  als placed before us, 1 shall refrain from going into much detail in my remarks on the  item under discussion. This has already been ably done by the Vice-Chairman of the  Executive Board in his presentation and supported by the Director-General. The pro-  posals for which the approval of the General Conference is being sought were largely  due to the consensus among members of the Executive Board. This was brought about  by the spirit of compromise in view of the grave financial difficulties which face our  Organization. This crisis, as the Director-General told us yesterday, is the first of  its kind in the history of Unesco and, having listened carefully to the statements of those  who have preceded me, 1 am happy to say that the spirit of compromise noticed during  the deliberations of the Board appears to have caught on also in this Bxtraordinary Con-  ference. The indications therefore are that our discussions will lead to a successful  conclusion.
4.2 Mr. President, we have met to find a solution to the financial crisis whichplagues  our Organization. The situation is an extraordinary one and therefore calls for extraor-  dinary measures. If 1 may borrow a local expression, we are assembled here to apply  what amounts to “traitement à chaud” to the crisis facing Unesco, brought about by the  combined effects of inflation and the devaluation of the dollar. Obviously, if this shock  treatment or, if you like, this form of therapy is to have any meaningful and lasting re-  sults, it follows that the efforts being deployed by us to find a solution to the crisis should  not end here. It is therefore gratifying to note that this concern of ours has been taken  up by the Director-General during his exposé in which he outlined the long-term plans of  the Organization aimed at checking a recurrence of the present unsatisfactory situation.
4.3 Mr. President, we are being asked to approve supplementary estimates totalling  After certainadjustments, $14, 252, 100. this figure has been brought down to $12, 652, 100 _-~---  which must be recovered from Member States in the form of assessed contributions.  This supplementary appropriation represents, we are told, just a small percentage of the  approved total budget and should not be unbearable. Truc, if we think of the vast sums  of money being swallowed up in the arms race in a world which has set up Unesco to con-  struct the defences of peace in men’s minds, the supplementary estimates now being dis-  cussed would appear to be a drop of water in the ocean. But cari we really and honestly  say this for the fragile economies of developing countries?
4. 4 In my statement to the seventeenth session of the General Conference last year, 1  made it clear that countries of the Third World were not in a position to increase their pre-  sent level of contribution. 1 further appealed to those Member States which were in a position  to c0ntribut.e more to do SO and not limit their efforts in the promotion of real happiness for
3XCf5  millions of people through the worthy cause of international development assistance.
4. 5 Although we were a party to the consensus reached in the Executive Board, this  was not without some reservation on our part. It is no secret that further degradation  in the capacity of developing countries to pay up as a result of fresh assessed contribu-  tions, would only perpetuate the ever-widening gulf between the industrialized and devel-  oping countries, a situation which constitutes a challenge to social justice and a threat  to world peace. Mr. President, poverty and affluence cannot coexist indefinitely. The  question of seeking the approval of supplementary estimates in our countries is not as  easy as we are given to understand. The present financial crisis facing Unesco, we are  told, is foreign to the Organization. It is equally SO with developing countries, and 1 am  in entire agreement with my colleague from India that it is “inequitable to pass on to  other countries the financial burden for which they are not responsible”.
4. 6 We do fully understand the argument that the imbalance in the Unesco finances  is directly the result of world monetary instability. For the devaluation of the US dollar  and the revaluation of the West German mark and a couple of other major currencies  are indeed important contributory factors in the inflationary pressures listed as the  second cause of imbalance in the Unesco accounts.
4. 7 It is clear, therefore, that the Member States of Unesco are very much interested  to see an early end to the work of the Groups of 20 and 24 currently looking into the re-  form of world monetary arrangements. Until that solution is found, 1 am sure that the  cal1 to poor countries to provide supplementary votes for the operations of international  organizations Will continue, and probably Will become more frequent.
4. 8 While the International Monetary Fund is seeking solutions which, hopefully, Will  give long-term relief, we the Member States of Unesco must necessarily seek ways of  reducing the unfavourable impact of world monetary instability.
4. 9 Two relevant proposals have been put forward by some other delegations. We  have been asked to adopt a more stable currency in which the budget of Unesco will,be  expressed. The main reason for such an arrangement is that over 70 per cent of the  expenditure of Unesco is in French Francs. For such an arrangement to prove bene-  ficial, two conditions must be fulfilled. Firstly, the French Franc must remain quite  stable SO that, for most Member States, their own domestic currency equivalent of con-  tributions assessed on them Will not vary.
4. 10 Secondly, domestic inflation in France must not get out of hand, SO that the real  value of contributions paid Will be maintained to a reasonable degree.
4.11 It has been proposed that a more stable currency should be adopted for the cur-  rency of accounting. In our view, this latter proposa1 coincides with the proposa1 for  the adoption of the Franch Franc for budgetary purposes, subject to the following stipu-
3xw5  lation: that a special unit of account be adopted with an established parity relationship  to the currency of every Member State. Suppose, for example, that we were to cal1  such a unit of accounting the Unec, it would mean that a parity between the Unec and -- --  the currency of every Member State would be established, and in this way, instability  in a world reserve currency would not affect the operations of Unesco. Of course, such  an arrangement would not insulate the operations of Unesco against price inflationin the  main markets where Unesco makes purchases. However, price inflation per se is easier  to dealwith, since a reasonable forecast cari be made and the shortfall or otherwise on  a forecast is not likely to be large, and in many cases could be accommodated within the  same budget. What is disturbing is the effect of devaluation which, sometimes, doesnot  even reflect relative efficiency and inefficiency in the domestic economies. In recent  times, it is not unusual to find the currency of the high-cost country appreciating against  the currency of the low-cost country. In any case, when devaluation does occur in the  principal currency, it usually triggers off devaluation and even revaluation of a few other  currencies.
4.12 Our two main proposals on these issues therefore are: (i) from the pointof view  of countries whose currencies are not directly exchangeable into French Francs, the  adoption of the French Franc as the budget currency Will still not insulate them from  the effects of the instability of the US dollar or of the Pound sterling. This is because  many of these countries hold their currency reserves in US dollars or the Pound sterling  and therefore changes in parity between the French Franc and these two or other reserve  currencies Will affect them then as now. Thus, even as a short-term solution, assess-  ing contributions in French Francs Will only help the Member States whose reserves are  held in French Francs. It is therefore important to seek a neutral unit of account such  as the suggested Unec,whichwould be insulated from the direct effects of currency de-  valuation.
4.13 In addition to this measure, we also wish to propose consideration of the IMF  type of quota subscription, which allows part of a member’s contribution to be made in  the domestic currency of the member. This Will reduce considerably the foreign ex-  change burden of Unesco membership for developing countries. Under such an arrange-  ment, if, say, it is planned to spend $200, 000 in Ghana, and the annual contribution of  Ghana in the particular year is assessed at ($300,000, then Ghana would be called upon  to pay in dollars or French Francs, etc., only the difference of $100, 000 whilst the  remaining g200,OOO would be credited to the account of Unesco in Ghana, probably in  the books of the Central Bank.
4.14 Mr. President, we in Ghana are really urging Unesco to seek as a matter of ur-  gency a viable financial arrangement which allons least instability in project implemen-
243    tation and makes the minimum adverse impact on the economies of the developing  members.
4.15 The Director-General has had to absorb as much as $7, 847, 900 resulting from  inflation over which he had no control, without touching the integrity of the programme.  He and his staff should be congratulated for their efforts.
4.16 We are equally impressed by the austerity measures envisaged by the Director-  General. But we still believe that it would not be asking too much if the Director-General  were requested to take yet another look at his Grganization, especially the administrative  sector. Any further savings realized need not be to the detriment of the programme.  They may not necessarily affect the effectiveness of the programme, for when we talk  about Growth it should not only be in terms of quantity but also in terms of quality.
4.17 Mr. President, we do support the recent innovation of country programming  under which some of the operations of Unesco are mapped out and executed directly  within the Member States. In many cases this is a way to make such projects effective  as Member States may then adopt them as their own projects and therefore wish to see  them fully implemented. 1 may add that it is quite common to be sceptical about  foreign consultants, since invariably they have to depend heavily on the knowledge of  local officiais. Unesco Will have to tut out some of the comings and goings of its staff  round the world. 1 am not sure half of such trips are necessary.
4. 18 We are therefore particularly happy that the Director-General in his address  indicated that serious attention was being given to this, and that staff travel has already  been considerably reduced. We would urge him to persist in the exercise.
4.19 Al1 told, in the light of Ghana’s unflinching support for Unesco, we would vote  for the draft resolution.
4.20 Finally, we would like to express our appreciation once again to the Director-  General. and the Executive Board for the work they have done;and we hope and pray for the  quick recovery of the latter’s energetic and enthusiastic Chairman, Dr. Faad Sarrfif.  Thank you, Mr. President.
