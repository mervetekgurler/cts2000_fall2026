1.4 1 now invite you to observe one minute’s silence in their memory.  (The Conference observes one minute’s silence)
1.5 Excellencies, distinguished delegates, ladies and gentlemen, MO years from today, we Will be  celebrating the ftitieth anniversary of UNESCO, which Will doubtless be an important event and also a unique  occasion for the international community at large to take stock of the immense contribution of this, our  common endeavour, to the consolidation and development of international intellectual co-operation in a myriad  of fields. We hope, and we have good reasons to hope, that by then the universality of the Organization Will  have been fully restored, thanks to the constant efforts of many and, in particular, of the Director-General,  Federico Mayor. For how unfortunate it would be to celebrate the ftitieth anniversary without two of the  founding members, the United States and the United Kingdom! As part of the preparation for the fiftieth  anniversary of the Organization, 1 think it is necessary to engage from now onwards in a sustained analysis of  UNESCO’s past and present and thus to shape its future. If such an analysis is to be of any use, it should be  critical and not self-congratulatory. Only thus Will we be able together to give UNESCO the profile and the  tools it Will need in a future that will hold many new and unexpected realities.
1.6 The last decade of this century Will be a transitional phase between the end of an era and the dawn of a  new one. For the moment we are ail moving forward together, in a world in which every country’s destiny is  deeply interlocked with those of the rest, at ail levels. This interdependency is a reality that cari be experienced  on a daily basis and which requires of us a major shift in the very way we perceive the world. Remoteness is a  Word that has lost its meaning in our contemporary world as events in one region have immediate and lasting  effects on all the others.
1.7 At the time of the foundation of our Organization, after the material and moral destruction brought  about by the Second World War, international intellectual co-operation was considered, and quite rightly SO, as  the common ground on which the basis of a lasting peace could be built for the future generations. Who among  us bas not at least once quoted, almost as a ritual incantation, the words of the Preamble of the Constitution of  UNESCO: ‘That since wars begin in the minds of men, it is in the minds of men that the defences of peace  must be constructed’? But ail of us, in the silence of our consciences, should now ask: have we done what was  necessary to build those defences?
1.8 Did we do the right thing? Did we do enough? Did we do all we should have done? In short, what  educational, scient& and cultural bridges of peace have we built?
1.9 Since 1945, the very abjects of the fields of competence of UNESCO have radically changed.  Education, science and culture were perceived at that time in a very specitïc manner, that is, as the basic  elements of a civilization that had to be consolidated in order to dispel, once and for ah, even the remotest  possibility of barbarity rearing once again its head as it did during the Second World War. Indeed, the spiritual  values of humanity had been attacked at their very tore, and it was indispensable to create the conditions for  this never to happen again.
1.10 As a certain, although precarious, stability pervaded international relations in the context of the Cold  War, peoples moved ahead in many regions of the world aud the long and complex process of decolonization  brought forth a large part of the SO-called Third World and in its wake, the vast array of development  alternatives which, in one way or another, continue to haunt us to this very day. In this new socio-cultural  context, education, science and culture took on new basic contents; education systems had to be built anew; the  belief in scient& inquiry and the search for rational solutions to problems were globalized; and cultural  identities in different regions of the world were fostered. UNESCO played a leading role in this process.
1.11 But on mauy counts, this record of the Organization’s life was contradictory aud the results diificult to  ascertain, let alone to quantify. hnlicators, as our experts cal1 them, were lacking, and the balance sheet was  based more on the constant affirmation of a forthright and sometimes courageous Will to tackle development  problems in their many dimensions than in an effort to ascertain that what was being done was, in fact, a step  forward and not counterproductive.
1.12 This characteristic was not peculiar to UNESCO, which shared it with the rest of the United Nations  Specialized Agencies, and indeed with the govemments of the Member States themselves. But it must also be  stressed that already then, the Organization was being asked to take on more and more new fields and tasks as  the complexity of the development process became increasingly daunting. Indeed, the very nature of the  Organization began to change as the needs of many countries became increasingly acute. The Organization’s  response had to take an immediite character, it had to remedy the urgent; and drus, gradually, it had less and  less the capacity to envisage the essential and prepare the long-term strategies. The responsibility for this state  of affairs must be borne by all of us; and, in fact, this dilemma of increasing demands versus dwindling  resources has become very acute during the last few years.
1.13 The collapse of communism and the cessation of the Cold War has not brought about the expected  peace at the global level. The age of ideological differences and clashes has been succeeded by the era of ethnie,  radical and religious clashes. It is reckoned that, given the present trend which accords pride of place to ethnie  nationalism, we are likely to end up with 44 republics in the former USSR and Eastem Europe. Last year the  United Nations Secretary-General, Mr Boutros Boutros-Ghali, warned that the world was in danger of  fragmenting into as many as 400 different countries, most of them in Africa.
1.14 The threat to global peace and security is still with us, rising from the production and proliferation of  nuclear weapons. According to military intelligence reports, 40 countries Will have acquired the ability to  manufacture nuclear weapons by the year 2000, witb 30 of them in the SO-called Third World. The promotion  of ‘global economy’ and ‘development’ has brought about what has been described a an ‘economic holocaust’.  According to the Report of the Director-General of the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization  (FAO), about 800 million people live in absolute poverty. Most of these poor people live in a part of the South  known as the ‘poverty belt’ or the ‘Fourth World’, comprising 32 countries. It is estimated that the population  of the ‘Fourth World’ Will reach 3.2 billion by the year 2005. According to UNESCO statistics, 814 million  adults are illiterate; and 123 million children of school age have no access to education. Against this backdrop,  the American economist, Kenneth Galbraith, has warned, correctly in my view, that ‘if the New World Order is  to bear meaning and be effective, it should transcend the conflicts and massacres and see to the reasons behind  these phenomena. Anything that appears to be called a New World Grder should regard the poor as the prime  source of disorder’.
1.15 At the same time, the strategies of development based on economic factors alone are by now obsolete  and the world is today confronted with tensions derived from such a vast array of causes, that no global analysis  based on ideology, politics or regional value systems cari provide a satisfactory explanation.
1.16 Major shifts in the political and economic order have indeed occurred, but cari anyone pinpoint today  the mechanisms of the changing order, new or not; aud, more important, eau anyone identify a coherent  political Will behind that order? Yes, this is the time for questions, not for ready-made answers, still less for
5    dogmatic ones such as those brought forth by the proponents of the ‘end of history’ theory. Indeed, some  pessimistic historians are even talking about the ‘retum of history’. Why not the beginning of history, of a  humanity confronted with the definitive interdependency of its peoples, sharing a common future at ail levels  and indeed, at its most fundamental level, that of its relationship with the enviromnent in which it lives?
1.17 This sense of new beginnings, in the context of a deep spiritual and intellectual crisis and in the wake  of new freedoms for many countries, is reflected in the efforts the Organization has made to set up a series of  organs, such as the ad hoc Forum of Reflection, the World Commission on Culture and Development and the  International Commission on Education for the Twenty-First Century. These forums provide a unique  opportunity to set out the principles that Will guide our action in the next decade and beyond.
1.18 For our times are exhilarating indeed, the questions are many, the problems intricate; but we cannot go  forward only on the basis of an impractical idealism, and even less SO if we disperse our efforts and  overestimate the scope and the resources of an instrument such as UNESCO.
1.19 As we gather now to adopt the Programme and Budget for 1994-1995 and to put forward propos& for  the next Medium-Term Plan, we must not limit ourselves to the fine-tuning of activities centred on the  immediate and undoubtedly critical needs our countries face; we must also be ready to sacrifice the subsidiary  for the essential.
1.20 We have been able to do this in the past as we moved away from trying to encompass many activities  that could be carried out in other ways and called upon the Organization to concentrate its programmes.
1.21 And, at least for the foreseeable future, we must continue on the road of concentration, in order for the  programmes approved by the General Conference to be effectively carried out., even if the Organization is  constantly being asked to assume new tasks, and even sometimes to act as a fmancing institution, which is not  its primary function.
1.22 This brings us, once again, to the dilemma to which 1 referred earlier, and which 1 Will state now in  other, more concrete terms: should the Organization delïne one essential task which it is to carry out in the next  few years worldwide, or should we continue as now, govemed by urgencies and new problematics as they arise,  which certainly have led us to make some very considerable achievements, but whose lasting value cari  legitimately be called in doubt?
1.23 Education, education for ah, the eradication of illiteracy by the year 2000 would be strong contenders  for the title of the essential task of UNESCO. The Executive Board’s ad hoc Forum of Reflection has made  some important proposals in this regard that are in complete agreement with the follow-up strategy of the  Jomtien Conference on Education for AU, and in consonance with UNESCO’s vast and recognized experience  in the field.
1.24 Education, in the widest sense of the term, enriched by the results of the many activities UNESCO has  carried out successfully in the scientific and cultural spheres, should be at the centre of our debates, for the only  response we cari give to many of the acute crises of which societies are victims today is the enhancement of an  education based on the solid principles of tolerance and the pursuit of peace. This is also truc for science-related  topics such as bioethics, which Will not be tackled outside a cuhurally sensitive education.
1.25 Excellencies, ladies and gentlemen, in a political assembly one advances certain interests against those  of others, closes off certain options which appear to harm those interests, or which suggest alternative ways of  proceeding. In this General Conference, we must net, and carmot proceed thus: what we must convey to the  international community is our conviction that we are assembled here to pursue a common goal and to set out  priorities which may seem at fust sight unfashionable but will, in tum, define a global course for UNESCO.  Yes, we must together make sense of the momentous events of our times, and with a determination to advance  proposals, even if they are outside the conventional contours of our own past decisions. But we must also bear  in mind that only by confronting what is essential Will we be able to ensure tbat immediate expediency does not  blur out the basic objectives of our Organization.
1.26 In my acceptance speech, fohowing my election as President of the General Conference in October
1991, 1 posed the question whether UNESCO then was the kind of Organization that the Member States  deserved. After two years, 1 cari now answer my own question in the affirmative. It is therefore not surprising that the Executive Board, in its own wisdom, has  unanimously recommended his nomination for a second term; and it is my sincere hope that the General  Conference will endorse this recommendation by acclamation.
