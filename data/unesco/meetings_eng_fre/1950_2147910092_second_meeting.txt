In the opinion of the Board, collaboration  with a great number of the international non-  governmental organizations had been fruitful for  Unesco which in many cases had been able to  hand over work which could be done by them  more professionally and more cheaply.        [11] Mr. MULLIKEN (United States of America)  suggested a general discussion on consultative  status for international non governmental organ-  izations, which raised important questions of  grants-in-aid, contracts or formal agreements.  He thought that, after the discussion, a com-  mittee might be appointed to deal with those  problems.  [12] Mr. COWELL (United Kingdom) believed  that Unesco was the one Specialized Agency which  had made it a deliberate and important part of  its policy to use existing international non-  governmental organizations to carry out its main  objectives, and that the Executive Board was con-  vinced of the importance of continuing that policy.  An important principle emerging from document
5C/OXR/10 was the clear distinction made be-  tween the awarding of consultative status to inter-  national non-governmental organizations and the  granting to them of financial aid. Previously, a  grant-in-aid was contingent upon the prior award  of consultative status.  [13] Mr. MULLIKEN (United States of America)  said that his delegation attached great importance  to the system of consultative status; they believed  that it was one of the principal means of making  available to Unesco the views and experience of  many groups of people, and it also offered op-  portunities to Unesco of making known the nature  of its programme. It was because of that im-  portance that the United States delegation had  been concerned with the effectiveness of the system  and dissatisfied with the way in which it had  heen operated up to the present time, though they  recognized the difficulties involved. The United  States delegation would, at the appropriate time  suggest several changes in the Directives proposed  bv the Executive Board [document 5C/OXR/10,  Annex II] and would make additional suggestions  regarding opportunities for circulation to, and  consideration by, Unesco’s appropriate bodies, of  written communications from international non-  governmental organizations; but opportunities  should also be afforded for verbal communications  from them.  The United States delegation believed that the  General Conference should be the body to grant  consultative status; this was the practice of  other Specialized Agencies and of the Economic  and Social Council. Motivated by the desire to  raise the status of consultative relations, the  United States delegation would suggest that no  reference to informal relations be included in the  Directives. They would like the Directives con-  fined to formal consultative relationships. The  United States delegation would also prefer to see  eliminated from the Directives any reference to  contracts entered into with such organizations by  Unesco. Such contracts were, in the view of  his delegation, no different from those entered  into with a private individual or an institution.  [14] The United States delegation would not  separate the granting of subsidies from enjoyment  of consultative status. A subsidy should be given  because an international non-governmental organ-  ization was closely concerned with Unesco’s gen-  eral activities. It would be money given for  which no specific return was expected; other-  wise, it was a contractual relationship. If an  international non-governmental organization was  not sufficiently interested in Unesco’s work to apply  for consultative status, such an organization was  certainly not entitled to receive a gift of money.  Mr. Mulliken did not think that there should be  formal agreements with only a limited number of  international non-governmental organizations of  a group or class. Those enjoying consultative  status should be subject to the same obligations.  The United States delegation was in whole-  hearted agreement with the Executive Board that  formal agreements should be discontinued. It  was further thought that there should be no pro-  visions for the establishment of new organizations  in the directives, though occasions might arise  when Unesco would wish to assist in establishing  a new organization; but the United States delega-  tion hoped that such occasions would become  increasingly rare; those cases should become a  programme responsibility and financed as part of  the programme.  [15] Professor KRUYT (Netherlands), referring  to Mr. Mulliken’s statement that there were two  categories of formal agreement with international  non-governmental organizations, said that, in his  view. this was absolutely logical. The organiza-  tion in the first category was really a consulting  organization; the second was a partner. Many  of the latter received grants-in-aid and did work  which, if they did not exist, Unesco would have  to do itself. He mentioned in particular the Inter-  national Council of Scientific Unions, which was  quite different from Boy Scouts or Pax Romana,  for example.  [16] Professor AGO (Italy) agreed with Mr. Mul-  liken’s point of view regarding subsidies: these  should be reserved for organizations enjoying  consultative status. On the other hand, when 1t  came to the question of formal agreements, he  seconded the remarks made by Professor Kruyt.  [17] Mr. de LACHARRIÈRE (France) announced  that his delegation would comment at a later stage  on certain points, but wished first of all to know                                   the opinion of the international non-governmental  organizations on the various questions. He  thought that the Commission could allow itself  to be guided by their opinion.  [18] Professor von MURALT (Switzerland)  agreed with Mr. Mulliken on the importance of  consultative status for international non-gov-  ernmental organizations, but did not agree with  what had been said concerning certain bodies, of  which there were several, with which Unesco had  formal agreements that the Executive Board pro-  posed to abolish. He thought such a move would  be a great mistake, because it would create bad  feeling among those who had hitherto aided and  worked with Unesco, and would give rise to  misunderstanding and criticism. Those organ-  izations were working in Unesco’s field more  cheaply and more effectively than Unesco could  itself. With regard to the International Council  of Scientific Unions, a glance at document 5C/6  would show that a great deal of international  work had been done by them in recent years.  [19] Mr. COWELL (United Kingdom) said it  was not easy to refuse consultative status to inter-  national non-governmental organizations of estab-  lished reputation. If the lists were kept within  reasonable limits, however, the temptation to  apply for consultative status in order to get money  would be removed. Turning to Mr. Mulliken’s  suggestion that contracts should be eliminated  from the Directives, Mr. Cowell pointed out that  such contracts were the means by which an inter-  national non-governmental organization could be  indirectly subsidized for an important purpose.  Such a purpose, however, would not be achieved 1f  that organization were unable to devote the neces-  sary funds to carry out its task. In fact, de-  parture from the Directives would tend to lead  to greater inflexibility.  The Executive Board felt that the Directives  would lack completeness if some reference were  not made in them to informal relationships.  What had obviously aroused great concern was the  proposal that formal agreements should be given  up; Mr. Cowell could assure Professor von Mu-  ralt that Unesco had no intention of ceasing re-  lationship with such an important body as the  International Council of Scientific Unions. The  United Kingdom delegation was prepared to move  the adoption of the Directives as they stood.

Report on the Conference où Observers of Interna-  tional Non-Governmental Organizations.  [2] Mr. ENNALS (Chairman, Conference of  Observers of International Non-Governmental  Organizations) expressed his appreciation of the  opportunity given to him for reporting on the  Conference held on 23 May 1950 at the invitation  of the Director-General of Unesco, under the  Honorary Chairmanship of Ambassador Cora.  It was the first time that all non-governmental  organizations having consultative relations with  Unesco had been invited to discuss their relation-  ship. Despite the variety of interests represented  and the fulness of the discussions, all the recom-  mendations adopted by the Conference were  unanimously approved. These proposals therefore  represented the wishes of all the forty-two non-  governmental organizations represented.  [3] Mr. Ennals read the text of the letter  addressed on 24 May 1950 to the Director-General  of Unesco by himself as Chairman, and by Miss  Charrot, as Rapporteur, of the Conference:  sé  e) 80  You were kind enough to invite the international non-  governmental organizations enjoying consultative  status with Unesco to send representatives to a special  conference in Florence, at the same time as the  Fifth Session of the General Conference.  That invitation has made it possible for the repre-  sentatives of forty-two international non-governmental  organizations to consider the relations between Unesco  and non-governmental organizations and to make  a detailed study of the Executive Board’s proposals,  submitted to the Fifth Session of Unesco’s General  Conference, regarding future relations between Unesco  and those organizations.  You have done us the honour of telling us, In a  brief speech, how much importance you attach to our  collaboration with Unesco. At the end of the Conference, a Committee of twelve  was set up, with instructions to draw the attention  of Unesco’s General Conference to these resolutions.  On behalf of that Committee, we would reques!  vou to be so kind as to transmit the resolutions to  the competent body of the General Conference, in  order that the wishes expressed by the international  non-governmental organizations may be taken into  account by Unesco when it prepares directives for  relations with international non-governmental organ-  izations.
