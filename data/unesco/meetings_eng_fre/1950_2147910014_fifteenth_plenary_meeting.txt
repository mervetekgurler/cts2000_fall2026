[23] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] We now come to item 18 of the revised  agenda: Establishment of a committee to prepare  a declaration of the duties of the State in regard  to education, science, and culture, for the purpose  of ensuring a better understanding between  peoples [document 5C/2). [24] Professor Alf SOMMERFELT (Norway) —  Mr. President, you will recall that, at the last Con-          pu e  cé        ference, in Paris, the subject before us was dealt  with in a series of lectures at the Maison de la Chi-  mie. The Norwegian National Commission thought  that this subject ought to be followed by the pre-  paration of a declaration of the duties of the States  in these matters, and therefore the question has  been included in the Agenda by the Executive  Board. We thought that such a declaration  might contribute to the aim of furthering under-  standing between the States. It cannot, of course,  enforce anything; but such a declaration might,  shall we say, be the conscience of the States. It  would set forth what they ought to do. It would  also have another object: it would show states-  men that certain things which they have practised  hitherto have not furthered them in their real  object, since, in many respects, repression has  had exactly the opposite effect. 1 may mention,  for instance, the question of linguistic oppression,  of which we have had so many cases in Europe  and elsewhere. Every linguistic historian will  tell you that if the States in Europe had not tried  to suppress minority languages, many of those  languages would have died peacefully. Though,  when the authorities tried to suppress them, they  sprang into new life, they were galvanized, so to  speak, into a new lease of life. The object is  therefore not only to show States what they ought  to do in these matters, but also to bring certain  facts to their knowledge. The Norwegian Na-  tional Commission, therefore, was very strongly in  favour of the preparation of such a declaration. It  applied to the Norwegian Government, and the  Norwegian Government was willing to take the  matter up. When it heard that, in my capacity as  a member of the Executive Board, I had the right  to propose that the matter be included in the  Agenda of this Conference, it preferred to let me  do so. That was why I proposed, at one of the  last meetings of the Board, that this item be in-  cluded in the Agenda, where it now appears as  item 18.  [25] This matter ought to be studied very  thoroughly, because we must avoid any overlap-  ping between such a declaration and the Declara-  tion of Human Rights. I therefore suggest that  the Conference ask the Director-General to have  the matter studied, with a view to possible action  at the next General Conference, and I have the  honour to propose the following resolution:  The Director-General is authorized to consider the  setting up of a Committee to prepare a Declaration of  the duties of the State in regard to education, science  and culture, for the purpose of ensuring a better  understanding between the people, and to report  thereon to the next session of the General Conference.  You will understand that this does not imply any
239  budgetary provision. The matter will be studied  and brought before the next General Conference.  [26] The PRESIDENT — [Translation from the  French] Are there any other speakers? I repeat, this  contribution must be made within the limits of  Unesco’s competence as an educational, scientific  and cultural body forming part of the larger  framework of the United Nations. On this  clearly defined basis, we should appeal to  Member States to take action within the  United Nations Specialized Agencies and the Uni-  ted Nations Organization itself to ensure the  smoother running of the now somewhat creaking  machinery of the United Nations, so that peace  may be firmly established for many generations  to come; and not to Member States alone; we  should also appeal to all who have power to bring  about better understanding between individuals  and nations, to the teachers and scholars at all  levels, to the journalists, film producers, special-  ists in broadcasting—all who do and must con-  tribute directly to the implementation of our  basic programme and our successive annual pro-  grammes. Having thus pledged Unesco's sincere  desire and resolve to work with every means at  its disposal for the cause of peace, which is the  common ideal of the United Nations and all the  Specialized Agencies, we should demonstrate the  sincerity of our aims to all the world and, at our  Conferences, expert committees and other meet-  ings, we should set an example of tolerance and  broad understanding, showing that we favour the  free and unimpeded exchange of all ideas and  principles. Mr. President, these are the brief  comments I would make on this draft resolution,  which I! should be extremely grateful to have  adopted unanimously, not, I assure you, for any  personal motive nor even for any national motive,  but because of my firm faith in Unesco. [82] Now, gentlemen, I should like to take up  an idea expressed by my colleague, Senator Casati,  and, as an Italian, to tell you that you owe  gratitude to no one, as Unesco may proudly make  the same claim as the King of France who was  wont to say: “The King is everywhere at home.”  Unesco is at home in every part of the world it  may wish to visit. Unesco is at home in Flor-  ence, as it has been in the past in Paris and  Beirut, and as it may be in the future in Mon-  tevideo or any other city. In every country it will  find hearts and minds ready to welcome it  and understand. That is why you owe gratitude  to none; it is rather the host countries that are  indebted to you. I forecast in my speech in  Beirut and again in Paris that you would be en-  tirely at home in Florence, and that this first-  hand contact with humanism, art and remote  antiquity, with the Renaissance and with periods  nearer to our own day, would, as it were, add to  your stock of spiritual values and help you to  look with greater optimism towards the work yet  to be accomplished. You have shown great dis-  cretion and tact in this matter of enriching your  minds, for Unesco’s work has not had to suffer  from your desire to see the art treasures of Italy.  I was at times afraid that the meeting rooms  would be deserted, but they never have been.  You have attended meetings here and still found  time to visit all the places of interest. You  certainly share the gift of ubiquity with one of  Italy’s saints, St. Anthony of Padua, who, by the  way, was Portuguese. [83] All this work that has been done, though  it has not been proclaimed with flourish of  trumpets, has nevertheless been of great value to  us and our Organization and will eventually go to  benefit mankind. We can, accordingly, find cause  for gratification in this work well done, in the  humble performance of our duty, a duty which  does not end in Florence, today, but must be  faced anew in the Executive Board, at the next  Paris Conference and at all the conferences to  come. Each successive conference marks a fur-  ther stage in the completion of a task that  depends more on inner conviction than on any  outward action, a task that has to do with basic  principles rather than with political factors. We  have attempted to reconcile planning and initia-  tive, organization and freedom, the encourage-  ment of spontaneous action with the necessity of  governmental control.
